Act IThe idea

What does it mean to generate?

A generative model is shown a pile of examples, say photos of faces, and learns to produce new ones that could have been in the pile but never were. Every dot below is one "example" in a world where images have only two pixels, so each image is just a point on a plane.

Every image is a point

A grayscale image with two pixels is two numbers, the brightness of each pixel, so it is a point on a plane. A real photo works the same way with far more numbers: a 512 by 512 color image is 786,432 of them, one point in a space with that many axes. Nobody can picture that space, but everything important already happens in two dimensions, which is why this page uses two.

Scatter random numbers into those axes and you get television static. Real photos are rare: they sit in a thin, strangely shaped region of the space, where neighboring pixels agree, edges are continuous and faces have two eyes. The collection of all real images, and how densely they crowd each part of the space, is called the data distribution, written p_{\text{data}}(x). In the plot it is the spiral: dense along the arm, empty elsewhere.

"Generating" means drawing a fresh point from that distribution. The catch is that we never get a formula for p_{\text{data}}; we only get the examples. We need a procedure that turns something we can sample, like random numbers, into something that looks like it came from the spiral.

Why this was hard before diffusion

Every earlier approach had a price.

  • GANs train a generator against a critic that tries to tell real from fake. Samples are sharp, but the two-player game is unstable, and a generator can win by covering only part of the data ("mode collapse": on our spiral, it might learn one arm and ignore the rest).
  • VAEs squeeze each example into a small code and decode it back. Training is stable, but samples come out blurry, because the decoder hedges between the images a code could have been.
  • Autoregressive models write an image one pixel at a time, each conditioned on the ones before. They fit the data well and are slow to sample.
  • Normalizing flows learn an invertible warp from noise to data. The math is exact, but the invertibility rule restricts which networks you can use.

The diffusion trick: destroying structure is easy

Here is the idea the whole page builds on. Going from data to noise needs no learning at all: shrink each point a little toward the center, add a little random jitter, and repeat. Drag the slider to the end and the spiral becomes a round blob of pure noise, the same blob no matter what the data looked like. That blob is easy to sample from.

So run the film backwards. If each forward step changes the picture only slightly, then each backward step is also a small, local correction ("this point probably came from a bit closer to the spiral arm"), and small local corrections are something a neural network can learn. String a thousand of them together and noise flows back into data. Press Now run it backwards to watch fresh noise condense into the spiral, in green.

The idea came from physics. In 12 Mar 2015 Sohl-Dickstein and colleagues, borrowing from non-equilibrium thermodynamics (think of a drop of ink spreading through water), proposed to destroy structure in data with a slow forward diffusion and learn the reverse diffusion that restores it. Their first demo was a two-dimensional swiss roll, much like the one above. The paper reported likelihoods rather than sample quality, its images were not competitive, and the idea sat mostly unused until DDPM (19 Jun 2020) found a simple way to train it that produced images competitive with the best GANs. The rest of this page follows that story.

The one equation to carry forward

One formula describes where a data point ends up after t noising steps. The next chapter derives it; for now read it as a recipe with two ingredients.

\htmlData{step=1, link=noisy}{x_t} = \htmlData{step=2, link=data}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0}} + \htmlData{step=3, link=noise}{\htmlClass{t-brown}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\;\epsilon}}

The reparameterized form of Eq. 4 in DDPM v2 (written out in its Sec. 3.2)

  • x_t: the noisy point after t steps (the dots at slider position t).
  • \htmlClass{t-yellow}{x_0}: the original clean data point, a dot on the spiral.
  • \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}: how much of the original survives. It starts at 1 and decays toward 0 as t grows; the readout under the plot shows its value.
  • \htmlClass{t-brown}{\epsilon}: fresh random noise drawn from a standard bell curve (a "Gaussian"), one per dot and kept fixed as you drag, which is why each dot moves smoothly.
  • \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}: how much noise is mixed in. The two weights are chosen so the cloud never blows up or collapses; its overall size stays about the same while its shape is erased.

Everything that follows is about the reverse direction: how to learn it (Act II), how to understand it as a continuous flow and run it in far fewer steps (Act III), how to steer it with text and make it cheap enough for large images (Act IV), and how to make the paths so straight that one or two steps suffice (Act V).

The forward process: adding noise on a schedule

The forward process is the half of a diffusion model that nobody has to learn. Pick one data point, slide t, and watch where it can end up; the left panel shows how much of the point survives at each step under two different schedules.

A Gaussian in one paragraph

Nearly every formula on this page is built from the Gaussian, or normal distribution: the bell curve. It has a center (the mean) and a spread (the variance, or its square root, the standard deviation). In two dimensions with equal spread in every direction it is a round cloud, densest in the middle. The notation \mathcal{N}(x;\, m,\, v\,I) reads "x is Gaussian with mean m and variance v in every coordinate"; the I (identity matrix) just says each coordinate gets its own independent noise. Two facts carry the whole chapter: shrink a Gaussian and it stays Gaussian, and add two independent Gaussians and you get a Gaussian whose variances add.

One step

Each forward step does two things to the current point: shrink it a little toward the origin, and add a little fresh noise.

\htmlData{step=1, link=noisy}{q(x_t \mid x_{t-1})} = \mathcal{N}\big(x_t;\; \htmlData{step=2, link=shrink}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{\sqrt{1-\beta_t}\, x_{t-1}}},\; \htmlData{step=3, link=noise}{\htmlClass{t-brown}{\beta_t\, I}}\big)

Eq. 2 in DDPM v2

  • q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}): the distribution of the next, noisier point given the current one. The letter q is reserved for this fixed, unlearned process.
  • \beta_t: a small number, the variance of the noise added at step t. The list \beta_1, \dots, \beta_T is the schedule.
  • \sqrt{1-\beta_t}: the shrink factor, slightly below one.

Why shrink at all? Bookkeeping on the variance. If the current point has variance 1, shrinking by \sqrt{1-\beta_t} leaves variance 1-\beta_t, and adding noise of variance \beta_t brings it back to exactly 1. The cloud never explodes, it only forgets. Score SDE (Chapter 5) later names this family variance preserving.

Jump to any step in one shot

Because every step is "shrink, then add Gaussian noise", a thousand steps compose into a single shrink and a single Gaussian. Write \alpha_t = 1-\beta_t for the fraction of variance each step keeps and \bar\alpha_t = \alpha_1 \alpha_2 \cdots \alpha_t for the fraction that survives all the way to step t. Then:

\htmlData{step=1, link=noisy}{q(x_t \mid x_0)} = \mathcal{N}\big(x_t;\; \htmlData{step=2, link=shrink}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0}},\; \htmlData{step=3, link=noise}{\htmlClass{t-brown}{(1-\bar\alpha_t)\, I}}\big)

Eq. 4 in DDPM v2

This is the formula from Chapter 1 in distribution form: x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon. Its practical meaning is enormous. To make a training example at step 700 you do not simulate 700 steps; you draw one noise vector and mix. Press Walk there step by step and then Jump there in one shot: the walk wanders, the jump is instant, and both land in the same dashed cloud.

The schedule matters

DDPM used \beta_t growing linearly from 10^-4 to 0.02. Before you look at the left panel, guess: at which step does the linear schedule leave less than a tenth of the original signal? Then press Go to step 750.

The signal curve of the linear schedule collapses early and then crawls along the floor, so the last few hundred steps turn pure noise into slightly different pure noise. Improved DDPM noticed this at low resolution (64x64 and 32x32): a linear-schedule model barely gets worse when you skip up to 20% of the reverse process, which means those steps were not doing much. Their fix defines \bar\alpha_t directly with a squared cosine, which falls gently at both ends:

\htmlData{step=1, link=shrink}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{\bar\alpha_t}} = \frac{f(t)}{f(0)}, \qquad \htmlData{step=2, link=cosine}{f(t) = \cos^2\!\left(\frac{t/T + s}{1+s}\cdot\frac{\pi}{2}\right)}

Eq. 17 in Improved DDPM v1 (written there as \cos(\cdot)^2)

  • t/T: progress through the chain, from 0 to 1.
  • s: a small offset, 0.008, so the first steps still add a little noise.
  • Dividing by f(0) makes \bar\alpha_0 = 1. The per-step \beta_t = 1 - \bar\alpha_t / \bar\alpha_{t-1} is clipped at 0.999 near the end.

A useful way to compare schedules is the signal-to-noise ratio, \mathrm{SNR}(t) = \bar\alpha_t / (1-\bar\alpha_t): signal variance over noise variance. A schedule is really a choice of how quickly SNR falls, and so of how many steps the model spends at each noise level. That framing comes back in EDM (Chapter 6) and SD3 (Chapter 12), which both argue about where on the noise axis the effort should go.

Learning to undo it: predict the noise

DDPM's network gets a noisy point and the step number, and has one job: say which noise was added. Draw a training example below and compare the true noise with the best possible prediction; the red gap is what training penalizes.

Predicting the noise is predicting the image

If you know the noisy point x_t and the noise \epsilon that went into it, you can solve Chapter 1's recipe backwards for the clean point. So a noise guess \htmlClass{t-blue}{\epsilon_\theta} is also a guess of the image:

\htmlData{step=1, link=xhat}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{\hat x_0}} = \frac{\htmlData{step=2, link=noisy}{x_t} - \htmlData{step=3, link=pred}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\;\htmlClass{t-blue}{\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)}}}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}}

Eq. 15 in DDPM v2 (also the argument inside Eq. 11)

The subscript \theta ("theta") stands for the network's weights. The network is a U-Net, an image-to-image convolutional network with a few attention layers, told the step t through a sinusoidal embedding, but nothing here depends on its internals.

The best guess is an average

Slide to Lots of noise. The noisy point could have come from almost anywhere on the spiral, and the lines fan out to all of it. When the answer is ambiguous, the guess that minimizes squared error is the average of the possibilities, weighted by how likely each is. So at high noise the predicted clean point sits near the center of the data, on no arm at all; at low noise the weight concentrates on one nearby point and the guess is sharp. This is why a single denoising step from heavy noise would give a blurry mush, and why diffusion takes many small steps: each step only has to be right about the next, slightly less ambiguous, noise level.

The readout shows a twist worth predicting before you read it: at high noise the noise error is tiny even though the clean-image guess is poor. A heavily noised point is almost entirely noise, so "the noise is roughly the point itself" is a good answer. At low noise it flips: the image is nearly determined but the small noise is hard to see, and the noise error is large. The same network is therefore solving very different problems at different t, which is why later papers (EDM, Chapter 6) rescale its inputs and outputs per noise level.

For a finite dataset \{x^{(1)}, \dots, x^{(N)}\} this optimal guess has a closed form, which is what the plot computes. It is Bayes' rule, not a formula from the papers:

\htmlClass{t-blue}{\hat x_0^{\star}(x_t)} = \sum_i w_i\, \htmlClass{t-yellow}{x^{(i)}}, \qquad w_i \propto \exp\!\left(-\frac{\lVert x_t - \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x^{(i)} \rVert^2}{2(1-\bar\alpha_t)}\right)

Each weight asks "if the clean point were x^{(i)}, how likely is this noisy point?" and the weights sum to one.

Where the loss comes from: the variational bound, gently

The principled way to train a probabilistic model is maximum likelihood: make the training images as probable as possible under the model. For a diffusion model that probability is an integral over every possible noisy path that could have led to the image, which is hopeless to compute. The standard workaround, used by VAEs too, is a variational bound (also called the ELBO): a quantity you can compute that is always below the true log-likelihood, so pushing it up pushes the likelihood up.

For diffusion the bound breaks into one term per step. Term t compares the model's reverse step p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t) with the true reverse step you could compute if you were also told the clean image. That cheat sheet is a plain Gaussian (Eqs. 6 and 7 in DDPM v2):

q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, \htmlClass{t-yellow}{x_0}) = \mathcal{N}\big(x_{t-1};\; \tilde\mu_t(x_t, \htmlClass{t-yellow}{x_0}),\; \tilde\beta_t I\big), \quad \tilde\mu_t = \frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\beta_t}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\htmlClass{t-yellow}{x_0} + \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1-\bar\alpha_t}x_t

In words: the best previous point is a blend of where you are and where the clean image is. The model does not know x_0, so it plugs in its own guess \hat x_0. Comparing two Gaussians with the same spread reduces to the squared distance between their centers, and after substituting the formula for \hat x_0 that distance is a squared error on the noise, times a weight that depends on t (Eq. 12 in DDPM v2). DDPM's key empirical move was to drop that weight:

L_{\text{simple}}(\theta) = \htmlData{step=1, link=sample}{\mathbb{E}_{t,\, x_0,\, \epsilon}}\Big[\big\lVert \htmlData{step=2, link=noise}{\htmlClass{t-brown}{\epsilon}} - \htmlData{step=3, link=pred}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{\epsilon_\theta\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon,\; t\big)}} \big\rVert^2\Big]

Eq. 14 in DDPM v2

  • \mathbb{E}_{t, x_0, \epsilon}: average over a random step t (uniform from 1 to T), a random training image, and random noise. Each training iteration draws one of each.
  • \htmlClass{t-brown}{\epsilon}: the noise actually added. \htmlClass{t-blue}{\epsilon_\theta(\cdot)}: the network's guess from the noisy input.
  • \lVert\cdot\rVert^2: squared length of the difference. That is the whole training loop: add noise, predict it, take a gradient step on the error.

Dropping the weight is not free. It is no longer the exact bound, and it spends relatively less effort on the easiest, lowest-noise steps. It traded likelihood for looks: on CIFAR-10, training on the true bound gave FID 13.51 and 3.70 bits per dimension, while L_{\text{simple}} gave FID 3.17 at 3.75 bits per dimension (lower is better for both; FID is explained in the next chapter). DDPM also noticed the loss "resembles denoising score matching over multiple noise scales", a thread Chapter 5 pulls on.

Improved DDPM: also learn how much noise to add back

DDPM fixed the variance of each reverse step to one of two hand-picked values, \beta_t or \tilde\beta_t. Improved DDPM lets the network output a number v per pixel and interpolates between them, \Sigma_\theta = \exp(v \log\beta_t + (1-v)\log\tilde\beta_t) (Eq. 15), trained with a small dose of the true bound: L_{\text{hybrid}} = L_{\text{simple}} + \lambda L_{\text{vlb}} with \lambda = 0.001 (Eq. 16). Training on the importance-sampled bound alone with the cosine schedule reached 2.94 bits per dimension on CIFAR-10 (at a much worse FID; the hybrid loss is the practical compromise), and, as the next chapter shows, the learned variance is what let it sample in far fewer steps.

Sampling: walking back from noise

To generate, start from pure noise and apply the learned reverse step once per noise level, a thousand times. Press play and watch a cloud of noise condense; the blue ghosts show each particle's current guess of where it will end up.

One reverse step

Each step removes the predicted noise, rescales, and then adds a little fresh noise back:

\htmlData{step=1, link=particles}{\htmlClass{t-green}{x_{t-1}}} = \htmlData{step=2, link=rescale}{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}}\Big(x_t - \htmlData{step=3, link=ghosts}{\frac{1-\alpha_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\,\htmlClass{t-blue}{\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)}}\Big) + \htmlData{step=4, link=fresh}{\htmlClass{t-brown}{\sigma_t z}}

Algorithm 2 (line 4) in DDPM v2; the same expression appears as Eq. 11 for the mean

  • \htmlClass{t-blue}{\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)}: the network's noise guess; subtracting a scaled copy moves the particle toward its current \hat x_0 guess.
  • 1/\sqrt{\alpha_t}: undoes the forward step's shrink.
  • \htmlClass{t-brown}{\sigma_t z}: fresh Gaussian noise z with a fixed size \sigma_t (DDPM tried \sigma_t^2 = \beta_t and \tilde\beta_t; both worked). It is skipped on the very last step.

Adding noise while trying to remove it sounds backwards. It is what makes each step a sample from the reverse distribution rather than a jump to its center: it keeps the particles spread over the whole spiral instead of piling onto the most typical spot, and it lets later steps correct earlier mistakes. DDPM pointed out that the update "resembles Langevin dynamics", a classic sampler that follows a density uphill while jittering, which is the bridge to Chapter 5.

Coarse first, details last

Watch the ghosts during play. Early on every particle's guess sits near the middle of the data (the average of everything); halfway through the guesses spread onto the arms; at the end each particle has committed to one spot. Real images behave the same way. DDPM tracked the quality of its \hat x_0 guesses along the reverse chain on CIFAR-10, and those numbers are plotted below from the paper's own data.

How image quality is scored: FID and IS

You cannot compute the likelihood of a GAN's samples, so image generation settled on two scores computed from a pretrained image classifier (Inception). FID (Frechet Inception Distance) compares the statistics of the classifier's features on generated and real images: lower is better, zero means identical statistics. IS (Inception Score) rewards samples the classifier finds confidently recognizable and varied: higher is better. Both are imperfect and depend on sample count and reference set, so compare numbers only within one table.

What it achieved, and what it cost

On unconditional CIFAR-10 (tiny 32x32 images), DDPM scored FID 3.17 and IS 9.46, better than the score-based NCSN before it (FID 25.32) and on par with the best GANs; on 256x256 LSUN scenes its samples matched ProgressiveGAN's quality. Diffusion was suddenly a serious contender.

The cost is the loop. Every sample needs one network evaluation per step, one after another. DDPM's CIFAR model took 17 seconds for a batch of 256 images on a TPU v3-8; the DDIM authors later estimated around 20 hours to draw 50,000 small images on a single consumer GPU, versus under a minute for a GAN, and nearly 1000 hours at 256x256. The 50,000 figure matters because that is how many samples an FID evaluation usually uses.

Try 10 steps and 3 steps above. With few, large steps the particles are pulled toward their blurry average guesses and cannot recover, so the spiral shrinks inward: the toy version of the blurry samples a naively shortened DDPM produces (with its larger-variance setting, a real shortened DDPM also drowns in leftover noise; see DDIM's table in Chapter 6). Improved DDPM's learned variances softened this a lot, giving good samples in 50 steps and near-optimal FID at 100 steps; below that, a different sampler, DDIM, did better (fewer than 50 steps). Understanding why takes a new point of view.

Act IIIThe score view and faster sampling

Scores, Langevin, and the SDE that unifies everything

A year before DDPM, a different group reached the same models from another direction: instead of predicting noise, learn an arrow field that points toward more likely data. Slide the noise level and watch the field; arrows fade where a network would never have seen a training example.

The score: an arrow toward more likely data

For a probability density p(x), the score is the direction in which the log-density rises fastest:

\htmlData{step=1, link=field}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{s(x)}} = \htmlData{step=2, link=uphill}{\nabla_x \log p(x)}

Definition used by NCSN v3 Sec. 2

The \nabla ("gradient") collects the slopes along each axis into one arrow. The score has a lovely property: you never need the density's normalizing constant, the number that makes it integrate to one and that is usually impossible to compute, because taking the log turns it into an additive constant and the gradient deletes it. That is why "learn the score" is a tractable goal where "learn the density" is not.

Given a score, Langevin dynamics turns it into a sampler: take a small step uphill, add a little noise, repeat.

\htmlData{step=1, link=particles}{\htmlClass{t-green}{\tilde x_t}} = \tilde x_{t-1} + \htmlData{step=2, link=field}{\frac{\epsilon}{2}\,\htmlClass{t-blue}{\nabla_x \log p(\tilde x_{t-1})}} + \htmlData{step=3, link=jitter}{\htmlClass{t-brown}{\sqrt{\epsilon}\; z_t}}

Eq. 4 in NCSN v3

With a tiny step \epsilon and many iterations, the jitter balances the uphill pull and the particles settle into exactly the distribution p. Compare it with DDPM's reverse step in the last chapter: move by the network's output, add scaled noise. Same shape.

NCSN: why one noise level is not enough

You still need the score of data, which you do not have. Denoising score matching gets it indirectly: add Gaussian noise of size \sigma to a data point and train a network to point from the noisy point back toward the clean one. The optimum of that game is the score of the noised data distribution. It is the same "denoise" task as DDPM in different clothes, and the two predictions are rescalings of each other:

\htmlData{step=1, link=field}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{\nabla_x \log p(x;\sigma)}} = \frac{\htmlData{step=2, link=guess}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{D(x;\sigma)}} - x}{\sigma^2} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \htmlClass{t-blue}{\epsilon_\theta} = -\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\;\htmlClass{t-blue}{\nabla_x \log p_t(x)}

Left: Eqs. 2-3 in EDM v2, where D is the best denoiser (the \hat x_0 guess). Right: the same fact in DDPM's variables.

Song and Ermon's NCSN (12 Jul 2019) found the catch. Press Small noise: the noised training samples hug the spiral, so a network would only ever be trained on a thin band, and its arrows everywhere else would be guesses. Starting a sampler from random noise means starting exactly where the arrows are untrustworthy. Press Large noise: now training samples fill the plane and the field is learnable everywhere, but it describes a blurred spiral.

The fix was to use both: train one network on a ladder of noise levels, from \sigma_1 = 1 down to 0.01 in L = 10 geometric rungs, told which rung it is on, and sample by annealed Langevin dynamics: a few Langevin steps at the largest noise, then the next level down, and so on (100 steps per level on images). Press Annealed Langevin. On CIFAR-10 NCSN reached an Inception Score of 8.87 and FID 25.32. A ladder of noise levels, a network told its level, a coarse-to-fine sampler: that is a diffusion model. DDPM's paper said as much.

Score SDE: let the ladder become a slope

Song and colleagues then asked what happens with infinitely many rungs (26 Nov 2020). The noising becomes a continuous random process, a stochastic differential equation (SDE). Read \mathrm{d}x as "the tiny change in x over a tiny time \mathrm{d}t":

\mathrm{d}x = \htmlData{step=1, link=drift}{f(x, t)\,\mathrm{d}t} + \htmlData{step=2, link=jitter}{\htmlClass{t-brown}{g(t)\,\mathrm{d}w}}

Eq. 5 in Score SDE v2

  • f(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}t: the drift, a deterministic push (for DDPM, a gentle shrink toward the origin).
  • g(t)\,\mathrm{d}w: the diffusion, a random kick; w is Brownian motion, and \mathrm{d}w is an infinitesimal Gaussian nudge.

Two choices recover the two earlier models. NCSN is a variance-exploding SDE, \mathrm{d}x = \sqrt{\mathrm{d}[\sigma^2(t)]/\mathrm{d}t}\;\mathrm{d}w (Eq. 9): no drift, noise piles up. DDPM is a variance-preserving SDE, \mathrm{d}x = -\tfrac12\beta(t)\,x\,\mathrm{d}t + \sqrt{\beta(t)}\,\mathrm{d}w (Eq. 11): shrink exactly as fast as noise is added, the continuous version of Chapter 2's bookkeeping.

A 1982 result by Anderson says any such process can be run backwards in time, and the reverse process needs just one extra ingredient, the score:

\mathrm{d}x = \big[f(x,t) - \htmlData{step=1, link=field}{g(t)^2\,\htmlClass{t-blue}{\nabla_x \log p_t(x)}}\big]\,\mathrm{d}t + \htmlData{step=2, link=jitter}{\htmlClass{t-brown}{g(t)\,\mathrm{d}\bar w}}

Eq. 6 in Score SDE v2

That is the whole generative model: learn \nabla_x \log p_t at every noise level with denoising score matching, then integrate this equation from noise back to data. DDPM's sampler is one discretization of it; NCSN's annealed Langevin becomes a Langevin "corrector" run at each noise level, and the paper's predictor-corrector samplers combine the two.

The deterministic twin: the probability flow ODE

The most consequential line in the paper is a quieter one. There is an ordinary differential equation, no noise at all, whose solutions have exactly the same distribution at every time as the SDE's:

\mathrm{d}x = \big[f(x,t) - \htmlData{step=1, link=half}{\tfrac12}\, g(t)^2\,\htmlData{step=2, link=field}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{\nabla_x \log p_t(x)}}\big]\,\mathrm{d}t

Eq. 13 in Score SDE v2

Half the score term, and the random kick is gone. Before you press the buttons, predict: will the two sets of paths end on the same points? Press Reverse SDE paths, then Probability flow ODE paths. The SDE paths jitter and end somewhere random on the spiral; the ODE paths are smooth curves, and each starting point always lands on the same spot. Across many particles both produce the same spiral.

Determinism buys three things. Any off-the-shelf ODE solver can take large adaptive steps (the paper cut score evaluations by over 90% without visible loss). The map from noise to data is invertible, so an image can be encoded into its noise. And the change-of-variables formula gives exact likelihoods: the same DDPM model scores 3.28 bits per dimension, against the bound of 3.75 DDPM reported for it (on discretized data, so the comparison is approximate). With better architectures the paper reached CIFAR-10 FID 2.20 and IS 9.89 with one model (a VE SDE), 2.99 bits per dimension with another (a sub-VP SDE), and the first 1024x1024 samples from a score-based model.

Fewer steps: DDIM and EDM

Once sampling is "follow a deterministic curve", speed becomes a numerical question: how few, and how well placed, can the steps along that curve be? Pick a sampler and a step budget and compare its path (green) with the exact curve (white).

DDIM: same model, a deterministic sampler

DDIM (6 Oct 2020, concurrent with Score SDE) started from an observation about DDPM's loss: it only depends on the one-shot marginals q(x_t \mid x_0), never on how consecutive steps are chained. So there is a whole family of different forward processes, most of them not step-by-step Markov chains, that share those marginals and therefore share the same trained network. Each has its own reverse sampler. DDIM's update reads naturally in three parts:

x_{t-1} = \htmlData{step=1, link=xhat}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\;\htmlClass{t-blue}{\hat x_0}} + \htmlData{step=2, link=direction}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}-\sigma_t^2}\;\htmlClass{t-blue}{\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)}} + \htmlData{step=3, link=fresh}{\htmlClass{t-brown}{\sigma_t\,\epsilon_t}}

Eq. 12 in DDIM v4, rewritten in DDPM's \bar\alpha (DDIM's own \alpha_t means DDPM's \bar\alpha_t, a notation trap)

  • \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\hat x_0: the predicted clean image, scaled to the next noise level.
  • The middle term re-adds the predicted noise at the next level's size: "keep the same noise direction, just less of it".
  • \sigma_t\,\epsilon_t: optional fresh noise. A knob \eta sets \sigma_t: \eta = 1 behaves like DDPM, \eta = 0 is fully deterministic.

With \eta = 0 the sampler is a fixed map from noise to image, and it may skip: jump from step 1000 straight to 950, 900 and so on, using only a subsequence of noise levels. DDIM also showed it is an Euler discretization, in the noise level rather than in time, of an ODE equivalent to the probability flow ODE from the last chapter (its Proposition 1). On the same trained CIFAR-10 model:

DDPM-like (\eta = 1)41.0718.368.015.784.73

At a thousand steps the original sampler is still marginally best, but DDIM degrades gracefully while the stochastic ones collapse. The paper summarizes this as 10x to 50x faster sampling for comparable quality. Because the map is deterministic, the same starting noise gives recognizably the same image at any step count, and interpolating between two noises interpolates between images.

EDM: one ODE, a handful of knobs

By 2022 there were many samplers and many training recipes, each described in its own notation. Karras and colleagues at NVIDIA (1 Jun 2022, "Elucidating the Design Space") rewrote them all as one ODE in terms of the noise level \sigma itself:

\mathrm{d}x = -\htmlData{step=1, link=rate}{\dot\sigma(t)\,\sigma(t)}\;\htmlData{step=2, link=direction}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{\nabla_x \log p\big(x;\sigma(t)\big)}}\;\mathrm{d}t

Eq. 1 in EDM v2

With EDM's preferred choice \sigma(t) = t this becomes \mathrm{d}x/\mathrm{d}\sigma = (x - D(x;\sigma))/\sigma: at every noise level, the direction of travel is from the current point toward the denoiser's guess. Then every earlier method is just a column of choices in one table (Table 1 in the paper): the schedule, the scaling, where the steps go, which solver, how the network's input and output are scaled, and which noise levels training emphasizes. Because the choices are independent, they could be improved one at a time. The two that matter most for sampling:

  • Where to step. Steps are spaced by \sigma_i = \big(\sigma_{\max}^{1/\rho} + \tfrac{i}{N-1}(\sigma_{\min}^{1/\rho} - \sigma_{\max}^{1/\rho})\big)^{\rho} (Eq. 5) with \rho = 7, \sigma_{\max} = 80 and \sigma_{\min} = 0.002 for images. Large \rho crowds steps near low noise, where the image's details are decided and errors are most visible.
  • How to step. Euler takes the slope at the start of a step and walks straight. Heun takes that trial step, measures the slope where it landed, and redoes the step with the average of the two slopes. Twice the network calls per step, but its error per step shrinks much faster as steps get smaller, so it wins overall.

Try the toy: switching DDIM's spacing to EDM's (Euler, EDM spacing) is what rescues the few-step samples here. Heun is subtler: at three steps its correction overshoots badly (look at the path error), but from about ten steps on it tracks the exact curve several times more closely than Euler for the same number of network calls. Higher-order solvers pay off once steps are small enough for their assumptions to hold. On real models, swapping in EDM's sampler alone, without retraining, cut the network evaluations needed for near-best quality by 7.3x for a VP model, 300x for a VE model and 3.2x for an ImageNet-64 ADM model with its DDIM sampler.

EDM also fixed training. The raw network is wrapped so it always sees inputs and targets of unit size (Eq. 7, "preconditioning"): at low noise it predicts a small correction to its input, at high noise it predicts the clean image directly, which answers the twist from Chapter 3. Training noise levels are drawn from a log-normal distribution concentrated on the middle levels that matter most. The result was CIFAR-10 FID 1.97 at 35 NFE unconditionally and 1.79 at 35 NFE class-conditionally, beating the previous records (1.85 and 2.10 for conditional and unconditional respectively) with 35 network function evaluations (NFE) instead of hundreds or thousands.

Act IVControl and scale

Steering: classifier guidance and classifier-free guidance

So far the model makes "some image". To ask for a particular kind, say "a dog", condition it on a label and then push the samples toward what is typical for that label. Split the spiral into two classes, pick one, and turn up the push.

Classifier guidance (ADM, 2021)

Dhariwal and Nichol's "Diffusion Models Beat GANs" (11 May 2021) did two things. First, a careful architecture search on the U-Net: attention at several resolutions, more attention heads, BigGAN-style residual blocks for resizing, and "adaptive group normalization", which injects the step and class information by scaling and shifting each layer's normalized activations. They call the result ADM. Second, classifier guidance. Train a separate classifier p_\phi(y \mid x_t) on noisy images, and at every sampling step nudge the mean of the reverse step up the classifier's gradient toward the label y:

x_{t-1} \sim \mathcal{N}\big(\htmlData{step=1, link=samples}{\mu} + \htmlData{step=2, link=push}{s\,\Sigma\,\nabla_{x_t} \log p_\phi(y \mid x_t)},\; \Sigma\big)

Algorithm 1 in ADM v4 (the "Beat GANs" paper)

  • \mu, \Sigma: the mean and variance of the ordinary, unguided reverse step.
  • \nabla_{x_t} \log p_\phi(y \mid x_t): "which direction makes this noisy image look more like class y to the classifier".
  • s: the guidance scale. Multiplying the gradient by s is the same as sharpening the classifier to p(y\mid x)^s, so larger s concentrates samples on what the classifier finds most unmistakably y.

On ImageNet at 256x256 this took a class-conditional model from FID 10.94 to 4.59 at scale 1 (Inception Score 186.70). At scale 10 the IS climbed to 283.92 but FID got worse again, 9.11, and recall, a measure of how much of the data's variety the samples cover, fell through 0.63, 0.52 and 0.32. The guided model beat BigGAN-deep's FID (4.59 vs 6.95) with much higher recall (0.52 vs 0.28): the title's claim, at the price of hundreds of network calls per image instead of one.

Classifier-free guidance (CFG, 2022)

A separate noisy-image classifier is a nuisance to train, and the CFG authors worried its gradients might act like an adversarial attack on the classifier rather than a meaningful direction. Ho and Salimans removed it (arXiv 26 Jul 2022; a short version circulated at a NeurIPS 2021 workshop, early enough for latent diffusion to use it). Train one network that is sometimes told the label and sometimes not: during training, replace the label with a "null" token some fraction of the time (0.1 and 0.2 worked equally well). Then at every sampling step run the network twice and extrapolate:

\tilde\epsilon_\theta(z, c) = \htmlData{step=1, link=cond}{(1+w)\,\htmlClass{t-blue}{\epsilon_\theta(z, c)}} - \htmlData{step=2, link=uncond}{w\,\htmlClass{t-blue}{\epsilon_\theta(z)}}

Eq. 6 in CFG v1

  • \epsilon_\theta(z, c): the noise prediction when told the label c; \epsilon_\theta(z): the same network with the null label.
  • Rewritten as \epsilon_\theta(z,c) + w\,[\epsilon_\theta(z,c) - \epsilon_\theta(z)]: start from the conditional prediction and push further along the direction by which knowing the label changed it.
  • That difference is, by Bayes' rule, the gradient of an implicit classifier p(c \mid z) \propto p(z \mid c)/p(z) built from the generative model itself. So this is classifier guidance without a classifier.

In the toy, predict before you drag: as w grows, will the samples spread over the whole inner arm or crowd somewhere? The push points away from what the other class looks like, so samples retreat to the part of their class that is farthest from the other one, and coverage falls. That is the same fidelity-for-diversity trade the real model shows below: FID (which punishes lost variety) is best with a little guidance, while IS (which rewards unmistakable samples) keeps rising.

The cost is two network evaluations per step instead of one. The benefit is a single knob that every text-to-image system since has exposed, often called the "guidance scale". Chapters 8 and 12 use it. Strong guidance also has a recognizable look, oversaturated and overly "typical" images, which is the toy's crowded inner coil in pixel form.

Diffuse in a compressed space: latent diffusion

Pixel-space diffusion spends most of its effort on detail nobody would miss. Latent diffusion first squeezes the image into a small grid with an autoencoder, and runs the whole diffusion process there. Pick a compression factor and see what the diffusion model is left to generate.

Two kinds of compression

DDPM's paper already noticed that most of a diffusion model's bits describe imperceptible detail. Rombach and colleagues (20 Dec 2021) separated the two jobs. A first-stage autoencoder handles perceptual compression: an encoder \mathcal{E} maps an image x to a latent z = \mathcal{E}(x) that is f times smaller along each side, and a decoder \mathcal{D} maps it back, trained with a perceptual loss and a small adversarial critic so textures come back sharp. The diffusion model then does the semantic work, deciding what is in the picture, on the latent grid:

L_{\text{LDM}} = \mathbb{E}_{\htmlData{step=1, link=encode}{\mathcal{E}(x)},\,\epsilon,\,t}\Big[\big\lVert \htmlClass{t-brown}{\epsilon} - \htmlData{step=2, link=cells}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{\epsilon_\theta(z_t, t)}}\big\rVert^2\Big]

Eq. 2 in LDM v2

It is DDPM's L_{\text{simple}} with x replaced by z. Nothing about diffusion changes; only the space does. To generate, sample a latent with any sampler from Act III, then decode it once.

How much to compress

Press through the factors. The number of cells the network processes falls with the square of f, and the cost with it. Reconstruction quality falls too, slowly at first and then sharply: R-FID 0.58 at f = 4, 1.14 at 8, 5.15 at 16 and 31.83 at 32. The paper found that small factors train slowly and very large ones stagnate, with f = 4-16 as the sweet spot; after the same 2M training steps (far less compute for the latent model), f = 8 beat pixel-space diffusion by an FID gap of 38. A 256x256 image becomes a 32x32x4 latent at f = 8.

In the paper's April 2022 revision (v2, which added classifier-free guidance), on class-conditional ImageNet at 256x256, LDM-4 with guidance scored FID 3.60, better than ADM-G, with fewer parameters (400M vs 608M), about a third of the training compute (271 vs 916 V100-days; 962 counting ADM-G's classifier) and several times the sampling speed (0.4 vs 0.07 samples per second on one A100).

Conditioning on text: cross-attention

The second contribution is a general way to condition on anything. A separate encoder \tau_\theta turns the condition y (a caption, a layout, a mask) into a sequence of vectors, and layers inside the U-Net let every latent cell look them up:

\mathrm{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \mathrm{softmax}\!\Big(\frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt{d}}\Big)V, \quad \htmlData{step=1, link=cells}{Q = W_Q\,\varphi(z_t)},\; \htmlData{step=2, link=text}{K = W_K\,\tau_\theta(y),\; V = W_V\,\tau_\theta(y)}

Sec. 3.3 of LDM v2 (unnumbered)

Each latent cell forms a query ("what am I looking for?"), each word of the caption offers a key ("what am I about?") and a value ("what do I contribute?"); the softmax picks which words each cell listens to. A 1.45B-parameter text-to-image LDM trained on LAION-400M captions reached MS-COCO FID 12.63 with classifier-free guidance, close to the far larger GLIDE (12.24). The openly released Stable Diffusion models descend from this recipe: DiT (next chapter), for example, uses Stable Diffusion's autoencoder, a fine-tuned version of the LDM f = 8 model.

Swap the U-Net for a transformer: DiT

Every model so far used a U-Net. Peebles and Xie asked whether the transformer, the architecture whose quality grows predictably with compute in language models, would do the same for diffusion. Cut the latent into patches, and choose a model size: the chart shows what the paper measured.

Patches are tokens

DiT (19 Dec 2022) works inside Stable Diffusion's autoencoder: a 256x256 image becomes a 32x32x4 latent. "Patchify" cuts that into p \times p squares and turns each into one vector, a token, so the transformer sees T = (I/p)^2 tokens for an I \times I latent: 16, 64 or 256 tokens for p = 8, 4, 2. Halving the patch size quadruples the tokens, and since every token attends to every other, the compute grows at least as fast, while the parameter count barely changes.

Telling every layer the noise level: adaLN-Zero

The network must know the step t and the class c. DiT tried four ways: appending them as extra tokens, cross-attention to them, and two variants of adaptive layer norm, where a small network maps (t, c) to a per-channel scale and shift applied after each normalization, like ADM's adaptive group norm. The winner, adaLN-Zero, also outputs a gate for each residual branch and initializes it to zero, so every block starts as the identity function and training eases each one in. At 400K steps the four gave FID 35.24 (tokens), 26.14 (cross-attention), 25.21 (adaLN) and 19.47 (adaLN-Zero), and adaLN-Zero was, with plain adaLN, the cheapest.

Quality follows compute

Before you press the buttons, guess which matters more for quality: the number of parameters (model size) or the compute per forward pass (which the patch size also controls)? The chart answers. DiT-S/2 and DiT-B/4 have about the same Gflops but four times different parameter counts, and nearly identical FID (68.40 vs 68.38). Across all twelve models, FID tracks forward-pass Gflops. The largest, DiT-XL/2 at 118.64 Gflops, 675M params, trained longer and sampled with classifier-free guidance, reached FID 2.27 on ImageNet 256x256 (9.62 without guidance), the best diffusion result at the time, while using far less compute per step than pixel-space ADM (1120 Gflops) though more than LDM-4's U-Net (104). That scaling behavior is why the next generation of systems, SD3 among them (Chapter 12), are transformers.

Act VStraight paths and one step

Rectified flow and flow matching: learn straight paths

The probability flow ODE's paths curve, and curved paths need many small steps. Two 2022 papers asked: why not train on straight paths? Pair noise with data, draw straight lines, and learn to move along them. Watch what happens where the lines cross.

Rectified flow: regress onto the straight direction

Liu, Gong and Liu (7 Sep 2022) start from the simplest possible path between a noise sample X_0 and a data sample X_1, the straight line, and train a velocity field v to point along it:

\min_v \int_0^1 \mathbb{E}\Big[\big\lVert \htmlData{step=2, link=lines}{(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{X_1} - \htmlClass{t-brown}{X_0})} - \htmlData{step=3, link=flow}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{v(X_t, t)}} \big\rVert^2\Big]\,\mathrm{d}t, \qquad \htmlData{step=1, link=lines}{X_t = t\,\htmlClass{t-yellow}{X_1} + (1-t)\,\htmlClass{t-brown}{X_0}}

Eq. 1 in Rectified Flow v1

  • X_t: the point a fraction t of the way along the segment. Note the direction of time: here t = 0 is noise and t = 1 is data, the opposite of DDPM.
  • X_1 - X_0: the segment's direction, which is also its velocity because the segment is crossed in unit time.
  • Generation: start at noise and follow \mathrm{d}Z_t = v(Z_t, t)\,\mathrm{d}t to t = 1.

Here is the subtlety. The noise and data are paired at random, so the segments cross each other (press Random pairs). At a crossing point, the "right" direction is ambiguous, and the least-squares answer is the average direction of all segments through that point at that time: v^{\star}(x, t) = \mathbb{E}[X_1 - X_0 \mid X_t = x] (Eq. 2). An ODE's solutions can never cross, so the learned flow rewires instead: paths bend away from each other at would-be crossings, while every time-slice keeps exactly the same distribution as the straight-line mixture. Press Learned flow to see the bends.

The flow's own endpoints give a new pairing of noise with data, the rectified coupling, and the paper proves it never increases any convex transport cost compared with the random pairing (Theorem 3.5): it moves mass no farther. Now the key move, reflow. Train a second flow on these new pairs. They came from a non-crossing flow, so their straight segments rarely cross, and the second flow's paths come out much straighter (the paper bounds how fast straightness improves, Theorem 3.7). Before pressing After reflow, predict: with one Euler step, which flow lands closer to the data? Then try 1, 2 and 4 steps on each.

A perfectly straight path is solved exactly by a single Euler step, Z_1 = Z_0 + v(Z_0, 0). On CIFAR-10, one Euler step of the plain flow gives FID 378 (the VP ODE: 451), one reflow brings it to 12.21, and a final distillation step to 4.85. With an adaptive solver the plain flow reached FID 2.58 at 127 evaluations.

Flow matching: the general recipe

Lipman and colleagues (6 Oct 2022) arrived at the same place from continuous normalizing flows, and made it general. The ideal objective is to match the network's velocity v_t(x) to the true velocity u_t(x) of some chosen path of distributions from noise to data, which nobody can compute. But if you build that path as a mixture of simple per-example paths, each ending at one training image x_1, you can regress onto the per-example velocity instead:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{CFM}}(\theta) = \htmlData{step=1, link=lines}{\mathbb{E}_{t,\; q(x_1),\; p_t(x \mid x_1)}}\,\big\lVert \htmlData{step=2, link=flow}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{v_t(x)}} - \htmlData{step=3, link=lines}{u_t(x \mid x_1)} \big\rVert^2

Eq. 9 in Flow Matching v2; Theorem 2 shows its gradient equals that of the intractable full objective

This is the same trick as denoising score matching, and the same trick as this page's ideal denoiser: the average of per-example targets is the right target. The recipe then leaves one choice open, the per-example path. Choose Gaussian paths that follow diffusion's schedule and you recover diffusion models (the paper shows this explicitly). Choose the optimal transport path, whose mean moves in a straight line \mu_t = t\,x_1 while its width shrinks linearly, \sigma_t = 1-(1-\sigma_{\min})t (Eq. 20), and each noise sample travels straight to its image at constant speed: rectified flow, derived independently. On ImageNet 64x64 the OT path reached FID 14.45, against 16.88 for the same method with a diffusion path and 17.36 for DDPM, using 138, 187 and 264 network evaluations respectively.

The payoff is conceptual as much as numerical. "Diffusion model", "score model" and "flow model" became three descriptions of one object: a learned vector field that transports noise to data, trained by regressing onto per-sample targets. Which path to pick, and which noise levels to emphasize in training, are design choices. SD3 (Chapter 12) is built on exactly this view.

Consistency models: jump to the end

Every deterministic sampler so far walks along the ODE path. But every point on one path ends at the same place. What if a network learned to jump straight from any point to its path's end? Pick a noise level and compare that jump with a plain one-step denoise.

A function that is constant along each path

Song, Dhariwal, Chen and Sutskever (2 Mar 2023) define the consistency function of a probability flow ODE: it maps any point x_t on a path to that path's starting point near the data, x_\epsilon. Its defining property is self-consistency:

\htmlData{step=1, link=jump}{\htmlClass{t-green}{f(x_t, t)}} = \htmlData{step=2, link=paths}{\htmlClass{t-green}{f(x_{t'}, t')}} \quad \text{for all } t, t' \in [\epsilon, T] \text{ on one path}, \qquad \htmlData{step=3, link=boundary}{f(x_\epsilon, \epsilon) = x_\epsilon}

Definition in Sec. 3 of CM v2

Press One denoising step first. From a high noise level the denoiser's guess \hat x_0 is the blurry average from Chapter 3: the arrows land in the middle of the data, on no arm. Now press Consistency jump: the arrows land on the spiral, each at the exact point its path would have reached after dozens of solver steps. That is the difference between "the average of where I could be going" and "where this deterministic path actually goes".

The boundary condition is built into the network so it cannot be forgotten: f_\theta(x, t) = c_{\text{skip}}(t)\,x + c_{\text{out}}(t)\,F_\theta(x, t) with c_{\text{skip}}(\epsilon) = 1 and c_{\text{out}}(\epsilon) = 0 (Eq. 5), the same skip-connection idea as EDM's preconditioning.

Two ways to train it

Consistency distillation starts from a trained diffusion model (the "teacher"). Take a noisy training point at level t_{n+1}, use the teacher to take one ODE solver step to the neighboring level t_n, and require the network to give the same answer at both points:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{CD}} = \mathbb{E}\Big[\lambda(t_n)\; d\big(\htmlData{step=1, link=jump}{f_\theta(x_{t_{n+1}}, t_{n+1})},\; \htmlData{step=2, link=paths}{f_{\theta^-}(\hat x_{t_n}^{\phi}, t_n)}\big)\Big]

Eq. 7 in CM v2

  • \hat x_{t_n}^{\phi}: the point one teacher solver step (Heun) further along the path.
  • \theta^-: a slowly updated copy of the network used as the target, with no gradient through it, a stabilizing trick borrowed from reinforcement and self-supervised learning.
  • d: a distance between images; a perceptual one (LPIPS) worked best.

Agreement between neighbors, chained back to the boundary condition, forces agreement along the whole path. Consistency training drops the teacher entirely, replacing its solver step with the known noise that produced the training point, so consistency models are also a standalone family of generative models.

Sampling is one network call: x = f_\theta(x_T, T). For more quality, re-noise the result to an intermediate level and jump again (Two-step). On CIFAR-10, consistency distillation scored FID 3.55 in one step and 2.93 in two, against 8.34 for the earlier progressive distillation at one step and 2.04 at 35 NFE for the EDM teacher itself (CM's own measurement; EDM reported 1.97). On ImageNet 64x64 the one-step gap was 6.20 vs 15.39. Consistency training from scratch reached 8.70 in one step. Because the jump can start from any partially noised image, the same model also does inpainting, colorization and super-resolution without retraining.

Where it landed: SD3

By 2024 the pieces were on the table: latent spaces, transformers, straight-path flows and classifier-free guidance. Stable Diffusion 3's paper put them together and ran the comparisons at scale. One of its central choices is which noise levels to train on; slide the distribution and see.

Which noise levels to train on

SD3 (Esser and colleagues, 5 Mar 2024) writes the rectified-flow path as z_t = (1-t)\,x_0 + t\,\epsilon and trains the network to predict the velocity \epsilon - x_0. Plain rectified flow draws t uniformly. But drawing some t more often is the same as weighting their loss more, so the timestep distribution is really a loss weighting, the same knob EDM tuned with its log-normal noise levels. The paper compared 61 formulations (different paths, prediction targets and timestep distributions), and the winner was rectified flow with the logit-normal distribution:

\pi_{\text{ln}}(t; m, s) = \frac{1}{s\sqrt{2\pi}}\,\frac{1}{t(1-t)}\,\exp\!\Big(-\frac{(\operatorname{logit}(t) - \htmlData{step=1, link=location}{m})^2}{2\htmlData{step=2, link=scale}{s}^2}\Big)

Eq. 19 in SD3 v1, with \operatorname{logit}(t) = \log\frac{t}{1-t}

In practice: draw an ordinary bell-curve sample u with mean m and spread s, and squash it with t = 1/(1+e^{-u}). The result concentrates training on intermediate noise and rarely visits the extremes. The paper's reasoning: near pure data or pure noise the best velocity prediction is simple (close to an average), while in the middle the network must really decide what the image is. The m slider moves the emphasis toward data or toward noise.

Resolution changes the picture. More pixels means more redundant evidence about the image at the same noise level: averaging many noisy pixels reveals the signal. So at 1024x1024 a given t is effectively less noisy than at 256x256, and SD3 shifts timesteps toward noise, t_m = \frac{\alpha t_n}{1 + (\alpha - 1)t_n} with \alpha = \sqrt{m/n}, the square root of the ratio of pixel counts (Eq. 23). The paper calls the constant-image argument behind it unrealistic and picked the final value, 3.0, with a human-preference study. Press High-resolution shift.

MM-DiT: two streams, one attention

The network is a DiT (Chapter 9) adapted for text. The caption is encoded by three frozen text encoders, two CLIP models and the large T5 language model, and becomes a sequence of text tokens. Image latent patches form a second sequence. Each stream keeps its own weights for its layers, because text and images are different kinds of data, but in every block the two sequences are concatenated for one joint attention, so image tokens read text and text tokens read the image. In the paper's comparison this "multimodal DiT" (MM-DiT) significantly outperformed a cross-attention DiT, a plain DiT and the UViT hybrid. The autoencoder was widened to 16 latent channels, whose reconstructions are markedly better (rFID 2.41, 1.56 and 1.06 for 4, 8 and 16 channels), which bigger diffusion models can exploit. Classifier-free guidance works as in Chapter 7, with each text encoder independently dropped during training.

The headline is scaling. Across model sizes up to 8B parameters, validation loss falls smoothly with size and training compute, and it tracks human judgments closely: the correlation between validation loss and human-preference ELO across scales is -0.982 (lower loss, higher preference). On the GenEval benchmark of prompt following, the largest model, preference-finetuned (DPO) at 1024x1024, scored 0.74, above DALL-E 3's 0.67. The paper also reports human-preference comparisons against commercial systems, read here only as "competitive", since the exact rates exist only in bar charts.

So the lineage of a modern image generator reads like this page's table of contents. Latent space from LDM, transformer backbone from DiT, straight-path flow training from rectified flow and flow matching, noise-level weighting in the spirit of EDM, classifier-free guidance, and an ODE sampler descended from DDIM, all resting on DDPM's "add noise, predict it" and Sohl-Dickstein's "destroy structure, learn to restore it".

Summary

A diffusion model learns to generate by learning to denoise. Noising data is free and exactly controllable, and at every noise level the best possible denoiser is an average over the clean data that could have produced the noisy input. A network trained with plain squared error on "which noise was added" (DDPM) therefore learns the score of every noised distribution (NCSN, Score SDE). Generation is integrating the resulting flow from noise to data, either stochastically (the reverse SDE, DDPM's sampler) or deterministically (the probability flow ODE, DDIM). Everything after that is engineering on four axes. Speed: better step placement and solvers (EDM), straighter paths (rectified flow, flow matching) and learned jumps (consistency models). Control: guidance that trades diversity for fidelity (ADM, CFG). Cost: a compressed latent space (LDM). Scale: transformer backbones whose quality tracks compute (DiT, SD3).
