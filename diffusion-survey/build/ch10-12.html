Act V: straight paths and one step

Rectified flow and flow matching: learn straight paths

The probability flow ODE's paths curve, and curved paths need many small steps. Two 2022 papers asked: why not train on straight paths? Pair noise with data, draw straight lines, and learn to move along them. Watch what happens where the lines cross.

Rectified flow: regress onto the straight direction

Liu, Gong and Liu (7 Sep 2022) start from the simplest possible path between a noise sample X_0 and a data sample X_1, the straight line, and train a velocity field v to point along it:

\min_v \int_0^1 \mathbb{E}\Big[\big\lVert \htmlData{step=2, link=lines}{(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{X_1} - \htmlClass{t-brown}{X_0})} - \htmlData{step=3, link=flow}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{v(X_t, t)}} \big\rVert^2\Big]\,\mathrm{d}t, \qquad \htmlData{step=1, link=lines}{X_t = t\,\htmlClass{t-yellow}{X_1} + (1-t)\,\htmlClass{t-brown}{X_0}}

Eq. 1 in Rectified Flow v1

  • X_t: the point a fraction t of the way along the segment. Note the direction of time: here t = 0 is noise and t = 1 is data, the opposite of DDPM.
  • X_1 - X_0: the segment's direction, which is also its velocity because the segment is crossed in unit time.
  • Generation: start at noise and follow \mathrm{d}Z_t = v(Z_t, t)\,\mathrm{d}t to t = 1.

Here is the subtlety. The noise and data are paired at random, so the segments cross each other (press Random pairs). At a crossing point, the "right" direction is ambiguous, and the least-squares answer is the average direction of all segments through that point at that time: v^{\star}(x, t) = \mathbb{E}[X_1 - X_0 \mid X_t = x] (Eq. 2). An ODE's solutions can never cross, so the learned flow rewires instead: paths bend away from each other at would-be crossings, while every time-slice keeps exactly the same distribution as the straight-line mixture. Press Learned flow to see the bends.

The flow's own endpoints give a new pairing of noise with data, the rectified coupling, and the paper proves it never increases any convex transport cost compared with the random pairing (Theorem 3.5): it moves mass no farther. Now the key move, reflow. Train a second flow on these new pairs. They came from a non-crossing flow, so their straight segments rarely cross, and the second flow's paths come out much straighter (the paper bounds how fast straightness improves, Theorem 3.7). Before pressing After reflow, predict: with one Euler step, which flow lands closer to the data? Then try 1, 2 and 4 steps on each.

A perfectly straight path is solved exactly by a single Euler step, Z_1 = Z_0 + v(Z_0, 0). On CIFAR-10, one Euler step of the plain flow gives FID 378 (the VP ODE: 451), one reflow brings it to 12.21, and a final distillation step to 4.85. With an adaptive solver the plain flow reached FID 2.58 at 127 evaluations.

Flow matching: the general recipe

Lipman and colleagues (6 Oct 2022) arrived at the same place from continuous normalizing flows, and made it general. The ideal objective is to match the network's velocity v_t(x) to the true velocity u_t(x) of some chosen path of distributions from noise to data, which nobody can compute. But if you build that path as a mixture of simple per-example paths, each ending at one training image x_1, you can regress onto the per-example velocity instead:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{CFM}}(\theta) = \htmlData{step=1, link=lines}{\mathbb{E}_{t,\; q(x_1),\; p_t(x \mid x_1)}}\,\big\lVert \htmlData{step=2, link=flow}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{v_t(x)}} - \htmlData{step=3, link=lines}{u_t(x \mid x_1)} \big\rVert^2

Eq. 9 in Flow Matching v2; Theorem 2 shows its gradient equals that of the intractable full objective

This is the same trick as denoising score matching, and the same trick as this page's ideal denoiser: the average of per-example targets is the right target. The recipe then leaves one choice open, the per-example path. Choose Gaussian paths that follow diffusion's schedule and you recover diffusion models (the paper shows this explicitly). Choose the optimal transport path, whose mean moves in a straight line \mu_t = t\,x_1 while its width shrinks linearly, \sigma_t = 1-(1-\sigma_{\min})t (Eq. 20), and each noise sample travels straight to its image at constant speed: rectified flow, derived independently. On ImageNet 64x64 the OT path reached FID 14.45, against 16.88 for the same method with a diffusion path and 17.36 for DDPM, using 138, 187 and 264 network evaluations respectively.

The payoff is conceptual as much as numerical. "Diffusion model", "score model" and "flow model" became three descriptions of one object: a learned vector field that transports noise to data, trained by regressing onto per-sample targets. Which path to pick, and which noise levels to emphasize in training, are design choices. SD3 (Chapter 12) is built on exactly this view.

Consistency models: jump to the end

Every deterministic sampler so far walks along the ODE path. But every point on one path ends at the same place. What if a network learned to jump straight from any point to its path's end? Pick a noise level and compare that jump with a plain one-step denoise.

A function that is constant along each path

Song, Dhariwal, Chen and Sutskever (2 Mar 2023) define the consistency function of a probability flow ODE: it maps any point x_t on a path to that path's starting point near the data, x_\epsilon. Its defining property is self-consistency:

\htmlData{step=1, link=jump}{\htmlClass{t-green}{f(x_t, t)}} = \htmlData{step=2, link=paths}{\htmlClass{t-green}{f(x_{t'}, t')}} \quad \text{for all } t, t' \in [\epsilon, T] \text{ on one path}, \qquad \htmlData{step=3, link=boundary}{f(x_\epsilon, \epsilon) = x_\epsilon}

Definition in Sec. 3 of CM v2

Press One denoising step first. From a high noise level the denoiser's guess \hat x_0 is the blurry average from Chapter 3: the arrows land in the middle of the data, on no arm. Now press Consistency jump: the arrows land on the spiral, each at the exact point its path would have reached after dozens of solver steps. That is the difference between "the average of where I could be going" and "where this deterministic path actually goes".

The boundary condition is built into the network so it cannot be forgotten: f_\theta(x, t) = c_{\text{skip}}(t)\,x + c_{\text{out}}(t)\,F_\theta(x, t) with c_{\text{skip}}(\epsilon) = 1 and c_{\text{out}}(\epsilon) = 0 (Eq. 5), the same skip-connection idea as EDM's preconditioning.

Two ways to train it

Consistency distillation starts from a trained diffusion model (the "teacher"). Take a noisy training point at level t_{n+1}, use the teacher to take one ODE solver step to the neighboring level t_n, and require the network to give the same answer at both points:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{CD}} = \mathbb{E}\Big[\lambda(t_n)\; d\big(\htmlData{step=1, link=jump}{f_\theta(x_{t_{n+1}}, t_{n+1})},\; \htmlData{step=2, link=paths}{f_{\theta^-}(\hat x_{t_n}^{\phi}, t_n)}\big)\Big]

Eq. 7 in CM v2

  • \hat x_{t_n}^{\phi}: the point one teacher solver step (Heun) further along the path.
  • \theta^-: a slowly updated copy of the network used as the target, with no gradient through it, a stabilizing trick borrowed from reinforcement and self-supervised learning.
  • d: a distance between images; a perceptual one (LPIPS) worked best.

Agreement between neighbors, chained back to the boundary condition, forces agreement along the whole path. Consistency training drops the teacher entirely, replacing its solver step with the known noise that produced the training point, so consistency models are also a standalone family of generative models.

Sampling is one network call: x = f_\theta(x_T, T). For more quality, re-noise the result to an intermediate level and jump again (Two-step). On CIFAR-10, consistency distillation scored FID 3.55 in one step and 2.93 in two, against 8.34 for the earlier progressive distillation at one step and 2.04 at 35 NFE for the EDM teacher itself. On ImageNet 64x64 the one-step gap was 6.20 vs 15.39. Consistency training from scratch reached 8.70 in one step. Because the jump can start from any partially noised image, the same model also does inpainting, colorization and super-resolution without retraining.

Where it landed: SD3

By 2024 the pieces were on the table: latent spaces, transformers, straight-path flows and classifier-free guidance. Stable Diffusion 3's paper put them together and ran the comparisons at scale. One of its central choices is which noise levels to train on; slide the distribution and see.

Which noise levels to train on

SD3 (Esser and colleagues, 5 Mar 2024) writes the rectified-flow path as z_t = (1-t)\,x_0 + t\,\epsilon and trains the network to predict the velocity \epsilon - x_0. Plain rectified flow draws t uniformly. But drawing some t more often is the same as weighting their loss more, so the timestep distribution is really a loss weighting, the same knob EDM tuned with its log-normal noise levels. The paper compared 61 formulations (different paths, prediction targets and timestep distributions), and the winner was rectified flow with the logit-normal distribution:

\pi_{\text{ln}}(t; m, s) = \frac{1}{s\sqrt{2\pi}}\,\frac{1}{t(1-t)}\,\exp\!\Big(-\frac{(\operatorname{logit}(t) - \htmlData{step=1, link=location}{m})^2}{2\htmlData{step=2, link=scale}{s}^2}\Big)

Eq. 19 in SD3 v1, with \operatorname{logit}(t) = \log\frac{t}{1-t}

In practice: draw an ordinary bell-curve sample u with mean m and spread s, and squash it with t = 1/(1+e^{-u}). The result concentrates training on intermediate noise and rarely visits the extremes. The paper's reasoning: near pure data or pure noise the best velocity prediction is simple (close to an average), while in the middle the network must really decide what the image is. The m slider moves the emphasis toward data or toward noise.

Resolution changes the picture. More pixels means more redundant evidence about the image at the same noise level: averaging many noisy pixels reveals the signal. So at 1024x1024 a given t is effectively less noisy than at 256x256, and SD3 shifts timesteps toward noise, t_m = \frac{\alpha t_n}{1 + (\alpha - 1)t_n} with \alpha = \sqrt{m/n}, the square root of the ratio of pixel counts (Eq. 23). Press High-resolution shift.

MM-DiT: two streams, one attention

The network is a DiT (Chapter 9) adapted for text. The caption is encoded by three frozen text encoders, two CLIP models and the large T5 language model, and becomes a sequence of text tokens. Image latent patches form a second sequence. Each stream keeps its own weights for its layers, because text and images are different kinds of data, but in every block the two sequences are concatenated for one joint attention, so image tokens read text and text tokens read the image. This "multimodal DiT" beat cross-attention designs like LDM's. The autoencoder was widened to 16 latent channels, whose reconstructions are markedly better (rFID 2.41, 1.56 and 1.06 for 4, 8 and 16 channels), which bigger diffusion models can exploit. Classifier-free guidance works as in Chapter 7, with each text encoder independently dropped during training.

The headline is scaling. Across model sizes up to 8B parameters, validation loss falls smoothly with size and training compute, and it tracks human judgments closely: the correlation between validation loss and human-preference ELO across scales is -0.982 (lower loss, higher preference). On the GenEval benchmark of prompt following, the largest model scored 0.74, above DALL-E 3's 0.67. The paper also reports human-preference comparisons against commercial systems, read here only as "competitive", since the exact rates exist only in bar charts.

So the lineage of a modern image generator reads like this page's table of contents. Latent space from LDM, transformer backbone from DiT, straight-path flow training from rectified flow and flow matching, noise-level weighting in the spirit of EDM, classifier-free guidance, and an ODE sampler descended from DDIM, all resting on DDPM's "add noise, predict it" and Sohl-Dickstein's "destroy structure, learn to restore it".
