Act III: the score view and faster sampling

Scores, Langevin, and the SDE that unifies everything

A year before DDPM, a different group reached the same models from another direction: instead of predicting noise, learn an arrow field that points toward more likely data. Slide the noise level and watch the field; arrows fade where a network would never have seen a training example.

The score: an arrow toward more likely data

For a probability density p(x), the score is the direction in which the log-density rises fastest:

\htmlData{step=1, link=field}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{s(x)}} = \htmlData{step=2, link=uphill}{\nabla_x \log p(x)}

Definition used by NCSN v3 Sec. 2

The \nabla ("gradient") collects the slopes along each axis into one arrow. The score has a lovely property: you never need the density's normalizing constant, the number that makes it integrate to one and that is usually impossible to compute, because taking the log turns it into an additive constant and the gradient deletes it. That is why "learn the score" is a tractable goal where "learn the density" is not.

Given a score, Langevin dynamics turns it into a sampler: take a small step uphill, add a little noise, repeat.

\htmlData{step=1, link=particles}{\htmlClass{t-green}{\tilde x_t}} = \tilde x_{t-1} + \htmlData{step=2, link=field}{\frac{\epsilon}{2}\,\htmlClass{t-blue}{\nabla_x \log p(\tilde x_{t-1})}} + \htmlData{step=3, link=jitter}{\htmlClass{t-brown}{\sqrt{\epsilon}\; z_t}}

Eq. 4 in NCSN v3

With a tiny step \epsilon and many iterations, the jitter balances the uphill pull and the particles settle into exactly the distribution p. Compare it with DDPM's reverse step in the last chapter: move by the network's output, add scaled noise. Same shape.

NCSN: why one noise level is not enough

You still need the score of data, which you do not have. Denoising score matching gets it indirectly: add Gaussian noise of size \sigma to a data point and train a network to point from the noisy point back toward the clean one. The optimum of that game is the score of the noised data distribution. It is the same "denoise" task as DDPM in different clothes, and the two predictions are rescalings of each other:

\htmlData{step=1, link=field}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{\nabla_x \log p(x;\sigma)}} = \frac{\htmlData{step=2, link=guess}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{D(x;\sigma)}} - x}{\sigma^2} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \htmlClass{t-blue}{\epsilon_\theta} = -\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\;\htmlClass{t-blue}{\nabla_x \log p_t(x)}

Left: Eqs. 2-3 in EDM v2, where D is the best denoiser (the \hat x_0 guess). Right: the same fact in DDPM's variables.

Song and Ermon's NCSN (12 Jul 2019) found the catch. Press Small noise: the noised training samples hug the spiral, so a network would only ever be trained on a thin band, and its arrows everywhere else would be guesses. Starting a sampler from random noise means starting exactly where the arrows are untrustworthy. Press Large noise: now training samples fill the plane and the field is learnable everywhere, but it describes a blurred spiral.

The fix was to use both: train one network on a ladder of noise levels, from \sigma_1 = 1 down to 0.01 in L = 10 geometric rungs, told which rung it is on, and sample by annealed Langevin dynamics: a few Langevin steps at the largest noise, then the next level down, and so on (100 steps per level on images). Press Annealed Langevin. On CIFAR-10 NCSN reached an Inception Score of 8.87 and FID 25.32. A ladder of noise levels, a network told its level, a coarse-to-fine sampler: that is a diffusion model. DDPM's paper said as much.

Score SDE: let the ladder become a slope

Song and colleagues then asked what happens with infinitely many rungs (26 Nov 2020). The noising becomes a continuous random process, a stochastic differential equation (SDE). Read \mathrm{d}x as "the tiny change in x over a tiny time \mathrm{d}t":

\mathrm{d}x = \htmlData{step=1, link=drift}{f(x, t)\,\mathrm{d}t} + \htmlData{step=2, link=jitter}{\htmlClass{t-brown}{g(t)\,\mathrm{d}w}}

Eq. 5 in Score SDE v2

  • f(x,t)\,\mathrm{d}t: the drift, a deterministic push (for DDPM, a gentle shrink toward the origin).
  • g(t)\,\mathrm{d}w: the diffusion, a random kick; w is Brownian motion, and \mathrm{d}w is an infinitesimal Gaussian nudge.

Two choices recover the two earlier models. NCSN is a variance-exploding SDE, \mathrm{d}x = \sqrt{\mathrm{d}[\sigma^2(t)]/\mathrm{d}t}\;\mathrm{d}w (Eq. 9): no drift, noise piles up. DDPM is a variance-preserving SDE, \mathrm{d}x = -\tfrac12\beta(t)\,x\,\mathrm{d}t + \sqrt{\beta(t)}\,\mathrm{d}w (Eq. 11): shrink exactly as fast as noise is added, the continuous version of Chapter 2's bookkeeping.

A 1982 result by Anderson says any such process can be run backwards in time, and the reverse process needs just one extra ingredient, the score:

\mathrm{d}x = \big[f(x,t) - \htmlData{step=1, link=field}{g(t)^2\,\htmlClass{t-blue}{\nabla_x \log p_t(x)}}\big]\,\mathrm{d}t + \htmlData{step=2, link=jitter}{\htmlClass{t-brown}{g(t)\,\mathrm{d}\bar w}}

Eq. 6 in Score SDE v2

That is the whole generative model: learn \nabla_x \log p_t at every noise level with denoising score matching, then integrate this equation from noise back to data. DDPM's sampler and NCSN's annealed Langevin are two particular ways to discretize it.

The deterministic twin: the probability flow ODE

The most consequential line in the paper is a quieter one. There is an ordinary differential equation, no noise at all, whose solutions have exactly the same distribution at every time as the SDE's:

\mathrm{d}x = \big[f(x,t) - \htmlData{step=1, link=half}{\tfrac12}\, g(t)^2\,\htmlData{step=2, link=field}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{\nabla_x \log p_t(x)}}\big]\,\mathrm{d}t

Eq. 13 in Score SDE v2

Half the score term, and the random kick is gone. Before you press the buttons, predict: will the two sets of paths end on the same points? Press Reverse SDE paths, then Probability flow ODE paths. The SDE paths jitter and end somewhere random on the spiral; the ODE paths are smooth curves, and each starting point always lands on the same spot. Across many particles both produce the same spiral.

Determinism buys three things. Any off-the-shelf ODE solver can take large adaptive steps (the paper cut score evaluations by over 90% without visible loss). The map from noise to data is invertible, so an image can be encoded into its noise. And the change-of-variables formula gives exact likelihoods: the same DDPM model scores 3.28 bits per dimension exactly, against its bound of 3.70. With a better architecture the paper reached CIFAR-10 FID 2.20, IS 9.89, 2.99 bits per dimension, and the first 1024x1024 samples from a score-based model.

Fewer steps: DDIM and EDM

Once sampling is "follow a deterministic curve", speed becomes a numerical question: how few, and how well placed, can the steps along that curve be? Pick a sampler and a step budget and compare its path (green) with the exact curve (white).

DDIM: same model, a deterministic sampler

DDIM (6 Oct 2020, concurrent with Score SDE) started from an observation about DDPM's loss: it only depends on the one-shot marginals q(x_t \mid x_0), never on how consecutive steps are chained. So there is a whole family of different forward processes, most of them not step-by-step Markov chains, that share those marginals and therefore share the same trained network. Each has its own reverse sampler. DDIM's update reads naturally in three parts:

x_{t-1} = \htmlData{step=1, link=xhat}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\;\htmlClass{t-blue}{\hat x_0}} + \htmlData{step=2, link=direction}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}-\sigma_t^2}\;\htmlClass{t-blue}{\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)}} + \htmlData{step=3, link=fresh}{\htmlClass{t-brown}{\sigma_t\,\epsilon_t}}

Eq. 12 in DDIM v4, rewritten in DDPM's \bar\alpha (DDIM's own \alpha_t means DDPM's \bar\alpha_t, a notation trap)

  • \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\hat x_0: the predicted clean image, scaled to the next noise level.
  • The middle term re-adds the predicted noise at the next level's size: "keep the same noise direction, just less of it".
  • \sigma_t\,\epsilon_t: optional fresh noise. A knob \eta sets \sigma_t: \eta = 1 behaves like DDPM, \eta = 0 is fully deterministic.

With \eta = 0 the sampler is a fixed map from noise to image, and it may skip: jump from step 1000 straight to 950, 900 and so on, using only a subsequence of noise levels. DDIM also showed it is a simple (Euler) discretization of the probability flow ODE from the last chapter (its Proposition 1). On the same trained CIFAR-10 model:

At a thousand steps the original sampler is still marginally best, but DDIM degrades gracefully while the stochastic ones collapse. The paper summarizes this as 10x to 50x faster sampling for comparable quality. Because the map is deterministic, the same starting noise gives recognizably the same image at any step count, and interpolating between two noises interpolates between images.

EDM: one ODE, a handful of knobs

By 2022 there were many samplers and many training recipes, each described in its own notation. Karras and colleagues at NVIDIA (1 Jun 2022, "Elucidating the Design Space") rewrote them all as one ODE in terms of the noise level \sigma itself:

\mathrm{d}x = -\htmlData{step=1, link=rate}{\dot\sigma(t)\,\sigma(t)}\;\htmlData{step=2, link=direction}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{\nabla_x \log p\big(x;\sigma(t)\big)}}\;\mathrm{d}t

Eq. 1 in EDM v2

With EDM's preferred choice \sigma(t) = t this becomes \mathrm{d}x/\mathrm{d}\sigma = (x - D(x;\sigma))/\sigma: at every noise level, the direction of travel is from the current point toward the denoiser's guess. Then every earlier method is just a row of choices in one table (Table 1 in the paper): the schedule, the scaling, where the steps go, which solver, how the network's input and output are scaled, and which noise levels training emphasizes. Because the choices are independent, they could be improved one at a time. The two that matter most for sampling:

  • Where to step. Steps are spaced by \sigma_i = \big(\sigma_{\max}^{1/\rho} + \tfrac{i}{N-1}(\sigma_{\min}^{1/\rho} - \sigma_{\max}^{1/\rho})\big)^{\rho} (Eq. 5) with \rho = 7, \sigma_{\max} = 80 and \sigma_{\min} = 0.002 for images. Large \rho crowds steps near low noise, where the image's details are decided and errors are most visible.
  • How to step. Euler takes the slope at the start of a step and walks straight. Heun takes that trial step, measures the slope where it landed, and redoes the step with the average of the two slopes. Twice the network calls per step, but its error per step shrinks much faster as steps get smaller, so it wins overall.

Try the toy: switching DDIM's spacing to EDM's (Euler, EDM spacing) is what rescues the few-step samples here, and Heun then tracks the exact curve most closely. On real models, swapping in EDM's sampler alone, without retraining, cut the network evaluations needed for near-best quality by 7.3x for a VP model, 300x for a VE model and 3.2x for DDIM.

EDM also fixed training. The raw network is wrapped so it always sees inputs and targets of unit size (Eq. 7, "preconditioning"): at low noise it predicts a small correction to its input, at high noise it predicts the clean image directly, which answers the twist from Chapter 3. Training noise levels are drawn from a log-normal distribution concentrated on the middle levels that matter most. The result was CIFAR-10 FID 1.97 at 35 NFE unconditionally and 1.79 at 35 NFE class-conditionally, beating the previous records (1.85 and 2.10) with 35 network function evaluations (NFE) instead of hundreds or thousands.
