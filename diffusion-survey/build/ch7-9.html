Act IV: control and scale

Steering: classifier guidance and classifier-free guidance

So far the model makes "some image". To ask for a particular kind, say "a dog", condition it on a label and then push the samples toward what is typical for that label. Split the spiral into two classes, pick one, and turn up the push.

Classifier guidance (ADM, 2021)

Dhariwal and Nichol's "Diffusion Models Beat GANs" (11 May 2021) did two things. First, a careful architecture search on the U-Net: attention at several resolutions, more attention heads, BigGAN-style residual blocks for resizing, and "adaptive group normalization", which injects the step and class information by scaling and shifting each layer's normalized activations. They call the result ADM. Second, classifier guidance. Train a separate classifier p_\phi(y \mid x_t) on noisy images, and at every sampling step nudge the mean of the reverse step up the classifier's gradient toward the label y:

x_{t-1} \sim \mathcal{N}\big(\htmlData{step=1, link=samples}{\mu} + \htmlData{step=2, link=push}{s\,\Sigma\,\nabla_{x_t} \log p_\phi(y \mid x_t)},\; \Sigma\big)

Algorithm 1 in ADM v4 (the "Beat GANs" paper)

  • \mu, \Sigma: the mean and variance of the ordinary, unguided reverse step.
  • \nabla_{x_t} \log p_\phi(y \mid x_t): "which direction makes this noisy image look more like class y to the classifier".
  • s: the guidance scale. Multiplying the gradient by s is the same as sharpening the classifier to p(y\mid x)^s, so larger s concentrates samples on what the classifier finds most unmistakably y.

On ImageNet at 256x256 this took a class-conditional model from FID 10.94 to 4.59 at scale 1 (Inception Score 186.70). At scale 10 the IS climbed to 283.92 but FID got worse again, 9.11, and recall, a measure of how much of the data's variety the samples cover, fell through 0.63, 0.52 and 0.32. The guided model beat BigGAN-deep's FID (4.59 vs 6.95) with much higher recall (0.52 vs 0.28): the title's claim, at the price of hundreds of network calls per image instead of one.

Classifier-free guidance (CFG, 2022)

A separate noisy-image classifier is a nuisance to train, and its gradients can be adversarial noise rather than meaningful direction. Ho and Salimans (26 Jul 2022) removed it. Train one network that is sometimes told the label and sometimes not: during training, replace the label with a "null" token some fraction of the time (0.1 and 0.2 worked equally well). Then at every sampling step run the network twice and extrapolate:

\tilde\epsilon_\theta(z, c) = \htmlData{step=1, link=cond}{(1+w)\,\htmlClass{t-blue}{\epsilon_\theta(z, c)}} - \htmlData{step=2, link=uncond}{w\,\htmlClass{t-blue}{\epsilon_\theta(z)}}

Eq. 6 in CFG v1

  • \epsilon_\theta(z, c): the noise prediction when told the label c; \epsilon_\theta(z): the same network with the null label.
  • Rewritten as \epsilon_\theta(z,c) + w\,[\epsilon_\theta(z,c) - \epsilon_\theta(z)]: start from the conditional prediction and push further along the direction by which knowing the label changed it.
  • That difference is, by Bayes' rule, the gradient of an implicit classifier p(c \mid z) \propto p(z \mid c)/p(z) built from the generative model itself. So this is classifier guidance without a classifier.

In the toy, predict before you drag: as w grows, will the samples spread over the whole inner arm or crowd somewhere? The push points away from what the other class looks like, so samples retreat to the part of their class least like the other one, the innermost coil, and coverage falls. That is the same fidelity-for-diversity trade the real model shows below: FID (which punishes lost variety) is best with a little guidance, while IS (which rewards unmistakable samples) keeps rising.

The cost is two network evaluations per step instead of one. The benefit is a single knob that every text-to-image system since has exposed, often called the "guidance scale". Chapters 8 and 12 use it. Strong guidance also has a recognizable look, oversaturated and overly "typical" images, which is the toy's crowded inner coil in pixel form.

Diffuse in a compressed space: latent diffusion

Pixel-space diffusion spends most of its effort on detail nobody would miss. Latent diffusion first squeezes the image into a small grid with an autoencoder, and runs the whole diffusion process there. Pick a compression factor and see what the diffusion model is left to generate.

Two kinds of compression

DDPM's paper already noticed that most of a diffusion model's bits describe imperceptible detail. Rombach and colleagues (20 Dec 2021) separated the two jobs. A first-stage autoencoder handles perceptual compression: an encoder \mathcal{E} maps an image x to a latent z = \mathcal{E}(x) that is f times smaller along each side, and a decoder \mathcal{D} maps it back, trained with a perceptual loss and a small adversarial critic so textures come back sharp. The diffusion model then does the semantic work, deciding what is in the picture, on the latent grid:

L_{\text{LDM}} = \mathbb{E}_{\htmlData{step=1, link=encode}{\mathcal{E}(x)},\,\epsilon,\,t}\Big[\big\lVert \htmlClass{t-brown}{\epsilon} - \htmlData{step=2, link=cells}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{\epsilon_\theta(z_t, t)}}\big\rVert^2\Big]

Eq. 2 in LDM v2

It is DDPM's L_{\text{simple}} with x replaced by z. Nothing about diffusion changes; only the space does. To generate, sample a latent with any sampler from Act III, then decode it once.

How much to compress

Press through the factors. The number of cells the network processes falls with the square of f, and the cost with it. Reconstruction quality falls too, slowly at first and then sharply: R-FID 0.58 at f = 4, 1.14 at 8, 5.15 at 16 and 31.83 at 32. The paper found that small factors train slowly and very large ones stagnate, with f = 4-16 as the sweet spot; at equal compute, f = 8 beat pixel-space diffusion by an FID gap of 38. A 256x256 image becomes a 32x32x4 latent at f = 8.

On class-conditional ImageNet at 256x256, LDM-4 with classifier-free guidance scored FID 3.60, better than ADM-G, with fewer parameters (400M vs 608M), about a third of the training compute (271 vs 916 V100-days, without guidance) and several times the sampling speed (0.4 vs 0.07 samples per second on one A100).

Conditioning on text: cross-attention

The second contribution is a general way to condition on anything. A separate encoder \tau_\theta turns the condition y (a caption, a layout, a mask) into a sequence of vectors, and layers inside the U-Net let every latent cell look them up:

\mathrm{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \mathrm{softmax}\!\Big(\frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt{d}}\Big)V, \quad \htmlData{step=1, link=cells}{Q = W_Q\,\varphi(z_t)},\; \htmlData{step=2, link=text}{K = W_K\,\tau_\theta(y),\; V = W_V\,\tau_\theta(y)}

Sec. 3.3 of LDM v2 (unnumbered)

Each latent cell forms a query ("what am I looking for?"), each word of the caption offers a key ("what am I about?") and a value ("what do I contribute?"); the softmax picks which words each cell listens to. A 1.45B-parameter text-to-image LDM trained on LAION-400M captions reached MS-COCO FID 12.63 with classifier-free guidance, close to the far larger GLIDE (12.24). The openly released Stable Diffusion models were built on this recipe, which is why "latent diffusion" and "Stable Diffusion" are often used interchangeably.

Swap the U-Net for a transformer: DiT

Every model so far used a U-Net. Peebles and Xie asked whether the transformer, the architecture whose quality grows predictably with compute in language models, would do the same for diffusion. Cut the latent into patches, and choose a model size: the chart shows what the paper measured.

Patches are tokens

DiT (19 Dec 2022) works inside Stable Diffusion's autoencoder: a 256x256 image becomes a 32x32x4 latent. "Patchify" cuts that into p \times p squares and turns each into one vector, a token, so the transformer sees T = (I/p)^2 tokens for an I \times I latent: 16, 64 or 256 tokens for p = 8, 4, 2. Halving the patch size quadruples the tokens, and since every token attends to every other, the compute grows at least as fast, while the parameter count barely changes.

Telling every layer the noise level: adaLN-Zero

The network must know the step t and the class c. DiT tried four ways: appending them as extra tokens, cross-attention to them, and two variants of adaptive layer norm, where a small network maps (t, c) to a per-channel scale and shift applied after each normalization, like ADM's adaptive group norm. The winner, adaLN-Zero, also outputs a gate for each residual branch and initializes it to zero, so every block starts as the identity function and training eases each one in. At 400K steps the four gave FID 35.24 (tokens), 26.14 (cross-attention), 25.21 (adaLN) and 19.47 (adaLN-Zero), and adaLN-Zero was also the cheapest.

Quality follows compute

Before you press the buttons, guess which matters more for quality: the number of parameters (model size) or the compute per forward pass (which the patch size also controls)? The chart answers. DiT-S/2 and DiT-B/4 have about the same Gflops but four times different parameter counts, and nearly identical FID (68.40 vs 68.38). Across all twelve models, FID tracks forward-pass Gflops. The largest, DiT-XL/2 at 118.64 Gflops, 675M params, trained longer and sampled with classifier-free guidance, reached FID 2.27 on ImageNet 256x256 (9.62 without guidance), the best diffusion result at the time, while using far less compute per step than pixel-space ADM (1120 Gflops) though more than LDM-4's U-Net (104). That scaling behavior is why the next generation of systems, SD3 among them (Chapter 12), are transformers.
