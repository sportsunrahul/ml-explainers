Pruning and Deep Compression

Most weights in a trained network are close to zero, and many of them can be set to exactly zero with little loss, especially if the network is trained a little more afterwards. Whether the zeros make the network faster is a separate question: a GPU kernel can skip only zeros that come in a shape it was built for.

Each cell is one weight. A row is one output channel: the weights that produce one output feature map. A column is one input. A GPU computes this layer as a matrix multiply, and the inputs are the dimension it sums over. The sparsity of the layer is the share of its weights that are zero.

Magnitude pruning is the simplest rule: weights with a small absolute value contribute little to the output, so set them to zero. Han et al. (NIPS 2015) made this work on large networks with three steps. Train the network, remove every connection whose weight is below a threshold, then train the survivors again so they make up for the loss. Repeating prune and retrain does better than pruning once: AlexNet went from 5x fewer weights in one aggressive step to 9x with iterations.

\htmlData{step=1, link=kept}{\htmlClass{t-green}{m_{ij}}} = \mathbf{1}\big[\, \htmlData{step=2, link=mag}{|w_{ij}|} > \htmlData{step=3, link=thr}{t} \,\big], \qquad \htmlData{step=4, link=pruned}{\htmlClass{t-brown}{w_{ij} \leftarrow 0 \ \text{where}\ m_{ij}=0}}, \qquad \htmlData{step=3, link=thr}{t = q \cdot \sigma_{\text{layer}}}

Our transcription of Han et al., arXiv 1506.02626v3, Sec. 4, which gives the rule in words: the threshold is "a quality parameter multiplied by the standard deviation of a layer's weights". Deep Compression (arXiv 1510.00149v5, Sec. 2) builds on this method and removes "all connections with weights below a threshold"; it does not restate the rule.

  • m_{ij}: the mask, 1 if weight (i, j) is kept and 0 if it is pruned. \mathbf{1}[\cdot] is 1 when the condition holds.
  • |w_{ij}|: the weight's magnitude, its absolute value. The sign does not matter, only the size.
  • t: the threshold. \sigma_{\text{layer}} is the standard deviation of the layer's weights, and q is a hand-set quality parameter: larger q, more zeros.

The results were large. AlexNet on ImageNet went from 61M to 6.7M weights, and its top-1 error moved from 42.78% to 42.77%. VGG-16 kept one weight in 13, with top-1 error 31.50% before and 31.34% after. The cost was training time: the pruned AlexNet took 173 hours to retrain, against 75 hours for the original.

Make a guess before you press "2:4". Both the unstructured pattern and 2:4 set half of the weights to zero. Which one keeps more of the total weight magnitude, and which one can a GPU turn into speed? Unstructured pruning keeps the most magnitude, because it removes exactly the smallest weights. The 2:4 pattern must remove two of every four inputs, even in a group where three weights are large, so the histogram shows some large weights removed. Whole channels lose the most. For speed the order reverses. A dense kernel multiplies the scattered zeros like any other number; sparse tensor cores skip half of the multiplies in a 2:4 layer; removing channels leaves a smaller dense layer that any kernel runs faster.

Why scattered zeros are not free

To skip unstructured zeros, a kernel must store where the survivors are, for example in compressed sparse row (CSR) format, and look each one up. Han et al. timed this for the fully connected (FC) layers of AlexNet and VGG-16 on a Titan X, with NVIDIA's dense library (cuBLAS) against its sparse library (cuSPARSE).

At batch 1 the pruned layers win, by 3.5x on average on the GPU. An FC layer on one input reads every weight once and does one multiply-add with it, so it waits on memory (see the roofline in chapter 1), and fewer weights means fewer bytes. At batch 64 the dense kernel reuses each weight for all 64 inputs and waits on arithmetic instead. Now the pruned layer is slower in every Titan X row, and in most of the paper's CPU and mobile rows too: the paper says "pruned network no longer shows its advantage". Li et al. say the same for convolutions: weight pruning "generates sparse convolutional kernels which can be hard to accelerate". Mishra et al. put a number on the bookkeeping: CSR indices can cost up to 200% of the values they describe.

Deep Compression: prune, share, Huffman-code

Deep Compression (Han, Mao and Dally, ICLR 2016) aims at storage, not speed: shrink a model until its weights fit in on-chip memory. It adds two steps after pruning, and their savings multiply.

Prune. The survivors are stored with their position. Deep Compression stores the gap to the previous survivor in a few bits instead of a full column index; if a gap is too long for those bits, it inserts a filler zero.

Share weights. Many surviving weights have nearly the same value, so give them exactly the same value. Within one layer, k-means clustering groups the weights into k clusters, and each weight stores only the index of its cluster: \log_2 k bits. A table of k full-precision centroids, the codebook, holds the shared values. This is quantization with a learned, uneven grid, unlike the evenly spaced integer grid of chapter 4. The paper initializes the centroids evenly between the smallest and largest weight, because that keeps a few centroids for the rare large weights. In the toy, one of those even starting points lands in the gap that pruning left, and it never gets a member.

The centroids are then trained. Every weight in a cluster is the same number, so the loss gradient for that number is the sum of the gradients of all its members:

\htmlData{step=1, link=centroid}{\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \htmlClass{t-yellow}{C_k}}} = \sum_{i,j} \htmlData{step=2, link=grad}{\htmlClass{t-red}{\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial W_{ij}}}} \frac{\partial W_{ij}}{\partial C_k} = \sum_{i,j} \htmlData{step=2, link=grad}{\htmlClass{t-red}{\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial W_{ij}}}} \, \htmlData{step=3, link=member}{\mathbf{1}(I_{ij} = k)}

Eq. 3 in Han, Mao and Dally, arXiv 1510.00149v5 (the indicator, a double-struck 1 in the paper, is written in bold).

  • C_k: the shared value (centroid) of cluster k, one codebook entry.
  • W_{ij}: the weight at position (i, j); its ordinary gradient \partial \mathcal{L} / \partial W_{ij} comes from normal backpropagation.
  • I_{ij}: the cluster index that weight (i, j) stores. \mathbf{1}(I_{ij} = k) is 1 for the members of cluster k and 0 otherwise, so the sum runs over the members only. The index assignment stays fixed; only the centroids move.

The storage gain of sharing alone is r = nb / (n \log_2 k + kb) (Eq. 1): n weights of b bits become n indices plus a codebook of k values. The paper uses 8-bit indices for convolutions and 5-bit indices for FC layers.

Huffman-code. Some clusters and some gap lengths are far more common than others. A Huffman code gives the common symbols short bit strings and the rare ones long strings, with no loss and no retraining. The paper reports a 20% to 30% saving from this step.

The toy reaches a smaller ratio than the paper, mostly because it keeps a third of its weights while AlexNet keeps a ninth. Fewer bits per code has a limit: with 4-bit convolution codes and 2-bit FC codes, AlexNet's top-1 error rose to 44.77%, 1.99 points worse (Table 6). And the shared-weight model was never timed, because the standard sparse libraries cannot look up a codebook. The speed table above is for pruning alone.

On a detector

Deep Compression's speed case is FC layers at batch 1. FC layers once took up to 38% of Fast R-CNN's time, but a modern detector with a feature pyramid is mostly convolutions, and a convolution reuses each weight at every pixel, like the batch-64 column above. So on a GPU, unstructured pruning makes a detector's file smaller, not its inference faster (our reading). For speed, the zeros need a structure the hardware skips: chapter 9 shows which ones, and the decision guide says when to use them.
