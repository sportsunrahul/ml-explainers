Knowledge distillation

A large trained network (the teacher) knows more than its top answer: it also knows which wrong answers are nearly right. Knowledge distillation trains a smaller network (the student) to copy the teacher's whole output distribution, made softer by a temperature, instead of only the correct label.

Start at T = 1 and look at the blue bars. The teacher is sure this image is a truck, and the other classes look like zero. They are not zero. In our toy, the teacher gives "bus" 0.047 and "cat" less than 0.001. That ratio is knowledge: a truck can look a bit like a bus, and not at all like a cat. Hinton, Vinyals and Dean give the same kind of example: "An image of a BMW, for example, may only have a very small chance of being mistaken for a garbage truck, but that mistake is still many times more probable than mistaking it for a carrot." The truck, bus and cat are our example; the BMW, garbage truck and carrot are theirs.

At T = 1 those small numbers barely affect a loss, because they are so close to zero. Now drag the slider to the right, or press "T = 3". Every bar moves toward the average, and the wrong classes become visible. The teacher now says bus 0.21 and cat 0.014, while our student says 0.096 for both. The red arrows show where the soft loss pushes the student: bus up, cat down. The student is right about "truck" but wrong about which mistakes are reasonable, and only a soft target can tell it so.

\htmlData{step=1, link=student}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{q_i}} = \frac{\exp(\htmlData{step=2, link=logits}{z_i} / \htmlData{step=3, link=temp}{T})}{\htmlData{step=4, link=teacher student}{\sum_j \exp(z_j/T)}}

Eq. 1 in Hinton et al., arXiv 1503.02531v1 (the source writes "exp" without the upright font).

  • z_i: the logit for class i, the raw score the network computes before the softmax. Hinton writes z for the student and v for the teacher.
  • T: the temperature. Dividing every logit by T shrinks the gaps between them, so a larger T gives a softer, more even distribution. The paper says T "is normally set to 1". The rows under the bars show the logits after this division.
  • q_i: the probability for class i. The sum over all classes j makes the probabilities add up to one.

Two losses, one weight

The paper trains the student on a weighted average of two objectives, and gives them in words, not as an equation. The first is the cross-entropy with the teacher's soft targets, computed at the same high temperature in both networks. The second is the ordinary cross-entropy with the correct label, computed from the same student logits at T = 1. The authors "found that the best results were generally obtained by using a condiderably [sic] lower weight on the second objective function". Later papers write the same recipe as one line:

\mathcal{L} = \htmlData{step=1, link=hard}{(1-\lambda)\,\htmlClass{t-red}{\mathrm{CE}}\big(y,\ \htmlClass{t-yellow}{q(z;1)}\big)} \;+\; \htmlData{step=2, link=tsq}{\lambda\,T^2}\, \htmlData{step=3, link=kl}{\htmlClass{t-red}{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\htmlClass{t-blue}{p(v;T)}\,\|\,\htmlClass{t-yellow}{q(z;T)}\big)}

Our transcription of Hinton et al. Sec. 2 (prose only), in the form of DeiT Eq. 2 (Touvron et al., arXiv 2012.12877v2). DeiT prints the KL arguments student first; the teacher-first direction here is our reading. Hinton writes a cross-entropy, which has the same gradient for the student (our reading).

  • p(v;T): the teacher's softmax at temperature T (blue bars); q(z;T): the student's (yellow bars); q(z;1): the student's ordinary output at T = 1.
  • \mathrm{CE}(y, \cdot): the hard-label loss, cross-entropy with the one-hot label y.
  • \mathrm{KL}(p \,\|\, q): the Kullback-Leibler divergence, how many extra nats it costs to describe the teacher's distribution with the student's. It is the cross-entropy minus the teacher's own entropy, which the student cannot change. The figure prints it at the top.
  • \lambda: the weight. The conventions differ between papers. Hinton's speech run used 0.5 as the relative weight on the hard term; DeiT puts \lambda = 0.1 on the soft term with \tau = 3.0, the opposite emphasis.
  • T^2: the factor the next section explains.

Why multiply by T2

Turning up the temperature has a side effect on training: it shrinks the soft loss's gradient. Hinton derives the gradient of the soft cross-entropy C with respect to one student logit:

\htmlData{step=1, link=plain}{\htmlClass{t-red}{\frac{\partial C}{\partial z_i}}} = \htmlData{step=2, link=temp}{\frac{1}{T}}\left(\htmlData{step=3, link=gap}{q_i - p_i}\right) = \frac{1}{T}\left(\htmlData{step=4, link=student}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{\frac{e^{z_i/T}}{\sum_j e^{z_j/T}}}} -\htmlData{step=5, link=teacher}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{\frac{e^{v_i/T}}{\sum_j e^{v_j/T}}}}\right)

Eq. 2 in Hinton et al., arXiv 1503.02531v1.

  • q_i - p_i: the gap between the student's and the teacher's probability for class i, the red arrows in the figure.
  • 1/T: a factor that comes from dividing the logits by T inside the softmax.
  • v_i: the teacher's logit. The teacher's logits are given inputs here; only the student's logits z_i are differentiated.

There are two shrinking effects, not one. The factor 1/T is visible. The gap q_i - p_i also shrinks, because at high temperature both distributions move toward uniform. When the temperature is large compared with the logits, Hinton expands e^x \approx 1 + x:

\htmlData{step=1, link=limit}{\frac{\partial C}{\partial z_i} \approx} \frac{1}{T}\left( \htmlData{step=2, link=student}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{\frac{1+z_i/T}{N + \sum_j z_j/T}}} - \htmlData{step=3, link=teacher}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{\frac{1+v_i/T}{N + \sum_j v_j/T}}} \right)

Eq. 3 in Hinton et al., arXiv 1503.02531v1. N is the number of classes.

If each logit vector has zero mean, the sums vanish and the result is short:

\htmlData{step=1, link=limit}{\frac{\partial C}{\partial z_i} \approx} \htmlData{step=2, link=plain}{\frac{1}{NT^2}}\htmlData{step=3, link=logits}{\left( z_i - v_i \right)}

Eq. 4 in Hinton et al., arXiv 1503.02531v1.

  • 1/(NT^2): the gradient falls as the square of the temperature. This is the dotted line in the figure.
  • z_i - v_i: at high temperature the student simply moves its logits toward the teacher's. Minimizing \tfrac{1}{2}(z_i - v_i)^2, the logit matching of earlier model-compression work, is therefore the high-temperature limit of distillation.

Make a guess before you press "with T2". Without the factor, the soft-loss gradient at T = 20 is about 55 times smaller than the hard-label gradient in our toy, so the hard label would drive training alone. What will the curve look like after you multiply by T^2? It stops falling. From about T = 6 on it stays near the Eq. 4 value, so the ratio between the two loss terms no longer depends on the temperature you pick. The paper's words: "Since the magnitudes of the gradients produced by the soft targets scale as 1/T^2 it is important to multiply them by T^2 when using both hard and soft targets." Note that "roughly unchanged" does not mean "equal": in our toy the soft term ends up about 7 times larger than the hard term, and the weight \lambda sets the final balance. At low temperature the factor does not hold the gradient constant, because the softmax is saturated there and the approximation does not apply.

Which temperature is right is a tuning choice. Hinton's small MNIST student used T = 20. For students with at least 300 units per layer, all temperatures above 8 gave fairly similar results; a 30-unit student did best at 2.5 to 4. The paper reads this as a sign that a very small student does better when it ignores the teacher's most negative logits, which "could be very noisy".

Inside distillation: forward, then backward

One training step runs the same image through two networks. The figure below steps through it with Hinton's MNIST pair. Make a guess before you press "6 Gradients": which boxes will the gradient reach?

The answer to the guess: the student only. The teacher sits upstream of the soft loss, but the gradient never enters its branch, because nothing there will be updated. A deep-learning framework skips that branch only if you tell it to, by freezing the teacher or computing its output without gradient tracking.

What distillation bought in the paper

On MNIST, the large teacher made 67 test errors. The small net trained on hard labels alone made 146. The same small net trained to match the teacher's soft targets at T = 20 made 74. These are counts from the prose of Sec. 3; the paper has no table for them.

The speech experiment is a different kind of compression. The teacher was an ensemble of 10 acoustic models, and the student was one model with the same architecture as each of them. So the gain is ten forward passes down to one, not a smaller network. "More than 80% of the improvement in frame classification accuracy" of the ensemble carried over:

The third result shows soft targets acting as a regularizer. With only 3% of the speech data, about 20M examples, a model trained on hard labels overfit badly; early stopping caught it at 44.5% test frame accuracy. The same model trained on soft targets reached 57.0%, close to the 58.9% of a baseline trained on all the data (Table 5). The soft-target model needed no early stopping. The authors' reading: "a lot of helpful information can be carried in soft targets that could not possibly be encoded with a single hard target."

Distilling a detector or a vision transformer

Hinton's method was built and tested on classifiers. Wang et al. give three reasons it transfers poorly to detection. "A detection model may only involve a few classes", so there is little inter-class similarity to distill. Detection also needs localization, which a softmax over classes does not describe. And background locations far outnumber objects. On their small one-stage detector, adding only Hinton's output loss to the quarter-width student raised mAP by just 0.9 points.

The alternative is to match features inside the network. FitNets (Romero et al.) does this with a "hint": a small regressor maps a middle layer of the student onto a middle layer of the teacher, and an L2 loss pulls them together before ordinary distillation. In their CIFAR-10 run the 11th student layer imitates the teacher's second layer, and a thin, deep FitNet with about 2.5M parameters beats its 9M-parameter teacher. On a detector, imitating the whole feature map hurts: it dropped the quarter-width toy detector by 8.9 mAP, because most of the map is noisy background.

Wang et al. imitate features only near objects. For each ground-truth box they compute its overlap (IoU) with every anchor on the last feature map that carries anchors, keep the locations whose IoU exceeds \psi times that box's best IoU, with \psi = 0.5, and add an L2 loss between an adapted student feature and the teacher feature at those locations only. The loss weight is not stated in the paper; the official code uses 0.01 (code-only). For an FPN detector they build the mask per pyramid level. DeiT (Touvron et al.) takes a third route for vision transformers: a learned distillation token sits beside the class token, and its output head is trained on the teacher's predicted class while the class head learns the true label.

Two cautions about these rows. The DeiT student is not smaller: DeiT-B has 86M parameters and its teacher 84M. The gain comes from learning from a different architecture, and plain soft distillation gave DeiT-B no gain at all (81.8). The Wang students, by contrast, are the teacher's own design with halved channels, which is the usual case for a deployed detector. Distillation also stacks with the other levers: a distilled student can still be quantized (chapter 4 onward) or pruned (chapter 8). Seen through the cost model, it is the only lever that cuts operations and bytes in every layer at once, but it is also the only one that needs a full training run.

On a detector

Do not expect logit distillation alone to help much: on Wang's toy detector it added under a point. Imitate the teacher's features near the objects, on each level of the feature pyramid, with a teacher of the same family (in their COCO run, a half-width Faster R-CNN R50-FPN gained 3.6 AP). It costs a training run with the teacher's forward pass on every image, so the decision guide places it after cheaper post-training quantization.
