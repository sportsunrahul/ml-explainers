Outliers: LLM.int8() and SmoothQuant

Large language models (LLMs) grow a few activation channels that are far larger than all the others, and those channels are large in almost every token. One INT8 scale for the whole tensor then spends nearly all of its 256 levels on those few channels, and every other channel rounds to almost nothing.

The front surface is the input of one linear layer: one row per token, one column per channel. Three ridges run through every token. With one scale for the whole tensor, the step between levels is set by the tallest ridge, and a typical channel spans only about 3 of the 256 levels. SmoothQuant computes the same quantity for a real layer and gets 2-3 levels for the non-outlier channels (Sec. 3). That is why a typical channel in the toy is off by almost half its own size, and why the floor of the surface is red.

How large are the outliers? The two papers measure different models and say it differently, so here are both, unmerged. Dettmers et al. find feature dimensions up to 20x larger than the others, which "usually have a range of [-3.5, 3.5]" (3-20x in their Table 4). Xiao et al. (SmoothQuant) write that outliers are "~100x larger than most of the activation values", and their Fig. 4 shows channels "greater than 70" in OPT-13B.

Claim card: outliers emerge with scale

Claim
Transformers develop a few fixed feature dimensions with very large values. In the 6.7B model they hold about 150k outliers per 2048-token sequence, all in 6 hidden dimensions. Measured by parameter count, they spread to every layer suddenly at 6.7B: from 65% to 100% of layers, and from 35% to 75% of tokens.
Their reading
Plotted against perplexity instead of size, the rise is smooth, and the number of outlier dimensions grows steadily as perplexity falls. The authors conclude that perplexity, not model size alone, decides when the phase shift happens (Sec. 4.2).
Definition
A dimension counts as an outlier if a value reaches at least 6.0, in the same dimension in at least 25% of layers and at least 6% of tokens.
Evidence
Setting the outlier dimensions to zero drops the mean top-1 softmax probability from about 40% to about 20% and raises perplexity by 600-1000%. Zeroing the same number of random dimensions raises it by only 0.1%.
Caveats
The thresholds were tuned so that the 125M model shows one outlier. The analysis stops at 13B. Our reading: the step to 6.7B also switches model family (GPT-J to fairseq), which could explain part of the jump.

Fix one: keep the outliers in 16-bit floating point (LLM.int8())

Dettmers et al. first make the scales finer. A matrix multiply is many independent dot products, one per row of X and column of W, so each token row gets its own scale and each output column of W gets its own scale. This is vector-wise quantization:

\begin{aligned} \htmlData{step=3, link=act wt}{\mathbf{C}_{f_{16}}} &\approx \frac{1}{\htmlData{step=1, link=act}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{\mathbf{c}_{x_{f16}}}}\otimes \htmlData{step=2, link=wt}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{\mathbf{c}_{w_{f16}}}}} \htmlData{step=3, link=act wt}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{\mathbf{C}_{i32}}} = S\cdot\mathbf{C}_{i32} \\ &= \mathbf{S}\cdot\mathbf{A}_{i8}\mathbf{B}_{i8} = \htmlData{step=4, link=act wt}{\mathbf{S}\cdot \htmlClass{t-yellow}{Q(\mathbf{A}_{f16})\phantom{.} Q(\mathbf{B}_{f16})}}, \end{aligned}

Eq. 7 in Dettmers et al., arXiv 2208.07339v2.

  • \mathbf{c}_{x_{f16}}: one scaling constant per row of X (per token), from that row's largest magnitude.
  • \mathbf{c}_{w_{f16}}: one scaling constant per column of W (per output).
  • \otimes: the outer product, which gives one rescaling factor for each entry of the output.
  • \mathbf{C}_{i32}: the integer result of the INT8 multiply, accumulated in 32 bits; Q is the absmax quantizer and \mathbf{S} undoes both scales.

Per-token scales do not help with the problem in the figure, though. The outliers live in columns of X (channels), and every token row contains them. So the second step splits the multiply by channel:

\mathbf{C}_{f16} \approx \htmlData{step=1, link=outlier}{\htmlClass{t-green}{\sum\limits_{h\in O} \mathbf{X}_{f16}^h \mathbf{W}_{f16}^h}} + \htmlData{step=2, link=act}{\mathbf{S}_{f16} \cdot \htmlClass{t-yellow}{\sum\limits_{h\not\in O} \mathbf{X}_{i8}^h \mathbf{W}_{i8}^h}}

Eq. 8 in Dettmers et al., arXiv 2208.07339v2 (the mixed-precision decomposition).

  • O: the set of feature dimensions h that hold at least one value larger than a threshold, 6.0 in the paper. They are multiplied in FP16 (green).
  • h \not\in O: all other dimensions, multiplied in INT8 with the vector-wise scales of Eq. 7 (yellow).
  • \mathbf{X}^h, \mathbf{W}^h: column h of X and row h of W, so each sum is one partial matrix multiply.

Press LLM.int8() in the figure. The three ridges turn green and leave the INT8 grid, and the rest of the tensor gets per-token scales. A typical channel's error falls from almost half its size to under 2%. In a real model the set O is tiny: at most 7 dimensions up to 13B parameters, about 0.1% of all dimensions. So more than 99.9% of the values are still multiplied in 8 bits.

The method is about memory first. It cuts the footprint of BLOOM-176B by 1.96x and lets OPT-175B run on 8x RTX 3090 consumer GPUs. The paper states no degradation up to 175B. Its own evidence at that size is a zero-shot accuracy plot (Fig. 1); its perplexity table stops at 13B, where LLM.int8() matches 32-bit exactly (12.45 against 12.45 on C4). An independent check comes from SmoothQuant's Table 3 on OPT-175B: average zero-shot accuracy 66.7 against 66.9 for FP16, so "near-zero" is the safer word.

Speed is the weak point. The FP16 side multiply and the splitting cost time. In the paper's own matrix-multiply benchmark (Table 5, GPU not stated), LLM.int8() runs at 0.86x the speed of FP16 for a layer of the 6.7B model's size, and is faster only from 13B-sized layers up (1.22x, then 1.81x at 175B size). SmoothQuant measured whole models: OPT-13B in PyTorch on an A100, batch 4, 256 tokens, takes 237.1 ms with LLM.int8() against 152.6 ms in FP16 (Table 11).

Fix two: move the difficulty into the weights (SmoothQuant)

If the trouble is that channels differ, why not give each activation channel its own scale, as chapter 5 did for weights? SmoothQuant's Table 1 shows that this would work: simulated per-channel INT8 activations keep OPT-175B at 71.4 average accuracy against 71.6 in FP16, where per-tensor activations fall to 32.3. The problem is the integer matrix multiply:

\mathbf{Y} = \htmlData{step=1, link=act}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{\text{diag}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{X}}^{\text{FP16}})}} \cdot \htmlData{step=3, link=act wt}{(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{\mathbf{\bar{X}}^{\text{INT8}}\cdot \mathbf{\bar{W}}^{\text{INT8}}})} \cdot \htmlData{step=2, link=wt}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{\text{diag}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{W}}^{\text{FP16}})}}

Eq. 2 in Xiao et al. (SmoothQuant), arXiv 2211.10438v7.

  • \mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{X}}: one step size per token (a row of X). It multiplies whole rows of the output, so it can be applied after the integer multiply.
  • \mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{W}}: one step size per output channel (a column of W), applied after the multiply in the same way.
  • \mathbf{\bar{X}}^{\text{INT8}}\cdot \mathbf{\bar{W}}^{\text{INT8}}: the integer multiply. Each output sums over the input channels, so a different scale per input channel would sit inside that sum and cannot be pulled out.

So SmoothQuant changes the tensors instead of the scales. Divide each input channel j of X by a number s_j and multiply row j of W by the same number. The product does not change:

\mathbf{Y} = \htmlData{step=1, link=act}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{(\mathbf{X}\text{diag}(\mathbf{s})^{-1})}} \cdot \htmlData{step=2, link=wt}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{(\text{diag}(\mathbf{s})\mathbf{W})}} = \htmlData{step=3, link=act wt}{\hat{\mathbf{X}} \hat{\mathbf{W}}}

Eq. 3 in Xiao et al., arXiv 2211.10438v7.

\htmlData{step=3, link=s}{\mathbf{s}_j} = \htmlData{step=1, link=act}{\htmlClass{t-red}{\max(|\mathbf{X}_j|)^{\alpha}}} / \htmlData{step=2, link=wt}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{\max(|\mathbf{W}_j|) ^{1-\alpha}}}

Eq. 4 in Xiao et al., arXiv 2211.10438v7.

  • \mathbf{s}_j: the smoothing factor of input channel j, drawn as the line between the two surfaces. It is computed once, offline, and folded into the layer that produces X (for example the LayerNorm before it), so it costs nothing at run time.
  • \max(|\mathbf{X}_j|): the largest magnitude of channel j over calibration data (512 random sentences from the Pile in the paper).
  • \max(|\mathbf{W}_j|): the largest magnitude in row j of W, the weights that multiply channel j.
  • \alpha: the migration strength. At 1, all the difficulty goes to the weights (every channel of X ends with maximum 1); at 0, all of it stays with the activations (every row of W ends with maximum 1).

Make a guess before you drag alpha to 1. The weights started flat and easy. Where do the three ridges go? They move into the weights: the activation surface goes flat and blue, and three red ridges rise in the weight surface, because those rows now carry the large values. At 0.5 the two errors are both a few percent per channel. The paper uses 0.5 for all OPT and BLOOM models and finds a sweet spot of 0.4-0.6 on OPT-175B; other model families need more, for example 0.9 for Llama-2-70B. In the toy, the three big channels dominate the output, so the output error stays small in every setting; it still falls to under a third from no smoothing to alpha 0.5.

The rescaling algebra is the same one that cross-layer equalization uses for weights in chapter 6 (our reading); SmoothQuant applies it across the activation and the weight of one layer.

Because the scales fold away, every linear layer stays a plain INT8 matrix multiply, and the method needs no FP16 side path. On OPT-175B:

And it is faster than FP16, not slower. All speed results are on A100 80GB GPUs. In PyTorch, OPT-13B at batch 4 and 256 tokens takes 112.1 ms, against 152.6 ms in FP16 (Table 11). The best cases are 1.51x in PyTorch and 1.56x in FasterTransformer when processing the prompt, and 1.96x less memory. When generating one token at a time (OPT-30B, batch 1), the speedup is 1.35x. That last step is memory-bound, which is where the next chapter starts.

Vision transformers have a different problem

Vision transformers (ViTs) are not known to grow LLM-style ridges in the sources here. Their trouble is the shape of two distributions. After the softmax in attention, almost every value is near 0 and a few are near 1; after the GELU activation, the values are lopsided, with large positives and a small negative tail. One uniform grid fits neither. PTQ4ViT (Yuan et al., arXiv 2111.12293v3) gives each of these tensors two ranges with two scales. With it, DeiT-S at 8-bit weights and activations keeps 79.47% ImageNet top-1 against 79.80% in FP32, where a plain post-training baseline reaches 77.65%. The paper reports image classification only, with no detection results.

On a detector

None of these sources measures LLM-style outlier channels in a convolutional detector, so treat this as a diagnosis to run, not a result. Record the per-channel maximum of each layer input over your calibration images. If a few channels are tens of times larger than the rest, per-tensor INT8 activations will fail in that layer as they do here. The two fixes are the ones in this chapter: keep that layer in FP16, or fold a per-channel scale into the previous layer. For a ViT or transformer-based detector, check the softmax and GELU outputs first. The decision guide puts this into the INT8 debugging steps.
