Lottery tickets and structure a GPU can use

If a pruned network is as accurate as the dense one, why not train the small network from the start? Frankle and Carbin found that this works, but only if the small network starts from the same initial weights it had inside the big one, and finding those weights takes full training first.

Each round of iterative magnitude pruning (IMP) has three steps. Train the network. Remove the smallest 20% of the weights that are still alive. Then set every survivor back to the value it had before any training, its initial value. Repeat. After round 7 about a fifth of the weights are left; after round 16, under 3%.

The surviving subnetwork with its original starting values is a winning ticket. The paper's hypothesis, verbatim: "A randomly-initialized, dense neural network contains a subnetwork that is initialized such that -- when trained in isolation -- it can match the test accuracy of the original network after training for at most the same number of iterations."

\htmlData{step=1, link=net}{f(x;\,} \htmlData{step=2, link=mask}{\htmlClass{t-green}{m}} \odot \htmlData{step=3, link=init}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{\theta_0}}\htmlData{step=1, link=net}{)}, \qquad \htmlData{step=4, link=pm}{P_m = \frac{\lVert m \rVert_0}{|\theta|}}

Frankle and Carbin, arXiv 1803.03635v5: the masked network of Sec. 1 and the sparsity notation of Sec. 2 (unnumbered in the paper).

  • f(x; \theta): the network applied to input x with weights \theta.
  • m: the mask, one 0 or 1 per weight, found by IMP. \odot multiplies element by element, so masked weights are zero.
  • \theta_0: the initial weights, drawn at random before the dense network was trained. Resetting to \theta_0 is what makes a ticket; the control draws new random values instead.
  • P_m: the share of weights that remain. \lVert m \rVert_0 counts the ones in the mask and |\theta| is the total number of weights. P_m = 25\% means 75% are pruned.

Make a guess before you press "random reinit (control)" at round 7. The mask is the same; only the starting values change. Does the structure alone explain the result? In Frankle's LeNet it does not. At about a fifth of the weights, the ticket reaches its best validation loss 2.51x faster than the same mask started from new random values, and the reinitialized network is already less accurate than the dense one. The pruned structure needs the starting values it grew up with.

The convolutional results are similar. On CIFAR10, Conv-2 and Conv-4 tickets reached early stopping up to 3.5x sooner than the dense network, and Conv-6 up to 2.5x. Conv-4 gained up to 3.5 points of test accuracy, at Pm = 11.1%. The cost is the search: iterative pruning trains the network 15 or more times. So the result explains why pruning works more than it saves training.

Claim card: the lottery ticket hypothesis (Frankle and Carbin, ICLR 2019, arXiv 1803.03635v5)

Claim
Dense, randomly initialized networks contain subnetworks, often less than 10-20% of the original size, that train in isolation from their original initial values to at least the dense test accuracy, in at most as many iterations.
Measured
IMP with reset to the initial weights on LeNet-300-100 (MNIST) and Conv-2, Conv-4 and Conv-6 (CIFAR10). For each mask: the iteration of minimum validation loss and the test accuracy, against the dense network and against the same mask with 15 fresh random initializations.
Falsifier
The same mask with new random values does as well, so the structure alone explains the result; or no sparse mask matches the dense network. The paper hits this itself: for VGG-19 and ResNet-20 (the paper calls it "ResNet-18") on CIFAR10 at learning rate 0.1, IMP finds no winning tickets. It finds them only with learning-rate warmup or a lower learning rate (Sec. 4).
At scale
Frankle et al. (ICML 2020, arXiv 1912.05671v4) pruned ResNet-50 on ImageNet in one shot to 30% of its weights. Reset to the initial weights, it reached 73.7% top-1 against 76.1% dense, only 0.3 points above random reinitialization at 73.4%. Rewinding the survivors to their values at epoch 5, 5.5% into training, instead of to the start, makes the subnetworks stable, and the paper finds that stable and accurate ("matching") subnetworks closely coincide. These are matching subnetworks, not tickets in the original sense.

Structure a GPU can use

Tickets, like Han's pruning, are unstructured: scattered zeros that a dense kernel still multiplies (chapter 8). For speed, remove whole pieces of the computation instead. Then what remains is an ordinary dense network, only thinner, and every kernel and compiler runs it as is.

Filters by L1 norm. Li et al. (ICLR 2017) score each convolution filter by the sum of the absolute values of its weights, s_j = \sum_{l=1}^{n_i} \sum |\mathcal{K}_l| (Sec. 3.1). They remove the filters with the smallest scores, the feature maps those filters produce, and the matching input kernels of the next layer, then retrain. The "Whole channels" button in chapter 8's figure uses this rule. On CIFAR-10, VGG-16 lost 34.2% of its operations and 64.0% of its parameters, with test error 6.75% before and 6.60% after. The paper also measured wall-clock time: inference over the test set went from 1.23 s to 0.73 s on a Titan X (Pascal) with cuDNN v5.1 at batch 128. In the authors' words, "the saved inference time is close to the FLOP reduction".

Channels by batch-norm scale. Network slimming (Liu et al., ICCV 2017) reuses a number every batch-norm layer already has. Batch norm multiplies each normalized channel by a learned scale \gamma, so a channel with a small \gamma contributes little. Slimming adds a penalty that pushes the scales toward zero during training:

L = \sum_{(x, y)} l\big(f(x, W), y\big) + \lambda \sum_{\gamma \in \Gamma} \htmlClass{t-green}{g(\gamma)}, \qquad g(s) = |s|

Eq. 1 in Liu et al. (network slimming), arXiv 1708.06519v1.

  • l(f(x, W), y): the normal training loss on input x with target y and weights W.
  • \Gamma: all batch-norm scales in the network; g(\gamma) = |\gamma| is an L1 penalty, which drives many scales to nearly zero.
  • \lambda: balances the two terms. After training, channels whose \gamma is below one global threshold are removed, and the thinner network is fine-tuned.

On CIFAR-10, a VGG network with 51.0% fewer operations and 2.30M instead of 20.04M parameters had 6.20% test error against 6.34% for the original.

Is it the weights or the shape? Liu et al. (ICLR 2019) took the thinner architectures these methods produce and trained them from random initial weights, with a fair training budget. For structured pruning, from scratch was as good as or better than fine-tuning the kept weights. So the value of structured pruning is the architecture it finds. Their detection row:

The claim has a scope. It holds for structured pruning with the standard learning rate of 0.1. For unstructured pruning of ResNet-50 on ImageNet, fine-tuning wins: at 60% pruned, 76.09% against 74.91% from scratch (Table 6).

A caution about the whole field. Blalock et al. (MLSys 2020) read 81 pruning papers. They used 49 datasets and 132 architectures in 195 combinations; the most common pair, VGG-16 on ImageNet, appears in only 22. Even the starting point differs: reported operation counts for AlexNet on ImageNet range from 371 to 1500 million. Their conclusion is that operation and parameter counts are a loose proxy for latency, and that results are hard to compare. Blalock et al. measure no hardware, so the lesson for a deployment is simply: time your own network on your own GPU.

2:4: zeros in a shape the tensor cores skip

Mishra et al. (NVIDIA) describe a middle ground. In every group of four consecutive weights along the dimension a matrix multiply sums over, at least two must be zero: the 2:4 pattern, exactly 50% sparse. The kept values are packed into half the space, each with a 2-bit index of its position in the group. Because every group has the same shape, the sparse tensor cores of the A100 GPU read memory at full width and pick the matching inputs in hardware. Their peak rate doubles: from 312 to 624 trillion operations per second for FP16, and from 624 to 1248 for INT8 (Table 1). The paper prunes only layers whose summed dimension is a multiple of 16 for FP16 and 32 for INT8, and TensorRT from version 8.0 runs 2:4 weights automatically.

The paper measures speed only for one INT8 matrix multiply on the A100, with both outer dimensions 10240: larger multiplies get "nearly a 2x speedup" (Fig. 3). It reports no speedup for a whole network. So the honest summary is up to 2x faster math on the layers that are bound by arithmetic, and the end-to-end gain of a detector is something to measure.

The recipe is short (Sec. 4.1). Train the network dense. Prune each group of four to its two largest weights. Retrain with the same optimizer, learning-rate schedule and number of epochs, with the mask held fixed. For detection and segmentation (Sec. 4.3), train the backbone on ImageNet and the detector on COCO as usual, then prune backbone and heads together and repeat only the COCO fine-tuning: "the task-specific heads have to be fine-tuned before they can be pruned". INT8 calibration comes after the sparse retraining (Sec. 4.4).

Every row stays within about a point of dense FP16, and the paper attributes the differences to run-to-run variation. Networks built for efficiency are harder. With the plain recipe, MobileNet v2 fell from 71.55% to 69.56% ImageNet top-1, and EfficientNet B0 from 77.25% to 75.98%. Reordering the input channels before pruning, so that large weights do not share a group, recovered both: 71.56% and 77.29% (Table 3).

On a detector

Of the methods in this act, two give a GPU detector speed without a custom kernel: a thinner backbone, from filter or channel pruning or simply trained from scratch at that width, and 2:4 sparsity on an A100-class or newer GPU. For 2:4, prune after the COCO fine-tune and repeat only that phase, calibrate INT8 afterwards, and expect accuracy within about a point on ResNet-style detectors. Plan on channel permutation for EfficientNet-style backbones such as EfficientDet's (our reading of Table 3). Then measure the whole engine: the paper only promises up to twice the math, and memory-bound layers gain less (chapter 1). The decision guide puts this after INT8.
