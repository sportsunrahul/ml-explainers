Training through the rounding: QAT and the straight-through estimator

Quantization-aware training (QAT) simulates the rounding in the forward pass, so the network can learn weights that survive it. The obstacle is the backward pass: rounding is a staircase, and a staircase has zero slope almost everywhere.

The top panel is the forward pass. The network keeps a full-precision copy of each weight, the shadow weight w (blue). Every forward pass rounds it onto the grid, \hat w (yellow), and uses only the rounded value. The bottom panel is what the backward pass needs: the slope of the staircase. Its true value is zero everywhere except at the jumps, where it is infinite. Press "true", then "1 step" a few times: the loss stays where it is, and the weight never moves.

The straight-through estimator (STE) ignores the staircase in the backward pass and treats rounding as the identity. Inside the grid's range, the gradient passes through unchanged; outside it, where the quantizer clips, it is zero. Now the weight moves, and the rounded value climbs the staircase toward the target.

\htmlClass{t-yellow}{\hat{v}} = \htmlData{step=3, link=round}{\Big\lfloor} \htmlData{step=2, link=clip}{\mathrm{clip}\big(} \htmlData{step=1, link=shadow}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{v} / s}\htmlData{step=2, link=clip}{,\, -Q_N,\, Q_P\big)} \htmlData{step=3, link=round}{\Big\rceil} \htmlData{step=4, link=grid}{\times s}

Eq. 1-2 in Esser et al. (LSQ), arXiv 1902.08153v3, written as one line (our notation). Nagel et al. Eq. 7 (arXiv 2106.08295v1) writes the same operation with a zero-point; Wu et al. Eq. 12 (arXiv 2004.09602v1) writes it as dequantize(quantize(x)).

  • v: the full-precision value, here the shadow weight; \hat v: the value the forward pass uses.
  • s: the step size, the distance between grid levels.
  • Q_N, Q_P: the number of levels below and above zero. For b-bit signed weights, Q_N = 2^{b-1} and Q_P = 2^{b-1} - 1.
  • \lfloor \cdot \rceil: round to the nearest integer. Multiplying back by s returns a float that lies on the grid: this is fake quantization, integer values carried in a float tensor.
\frac{\partial \hat{v}}{\partial v} = \htmlData{step=1, link=ste}{\begin{cases} \htmlClass{t-red}{1} & \text{if } -Q_N < v/s < Q_P \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}} \qquad \text{instead of} \qquad \htmlData{step=2, link=true}{\frac{\partial \lfloor y \rceil}{\partial y} = 0 \text{ almost everywhere}}

Eq. 5 in Esser et al., arXiv 1902.08153v3. The same clipped rule is Eq. 14-15 in Krishnamoorthi, arXiv 1806.08342v1, and Eq. 13 in Wu et al., arXiv 2004.09602v1. Bengio et al., arXiv 1308.3432v1 (Sec. 4, Eq. 13), named the straight-through estimator and define its plain form, identity with no clipping, crediting Hinton's lecture 15b.

Make a guess before you press "5 steps" with the straight-through gradient. The target lies above the top level of the grid. Where will the weight stop? It climbs to the top level and stops there, short of the target. Once the shadow weight passes the top of the range, the clipped STE passes no gradient, so nothing pulls the weight back and nothing moves the range.

The fix is to learn the range too. Learned step size quantization (LSQ, Esser et al.) makes the step s a trainable parameter and differentiates the quantizer with respect to it, again with the STE for the rounding:

\htmlData{step=1, link=step}{\frac{\partial \hat{v}}{\partial s}} = \htmlData{step=2, link=round}{\begin{cases} -v/s + \lfloor v/s \rceil & \text{if } -Q_N < v/s < Q_P \\ -Q_N & \text{if } v/s \le -Q_N \\ Q_P & \text{if } v/s \ge Q_P \end{cases}}

Eq. 3 in Esser et al., arXiv 1902.08153v3 (\sfrac written as a slash). Nagel et al. Eq. 38 is the same gradient in their notation.

Inside the range, the step's gradient is the distance from v/s to its rounded value, so it is largest near a jump. Outside the range it is the full level count, so a clipped value pushes the range outward. LSQ multiplies this gradient by g = 1/\sqrt{N Q_P}, where N is the number of weights in the layer, so the step changes about as fast as the weights do. Press "straight-through + learned step": the step grows until the top level reaches the target, and the loss goes to nearly zero.

Three papers set the range in three ways. Jacob et al. (arXiv 1712.05877v1) do not learn it: weight ranges are the minimum and maximum of the weights, activation ranges are a moving average of the values seen in training, and "backpropagation still happens as usual". The paper does not name the STE. It also turns activation quantization off at the start of training, for 50 thousand to 2 million steps (500 thousand in all its training protocols). PACT (Choi et al., arXiv 1805.06085v2) learns a clipping value for each layer's activations; its gradient comes only from the inputs that are clipped. LSQ learns the step for weights and activations. On the first and last layers they differ too: LSQ always keeps them at 8 bits, PACT leaves them unquantized in its accuracy runs (but assumes 8 bits in its hardware model), and Jacob et al. do not discuss them.

Inside one QAT step on a detector: forward, then backward

The same machinery runs at the scale of a detector. Every convolution gets a fake quantizer on its weights and on its output activations; the rest is ordinary fine-tuning.

Make a guess before you press "6 Gradients": does the gradient stop at the fake quantizers, or reach the weights in the first convolution? It reaches every shadow weight, because the STE lets it through each quantizer. Only values outside the clipping range block it.

What QAT buys on detectors

For 8-bit CNN detectors, very little: post-training quantization is already close. Nagel et al. ran both pipelines on the same models (absolute points, mean of 5 PTQ runs and 3 QAT runs):

QAT matters at 4 bits. With 4-bit weights and activations, the EfficientDet-D1 detector still ends at 28.68 mAP after QAT, against 40.08 in FP32, so low-bit activations remain hard for this kind of network. For these networks QAT does not help: Faster R-CNN and Mask R-CNN end slightly below their PTQ scores, and Wu et al. put the Mask R-CNN gap at the noise level (their text does not discuss Faster R-CNN). The classification rows of the same table tell the other side. EfficientNet b0, full of depthwise convolutions, goes from -6.23% after PTQ to 0.13% after QAT.

On a detector

For an 8-bit ResNet-FPN detector, try PTQ first: in Wu's measurements QAT added nothing. Reach for QAT when PTQ leaves a gap you cannot accept, typically with depthwise-separable backbones or 4-bit weights. Then start from the best calibrated model, fine-tune for about a tenth of the original schedule at a small learning rate, and export the folded graph that matches the inference engine. The decision guide places this step after partial quantization.
