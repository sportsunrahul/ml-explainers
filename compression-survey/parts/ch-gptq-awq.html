Four-bit weights: GPTQ and AWQ

When a language model writes text one token at a time, each layer reads all of its weights to do a little arithmetic on one token, so it waits on memory (the "LLM layer, 1 token" dot in chapter 1). Storing the weights in 4 bits cuts those bytes by four, and the two methods in this chapter make 4-bit weights accurate without any retraining.

GPTQ: round one column, repair the rest

Round to nearest treats every weight alone. GPTQ (Frantar et al.) asks what the layer's output needs, using real inputs from a small calibration set:

\htmlData{step=3, link=loss}{\text{argmin}_{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{\mathbf{\widehat{W}}}} \, ||}\htmlData{step=1, link=w}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{\mathbf{W} \mathbf{X}}} - \htmlData{step=2, link=q}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{\mathbf{\widehat{W}} \mathbf{X}}}\htmlData{step=3, link=loss}{||_2^2.}

Eq. 1 in Frantar et al. (GPTQ), arXiv 2210.17323v2.

  • \mathbf{W}: the layer's weights, one row per output; blue bars in the figure.
  • \mathbf{X}: the layer's inputs for the calibration samples, one column per sample.
  • \mathbf{\widehat{W}}: the quantized weights, restricted to the grid; yellow bars.
  • The squared norm is the total output error, the red line in the chart.

The algorithm walks the columns from left to right. It rounds one column onto the grid, measures the rounding error, and shifts every column not yet rounded so that the layer's output moves back toward where it was. Press the column buttons and watch the blue bars to the right of the frame move away from their outlines: that is the repair. The red bars above the grid say how much of the error each later column takes. They come from H, which records how the input channels move together; when two channels are correlated, one can stand in for the other. This is the same input Hessian that AdaRound uses to choose up or down in chapter 6.

The shift is the update of Optimal Brain Surgeon, applied to quantization by the earlier OBQ method (Frantar et al., arXiv 2208.11580v2), which GPTQ builds on:

\htmlData{step=3, link=err}{\htmlClass{t-red}{\boldsymbol{\delta}_F}} = - \frac{\htmlData{step=1, link=q}{\htmlClass{t-red}{w_q - \text{quant}(w_q)}}}{[\mathbf{H}_F^{-1}]_{qq}} \cdot \htmlData{step=2, link=u}{(\mathbf{H}_F^{-1})_{:, q}}

Eq. 2 in Frantar et al., arXiv 2210.17323v2 (the update half; the paper's Eq. 2 also picks which weight goes next, and GPTQ replaces that choice with a fixed left-to-right order).

  • w_q - \text{quant}(w_q): the rounding error of the weight just quantized.
  • \mathbf{H}_F = 2\mathbf{X}_F\mathbf{X}_F^\top: the Hessian of the output error over the weights F that are still in full precision. GPTQ adds a small damping term, 1% of its mean diagonal.
  • (\mathbf{H}_F^{-1})_{:, q}: how strongly each remaining weight should move to cancel that error.
  • \boldsymbol{\delta}_F: the change applied to all the remaining weights in that row.

Two observations make this cheap. Every row uses the same column order, so all rows share one sequence of inverse Hessians. And that whole sequence is one Cholesky factorization of H-1 (Sec. 4, Step 3). After line 3 of Algorithm 1, the symbol H-1 means that upper Cholesky factor U, not the inverse itself:

\begin{aligned} &\htmlData{step=1, link=q}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{\mathbf{Q}_{:, j} \gets \text{quant}(\mathbf{W}_{:, j})}} \\ &\htmlData{step=2, link=err}{\htmlClass{t-red}{\mathbf{E}_{:, j - i}} \gets (\mathbf{W}_{:, j} - \mathbf{Q}_{:, j}) \, / \, [\mathbf{H}^{-1}]_{jj}} \\ &\htmlData{step=3, link=u}{\mathbf{W}_{:, j:(i + B)} \gets \mathbf{W}_{:, j:(i + B)} - \mathbf{E}_{:, j - i} \cdot \mathbf{H}^{-1}_{j, j:(i + B)}} \end{aligned}

Algorithm 1, lines 6 to 8, in Frantar et al., arXiv 2210.17323v2. The paper uses blocks of B = 128 columns and applies the update to later blocks in one batch.

Everything here is forward passes and linear algebra: no gradient, no training. GPTQ feeds 128 random segments of 2048 tokens from C4 (generic web text) through the model one block at a time, and quantizes each layer from its own inputs. In the toy, GPTQ ends about 5 times below round to nearest on the same 4-bit grid. Reading H-1 in Algorithm 1 as the plain inverse instead of the Cholesky factor gives a different, wrong update.

At full scale, GPTQ quantizes OPT-175B in 4.2h and BLOOM-176B in 3.8h on a single A100 with 80GB. On OPT-175B, WikiText2 perplexity goes from 8.34 in FP16 to 8.37 with 4-bit GPTQ, where 4-bit round to nearest gives 10.54. At 3 bits, round to nearest collapses to 7.3e3 and GPTQ holds 8.68. The algorithm also works on vision models: ResNet-50 with 4-bit weights keeps 75.71% ImageNet top-1 against 76.13% in full precision (Table 1).

The speed numbers need their conditions. The 3-bit OPT-175B model takes 63GB and fits on one 80GB A100; the paper says a 4-bit version would not fully fit. Generating one token at a time (batch size 1), it takes 71 ms per token on that one GPU, against 230 ms for FP16 spread over 5x80GB GPUs: 3.24x. The custom kernel multiplies a quantized matrix by one FP16 vector, so the gain comes from moving fewer bytes, and the paper lists speedups in batched execution as outside its study.

AWQ: protect the weights that see large inputs

AWQ (Lin et al.) starts from an experiment. Keep a small share of a layer's input channels in FP16 and round the rest to 3 bits. Which channels should you keep? Choosing them by the size of their activations recovers almost everything; choosing them by the size of their weights does about as well as choosing at random:

Mixed precision is awkward for hardware, so AWQ protects those channels another way. Multiply input channel j of W by a factor s greater than 1 before rounding, and divide the matching input by s (that division folds into the previous layer). The rounding error of a weight is at most half a step either way, so after dividing by s it shrinks by about s, as long as the scaled weight does not raise the group's maximum (AWQ Eq. 3 gives the error ratio as (\Delta'/\Delta)(1/s)). AWQ picks one scale per input channel from the activation sizes, with a single exponent found by search:

\begin{aligned} &\htmlData{step=3, link=loss}{\mathbf{s}^* = \mathop{\arg\min}_{\mathbf{s}} \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{s}),} \\ &\htmlData{step=3, link=loss}{\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{s})} = \lVert \htmlData{step=1, link=ch}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{Q(\mathbf{W}\cdot \text{diag}(\mathbf{s}))}} \htmlData{step=2, link=sx}{(\mathbf{\text{diag}(s)^{-1}} \cdot \mathbf{X})} - \htmlClass{t-blue}{\mathbf{W}\mathbf{X}} \rVert \end{aligned}

Eq. 4 in Lin et al. (AWQ), arXiv 2306.00978v6 (the source splits "diag" across an alignment mark; joined here).

\mathbf{s}=\htmlData{step=1, link=sx}{\htmlClass{t-green}{\mathbf{s_X}}}^{\htmlData{step=2, link=alpha}{\alpha}}, \quad \htmlData{step=3, link=loss}{\alpha^*=\mathop{\arg\min}_{\alpha}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{s_X}^{\alpha})}

Eq. 5 in Lin et al., arXiv 2306.00978v6.

  • \mathbf{s_X}: the average magnitude of each input channel's activation over calibration data, the top bars. SmoothQuant used the maximum; AWQ uses the mean.
  • Q: the weight quantizer, INT3 or INT4 with a group size of 128 in the paper.
  • \alpha: one exponent for the whole layer. At 0 nothing is scaled (round to nearest); at 1 the scale equals the activation size.
  • \mathcal{L}: the output error; the paper prints no norm subscript, and the toy uses the Frobenius norm. The paper searches 20 values of alpha on [0, 1].

Drag alpha up slowly. The two tall red bars, the error terms of the large-activation channels, shrink fast. Past a point, the scaled weights start to set the maximum of their group, the step grows for every other weight in it, and the small red bars rise. Make a guess before you press "Grid search over alpha": will the best alpha be 0, 1, or in between? In the toy the curve is a U, with its bottom near 0.42 and the error about halved. AWQ's Table 2 shows the same U on OPT-6.7B when it scales the salient 1% by a fixed s: perplexity 23.54 at s = 1, 11.92 at s = 2, and back up to 12.36 at s = 4.

Unlike GPTQ, AWQ does not fit the weights to the calibration data; it only measures activation sizes. The paper argues this makes it less sensitive to the calibration text. On OPT-6.7B at INT3, calibrating on PubMed and testing on Enron emails costs GPTQ +4.89 perplexity and AWQ +0.50 (Fig. 8). With 4-bit weights in groups of 128, Llama-2-7B goes from 5.47 perplexity in FP16 to 5.60 with AWQ, 5.69 with GPTQ and 5.73 with round to nearest (Table 4). With its own inference engine, TinyChat, at batch size 1, Llama-2-7B generates 194 tokens per second on an RTX 4090 with 4-bit weights, against 52 for Hugging Face in FP16 (Fig. 9).

The same tools, used to prune

SparseGPT (Frantar and Alistarh, arXiv 2301.00774v3) runs GPTQ's column-by-column update with zero as the target instead of a grid point, and prunes OPT-175B to half its weights with WikiText2 perplexity 8.21 against 8.35 dense, or 8.74 in the GPU-friendly 2:4 pattern of chapter 9. Wanda (Sun et al., arXiv 2306.11695v3) brings the same signal to pruning (our framing; the paper builds on LLM.int8()'s outlier features, not on AWQ): score each weight by its magnitude times the size of its input channel, with no weight update at all; LLaMA-7B at half sparsity reaches 7.26 perplexity, against 17.29 for plain magnitude pruning (dense 5.68).

On a detector

Every speedup in this chapter is for generating one token at a time, which is memory-bound. A detector backbone at normal resolution spends its time on arithmetic (chapter 1), so 4-bit weights with FP16 arithmetic save memory there, not much time (our reading). The algorithms themselves transfer: GPTQ's Table 1 and OBC's ImageNet rows are vision models, and OBC prunes YOLOv5l on COCO from 66.97 mAP@0.5 dense to 64.05 at a 4x cut in operations, with the same layer-wise Hessian (Table 1); its YOLO quantization results exist only as figure curves. Where 4-bit weights do pay off directly is a vision-language model on an edge GPU, like VILA on Orin. The decision guide keeps detectors on INT8 or FP8 for both weights and activations.
