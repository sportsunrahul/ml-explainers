Toy model

Compressing vision models for GPU inference

A trained detector is a pile of 32-bit numbers and a chain of GPU kernels. Make the model smaller, the numbers shorter, the pile sparser and the chain shorter, and the same network runs faster on the same GPU. This page builds each of those four levers from first principles, shows what each one costs in accuracy, and ends with which one to pull first.

Act IWhat costs time

Where the time goes

A GPU spends its time on two jobs: doing arithmetic, and moving numbers between its memory and its compute units. A layer finishes when the slower of the two jobs finishes, so before compressing anything, ask which job a layer is waiting on.

Read the chart from left to right. The horizontal position of a layer is its arithmetic intensity: how many operations it does for every byte it moves. A layer on the left does little work per byte, so it waits on memory and sits under the slanted line. A layer on the right does a lot of work per byte, so it waits on arithmetic and sits under the flat line. The corner where the two lines meet is the GPU's balance point.

\htmlData{step=1, link=point}{t_{\text{layer}}} = \max\Big(\htmlData{step=2, link=roof}{\frac{\text{operations}}{P_{\text{math}}}},\; \htmlData{step=3, link=mem}{\frac{\text{bytes moved}}{B_{\text{memory}}}}\Big)

The roofline model, our notation. The chart plots operations divided by this time.

  • t_{\text{layer}}: how long one layer takes.
  • P_{\text{math}}: the GPU's peak rate of arithmetic for the chosen number format. Tensor cores raise it a lot for FP16 and again for INT8.
  • B_{\text{memory}}: the memory bandwidth in bytes per second. The number format does not change it, but a shorter format means fewer bytes to move.

Now press the format buttons with the big 3x3 conv selected. From FP32 to FP16 it gets much faster, because it was waiting on arithmetic and tensor cores raise the math limit. Then select the depthwise conv and do the same: it gets only twice as fast, because it was waiting on memory and FP16 only halves the bytes.

Make a guess before you press "4-bit weights". This format stores weights in 4 bits but still does FP16 arithmetic. Which layers will it speed up? The answer is the ones whose bytes are mostly weights: the head conv on a small 5x5 map, and a language-model layer that processes one token at a time. The big 3x3 conv does not get faster at all. Its bytes are mostly activations, and its time is spent on arithmetic. That single fact explains why the large-language-model methods in Act V store weights in 4 bits, while vision models are usually quantized to 8 bits throughout.

Four levers

Everything on this page pulls one of four levers, and each lever acts on a different term of the equation:

  • A smaller model (distillation): fewer operations and fewer bytes, because the network itself is smaller. A small student network learns from a large teacher.
  • Fewer bits per number (quantization, Act III and FP8): fewer bytes, and on tensor cores a higher math limit.
  • Fewer numbers (pruning, Act IV): fewer operations and bytes, but only when the zeros come in a shape the hardware can skip.
  • Better kernels (the compiler, chapter 13): fusing layers removes trips to memory, and choosing the fastest kernel for each layer moves every dot closer to its roof.

On a detector

In the toy, the 3x3 convolutions on large feature maps are bound by arithmetic, so FP16 and then INT8 tensor cores speed them up most. The 1x1 and depthwise convolutions are bound by memory, and their bytes are mostly activations, so they speed up only when their activations get shorter too, not only their weights. (The head conv on a 5x5 map is the exception: its bytes are mostly weights.) This is why detectors are normally quantized with 8-bit weights and 8-bit activations. Chapters 4 to 7 build that from first principles.

The field at a glance

Model compression is older than the deep-learning boom, and the large language model (LLM) wave made it popular again. A count of arXiv titles shows where the activity went, and a family tree shows where each method on this page came from.

Make a guess first: in 2022, how many quantization titles named a language model? The answer is 5 of 217. By 2025 it was 160 of 600. Titles naming detection or segmentation stayed small: 21 quantization titles and 26 pruning titles in 2025.

For a detector, that is good news. The methods that matter most for a convolutional detector (per-channel INT8, calibration, short quantization-aware fine-tuning, 2:4 sparsity) were published between 2017 and 2021 and have been tested at length since. The newer LLM methods solve problems that detectors mostly do not have, such as single-token decoding that waits on weight memory. Chapters 10 and 11 explain when they still apply.

Act IIA smaller student

Knowledge distillation

A large trained network (the teacher) knows more than its top answer: it also knows which wrong answers are nearly right. Knowledge distillation trains a smaller network (the student) to copy the teacher's whole output distribution, made softer by a temperature, instead of only the correct label.

Start at T = 1 and look at the blue bars. The teacher is sure this image is a truck, and the other classes look like zero. They are not zero. In our toy, the teacher gives "bus" 0.047 and "cat" less than 0.001. That ratio is knowledge: a truck can look a bit like a bus, and not at all like a cat. Hinton, Vinyals and Dean give the same kind of example: "An image of a BMW, for example, may only have a very small chance of being mistaken for a garbage truck, but that mistake is still many times more probable than mistaking it for a carrot." The truck, bus and cat are our example; the BMW, garbage truck and carrot are theirs.

At T = 1 those small numbers barely affect a loss, because they are so close to zero. Now drag the slider to the right, or press "T = 3". Every bar moves toward the average, and the wrong classes become visible. The teacher now says bus 0.21 and cat 0.014, while our student says 0.096 for both. The red arrows show where the soft loss pushes the student: bus up, cat down. The student is right about "truck" but wrong about which mistakes are reasonable, and only a soft target can tell it so.

\htmlData{step=1, link=student}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{q_i}} = \frac{\exp(\htmlData{step=2, link=logits}{z_i} / \htmlData{step=3, link=temp}{T})}{\htmlData{step=4, link=teacher student}{\sum_j \exp(z_j/T)}}

Eq. 1 in Hinton et al., arXiv 1503.02531v1 (the source writes "exp" without the upright font).

  • z_i: the logit for class i, the raw score the network computes before the softmax. Hinton writes z for the student and v for the teacher.
  • T: the temperature. Dividing every logit by T shrinks the gaps between them, so a larger T gives a softer, more even distribution. The paper says T "is normally set to 1". The rows under the bars show the logits after this division.
  • q_i: the probability for class i. The sum over all classes j makes the probabilities add up to one.

Two losses, one weight

The paper trains the student on a weighted average of two objectives, and gives them in words, not as an equation. The first is the cross-entropy with the teacher's soft targets, computed at the same high temperature in both networks. The second is the ordinary cross-entropy with the correct label, computed from the same student logits at T = 1. The authors "found that the best results were generally obtained by using a condiderably [sic] lower weight on the second objective function". Later papers write the same recipe as one line:

\mathcal{L} = \htmlData{step=1, link=hard}{(1-\lambda)\,\htmlClass{t-red}{\mathrm{CE}}\big(y,\ \htmlClass{t-yellow}{q(z;1)}\big)} \;+\; \htmlData{step=2, link=tsq}{\lambda\,T^2}\, \htmlData{step=3, link=kl}{\htmlClass{t-red}{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\htmlClass{t-blue}{p(v;T)}\,\|\,\htmlClass{t-yellow}{q(z;T)}\big)}

Our transcription of Hinton et al. Sec. 2 (prose only), in the form of DeiT Eq. 2 (Touvron et al., arXiv 2012.12877v2). DeiT prints the KL arguments student first; the teacher-first direction here is our reading. Hinton writes a cross-entropy, which has the same gradient for the student (our reading).

  • p(v;T): the teacher's softmax at temperature T (blue bars); q(z;T): the student's (yellow bars); q(z;1): the student's ordinary output at T = 1.
  • \mathrm{CE}(y, \cdot): the hard-label loss, cross-entropy with the one-hot label y.
  • \mathrm{KL}(p \,\|\, q): the Kullback-Leibler divergence, how many extra nats it costs to describe the teacher's distribution with the student's. It is the cross-entropy minus the teacher's own entropy, which the student cannot change. The figure prints it at the top.
  • \lambda: the weight. The conventions differ between papers. Hinton's speech run used 0.5 as the relative weight on the hard term; DeiT puts \lambda = 0.1 on the soft term with \tau = 3.0, the opposite emphasis.
  • T^2: the factor the next section explains.

Why multiply by T2

Turning up the temperature has a side effect on training: it shrinks the soft loss's gradient. Hinton derives the gradient of the soft cross-entropy C with respect to one student logit:

\htmlData{step=1, link=plain}{\htmlClass{t-red}{\frac{\partial C}{\partial z_i}}} = \htmlData{step=2, link=temp}{\frac{1}{T}}\left(\htmlData{step=3, link=gap}{q_i - p_i}\right) = \frac{1}{T}\left(\htmlData{step=4, link=student}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{\frac{e^{z_i/T}}{\sum_j e^{z_j/T}}}} -\htmlData{step=5, link=teacher}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{\frac{e^{v_i/T}}{\sum_j e^{v_j/T}}}}\right)

Eq. 2 in Hinton et al., arXiv 1503.02531v1.

  • q_i - p_i: the gap between the student's and the teacher's probability for class i, the red arrows in the figure.
  • 1/T: a factor that comes from dividing the logits by T inside the softmax.
  • v_i: the teacher's logit. The teacher's logits are given inputs here; only the student's logits z_i are differentiated.

There are two shrinking effects, not one. The factor 1/T is visible. The gap q_i - p_i also shrinks, because at high temperature both distributions move toward uniform. When the temperature is large compared with the logits, Hinton expands e^x \approx 1 + x:

\htmlData{step=1, link=limit}{\frac{\partial C}{\partial z_i} \approx} \frac{1}{T}\left( \htmlData{step=2, link=student}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{\frac{1+z_i/T}{N + \sum_j z_j/T}}} - \htmlData{step=3, link=teacher}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{\frac{1+v_i/T}{N + \sum_j v_j/T}}} \right)

Eq. 3 in Hinton et al., arXiv 1503.02531v1. N is the number of classes.

If each logit vector has zero mean, the sums vanish and the result is short:

\htmlData{step=1, link=limit}{\frac{\partial C}{\partial z_i} \approx} \htmlData{step=2, link=plain}{\frac{1}{NT^2}}\htmlData{step=3, link=logits}{\left( z_i - v_i \right)}

Eq. 4 in Hinton et al., arXiv 1503.02531v1.

  • 1/(NT^2): the gradient falls as the square of the temperature. This is the dotted line in the figure.
  • z_i - v_i: at high temperature the student simply moves its logits toward the teacher's. Minimizing \tfrac{1}{2}(z_i - v_i)^2, the logit matching of earlier model-compression work, is therefore the high-temperature limit of distillation.

Make a guess before you press "with T2". Without the factor, the soft-loss gradient at T = 20 is about 55 times smaller than the hard-label gradient in our toy, so the hard label would drive training alone. What will the curve look like after you multiply by T^2? It stops falling. From about T = 6 on it stays near the Eq. 4 value, so the ratio between the two loss terms no longer depends on the temperature you pick. The paper's words: "Since the magnitudes of the gradients produced by the soft targets scale as 1/T^2 it is important to multiply them by T^2 when using both hard and soft targets." Note that "roughly unchanged" does not mean "equal": in our toy the soft term ends up about 7 times larger than the hard term, and the weight \lambda sets the final balance. At low temperature the factor does not hold the gradient constant, because the softmax is saturated there and the approximation does not apply.

Which temperature is right is a tuning choice. Hinton's small MNIST student used T = 20. For students with at least 300 units per layer, all temperatures above 8 gave fairly similar results; a 30-unit student did best at 2.5 to 4. The paper reads this as a sign that a very small student does better when it ignores the teacher's most negative logits, which "could be very noisy".

Inside distillation: forward, then backward

One training step runs the same image through two networks. The figure below steps through it with Hinton's MNIST pair. Make a guess before you press "6 Gradients": which boxes will the gradient reach?

The answer to the guess: the student only. The teacher sits upstream of the soft loss, but the gradient never enters its branch, because nothing there will be updated. A deep-learning framework skips that branch only if you tell it to, by freezing the teacher or computing its output without gradient tracking.

What distillation bought in the paper

On MNIST, the large teacher made 67 test errors. The small net trained on hard labels alone made 146. The same small net trained to match the teacher's soft targets at T = 20 made 74. These are counts from the prose of Sec. 3; the paper has no table for them.

The speech experiment is a different kind of compression. The teacher was an ensemble of 10 acoustic models, and the student was one model with the same architecture as each of them. So the gain is ten forward passes down to one, not a smaller network. "More than 80% of the improvement in frame classification accuracy" of the ensemble carried over:

Distilling a detector or a vision transformer

Hinton's method was built and tested on classifiers. Wang et al. give three reasons it transfers poorly to detection. "A detection model may only involve a few classes", so there is little inter-class similarity to distill. Detection also needs localization, which a softmax over classes does not describe. And background locations far outnumber objects. On their small one-stage detector, adding only Hinton's output loss to the quarter-width student raised mAP by just 0.9 points.

The alternative is to match features inside the network. FitNets (Romero et al.) does this with a "hint": a small regressor maps a middle layer of the student onto a middle layer of the teacher, and an L2 loss pulls them together before ordinary distillation. In their CIFAR-10 run the 11th student layer imitates the teacher's second layer, and a thin, deep FitNet with about 2.5M parameters beats its 9M-parameter teacher. On a detector, imitating the whole feature map hurts: it dropped the quarter-width toy detector by 8.9 mAP, because most of the map is noisy background.

Wang et al. imitate features only near objects. For each ground-truth box they compute its overlap (IoU) with every anchor on the last feature map that carries anchors, keep the locations whose IoU exceeds \psi times that box's best IoU, with \psi = 0.5, and add an L2 loss between an adapted student feature and the teacher feature at those locations only. The loss weight is not stated in the paper; the official code uses 0.01 (code-only). For an FPN detector they build the mask per pyramid level. DeiT (Touvron et al.) takes a third route for vision transformers: a learned distillation token sits beside the class token, and its output head is trained on the teacher's predicted class while the class head learns the true label.

Two cautions about these rows. The DeiT student is not smaller: DeiT-B has 86M parameters and its teacher 84M. The Wang students, by contrast, are the teacher's own design with halved channels, which is the usual case for a deployed detector. Distillation also stacks with the other levers: a distilled student can still be quantized (chapter 4 onward) or pruned (chapter 8). Seen through the cost model, it is the only lever that cuts operations and bytes in every layer at once, but it is also the only one that needs a full training run.

On a detector

Do not expect logit distillation alone to help much: on Wang's toy detector it added under a point. Imitate the teacher's features near the objects, on each level of the feature pyramid, with a teacher of the same family (in their COCO run, a half-width Faster R-CNN R50-FPN gained 3.6 AP). It costs a training run with the teacher's forward pass on every image, so the decision guide places it after cheaper post-training quantization.

Act IIIFewer bits

Numbers on a grid: scale and zero-point

To store a tensor in 8 bits, lay a ruler with 256 evenly spaced marks over the range of its values and replace each value by the number of its nearest mark. Everything in this act is a choice about that ruler: where its ends go, how far apart its marks are, and which mark stands for zero.

The blue histogram is the data: real numbers that a layer produced. The yellow ticks are the marks of the ruler. Below the axis, each yellow block shows how many values landed on that mark. At 8 bits the yellow shape copies the blue one closely. At 2 bits there are only four marks, and the shape collapses into four blocks.

Two different errors appear. A value inside the range moves to its nearest mark, so it is off by at most half a mark spacing: that is rounding error. A value outside the range is pushed to the end mark, however far away it was: that is clipping error. The two red bars measure each one as a mean squared error over the sample.

Make a guess before you press "wide". Press "tight" first and look at the two bars. A wider range clips nothing, but its marks are farther apart. Which bar grows and which shrinks? Is wider always better? Then try the same three ranges at 2 bits.

At 8 bits the clipping bar dominates the tight range, so covering every value wins: min to max gives the smallest total. At 2 bits the order flips. The marks are so coarse that rounding error swamps everything, and the tight range has the smaller total (0.12 against 0.44 in this toy). The best range depends on the bit-width. Chapter 5 turns that trade into a rule for choosing it.

The ruler as an equation

Jacob and colleagues at Google wrote the ruler as one affine map from an integer code back to a real number. The scale says how far apart the marks are, and the zero-point says which code means 0.0.

\htmlData{step=1, link=real}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{r}} = \htmlData{step=2, link=scale}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{S}}\,(\htmlData{step=3, link=code}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{q}} - \htmlData{step=4, link=zero}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{Z}})

Eq. 1 in Jacob et al., arXiv 1712.05877v1 ("our quantization scheme").

  • r: the real value that the code stands for (blue histogram).
  • S: the scale, the real distance between two neighboring marks. It is a positive real number.
  • q: the integer code. Jacob uses unsigned 8-bit codes, 0 to 255. The readout shows one code as its bit pattern.
  • Z: the zero-point, the code that represents real 0.0 exactly. It has the same integer type as q.

Why insist that 0.0 is exact? Convolutions pad the border of a feature map with zeros, and ReLU produces many exact zeros. If 0.0 fell between two marks, every padded pixel would carry a small error. So the range is nudged until 0.0 lands exactly on a mark. In the figure the yellow triangle marks that code.

Going the other way, from a real value to a code, means dividing by the scale, rounding, shifting by the zero-point and clamping to the codes that exist. Krishnamoorthi's 2018 whitepaper writes it in two lines, with \Delta for the scale and z for the zero-point:

\htmlData{step=1, link=round}{x_{int}= round\big(\frac{\htmlClass{t-blue}{x}}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{\Delta}}\big)} \htmlData{step=2, link=zero}{+\,\htmlClass{t-yellow}{z}} \qquad \htmlData{step=3, link=clip}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{x_Q}=clamp(0,N_{levels} - 1, x_{int})}

Eq. 1-2 in Krishnamoorthi, arXiv 1806.08342v1.

  • round(x/\Delta): snap the value to the nearest mark. The difference is the rounding error, at most \Delta/2.
  • +\,z: shift so that real 0.0 maps to code z.
  • clamp(0, N_{levels}-1, \cdot): codes outside the ruler are forced to its ends. This is where clipping error comes from. N_{levels} is 256 for 8 bits.

Jacob's own quantizer, Eq. 12, says the same thing in terms of a range [a; b] and a number of levels n, and returns a real number on the grid. We quote it as printed; the x inside the clamp is the source's typo for r:

\operatorname{clamp}(r;a,b) \coloneqq \min\left(\max(x, a), b\right), \quad s(a,b,n) \coloneqq \frac{b - a}{n - 1}, \quad q(r;a,b,n) \coloneqq \left\lfloor \frac{\operatorname{clamp}(r;a,b) - a}{s(a,b,n)} \right\rceil s(a,b,n) + a

Eq. 12 in Jacob et al., arXiv 1712.05877v1, as printed. Eq. 13 maps it to S = s(a, b, n) and Z = z(a, b, n).

Symmetric or asymmetric

Press "symmetric". A symmetric grid fixes Z = 0 and uses signed codes, so its range is always [−m, m]. That is simpler arithmetic, but this data is mostly positive: the codes on the far left stand for values that never occur. The grid spends them anyway, so its marks are farther apart and the rounding bar grows. An asymmetric grid fits [min, max] instead and uses more of its codes on real data.

The zero-point is not free, though. The next section shows the extra integer work it causes, and why GPU toolchains avoid it for weights.

Integer-only arithmetic

Quantization saves memory only if the arithmetic also stays in integers. Jacob shows that a whole convolution can: multiply the codes as integers, correct for the zero-points with a few sums, and rescale once at the end. Step through one output value of a 3x3 convolution.

Substitute r = S(q - Z) for weights (1), inputs (2) and the output (3) into a dot product, and solve for the output code. Jacob's Eq. 7 is the result:

\htmlData{step=1, link=zout}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{q_3^{(i, k)}} = Z_3} + \htmlData{step=2, link=mult}{\htmlClass{t-green}{M}} \Big( \htmlData{step=3, link=const}{\htmlClass{t-red}{NZ_1 Z_2}} \htmlData{step=4, link=cross}{\htmlClass{t-red}{- Z_1 a_2^{(k)} - Z_2 \bar a_1^{(i)}}} + \htmlData{step=5, link=core}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{\sum_{j=1}^N q_1^{(i, j)}q_2^{(j, k)}}}\Big)

Eq. 7 in Jacob et al., arXiv 1712.05877v1, joined on one line; the sums a_2 and \bar a_1 are Eq. 8.

  • \sum_j q_1 q_2: the core, a plain integer dot product of weight codes and input codes, accumulated in 32 bits (\texttt{int32 += uint8 * uint8}, Eq. 10). For a matrix product it costs 2N^3 operations.
  • a_2^{(k)} = \sum_j q_2^{(j,k)} and \bar a_1^{(i)} = \sum_j q_1^{(i,j)}: the sum of the input codes and the sum of the weight codes. Each is multiplied by the other side's zero-point. These cost only 2N^2 additions.
  • N Z_1 Z_2: a constant. N is the length of the dot product, 9 for one 3x3 output.
  • M: the only number that is not an integer.
\htmlData{step=1, link=mult}{\htmlClass{t-green}{M} \coloneqq \frac{S_1 S_2}{S_3}} \qquad \htmlData{step=2, link=shift}{\htmlClass{t-green}{M} = 2^{-n} M_0}

Eq. 5 and Eq. 6 in Jacob et al., arXiv 1712.05877v1.

  • S_1, S_2, S_3: the scales of the weights, the inputs and the output. They are known before inference, so M is computed offline.
  • M_0: M scaled up into [0.5, 1) and stored as a 32-bit integer, which keeps at least 30 bits of relative accuracy. Jacob found M always lies in (0, 1).
  • 2^{-n}: a right shift by n bits, with rounding to nearest. Jacob notes that a shift which rounds ties upward biases the outputs and costs accuracy.

The bias joins the accumulator as a 32-bit integer whose scale is S_1 S_2 and whose zero-point is 0 (Eq. 11), so adding it is one integer add. In step 5 the error against floating point is smaller than half an output step: the integer pipeline loses nothing beyond the rounding of the final code.

Now look at step 4 again. The term Z_2 \bar a_1 depends only on the weights, so it can be folded into the bias offline. But Z_1 a_2 depends on the input, so it must be computed at run time for every output. If the weights have Z_1 = 0, that term disappears. This is why Wu and colleagues at NVIDIA, and Nagel and colleagues at Qualcomm, recommend symmetric weights and allow a zero-point only for activations (Wu Eq. 11, Nagel Eq. 13).

Where these numbers were measured

Jacob's results are for phones, not GPUs. The integer kernels ran on ARM CPU cores of a Snapdragon 835, one thread, with one scale per tensor and training that simulates the rounding (chapter 7). On COCO, a MobileNet SSD detector at 320x320 went from 22.1 mAP in floating point to 21.7 in 8 bits. The half-width model ran in 61 ms instead of 121 ms on a big core, the paper's "up to a 50% reduction in running time". The model also gets 4x smaller.

On a detector

TensorRT, the usual GPU path for a detector, uses a symmetric scheme: no zero-point, and INT8 codes in [-128,127] with a single scale per tensor or per channel. So on a GPU the real decision is the scale, which means the range. A detector's activations have long tails, like the toy above, so the range you pick trades clipping against rounding. Chapter 5 shows how calibration picks it, and why weights get one scale per output channel.

One scale or many? Calibration

A tensor gets one ruler, but a convolution's weights are really many small filters, one per output channel, and their ranges can differ a lot. When one ruler must cover the widest filter, the narrow filters fall between a few marks; giving each output channel its own scale fixes that.

Each row of blue dots is one output channel's nine weights. On the true scale, six channels tower over the rest, and most channels look flat. A per-tensor grid must stretch from the lowest weight to the highest, so its marks are about 0.6 apart. That is fine for the tall channels and terrible for the flat ones.

Make a guess before you press "zoom each channel". The zoom stretches every channel to the same height so you can see its marks. How many of the 256 levels does a typical flat channel get? Press it: most get between three and a dozen, and their nine weights snap onto a few terraces. The red sticks are the rounding error, often a large part of the weight itself. Then press "per-channel": every channel gets its own scale, all 256 levels fit its own range, and the red sticks vanish.

Why do channel ranges differ so much? Batch normalization is folded into the convolution before quantization, which multiplies each output channel's weights by its own factor \gamma_k/\sqrt{\sigma_k^2+\epsilon} (Nagel Eq. 11). Depthwise layers have only nine weights per channel, so nothing averages the spread out. Jacob named this failure mode: weight ranges of different output channels that differ by more than 100x.

One scale per output channel

The scale may change from one output channel to the next because each output value comes from one channel's weights only. The integer dot product for that output still uses a single weight scale, so it can be applied after the sum. A scale per input channel would not factor out of the sum, which is why only output channels get their own (Wu Eq. 10). The TensorRT documentation writes the per-channel rule for convolution weights like this:

\htmlData{step=1, link=grid}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{\mathrm{output}[k,c,r,s]} := \mathrm{clamp}(\mathrm{round}(\htmlClass{t-blue}{\mathrm{input}[k,c,r,s]}} \,/\, \htmlData{step=2, link=weights}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{\mathrm{scale}[k]}}))

Per-channel weight quantization in the TensorRT 11.4 documentation (Quantized Types, Quantization Granularities); k must be the output-channel axis for a convolution.

  • \mathrm{input}[k,c,r,s]: the real weight for output channel k, input channel c, and kernel row and column r, s.
  • \mathrm{scale}[k]: one scale for each output channel, fitted to that channel's own range.
  • \mathrm{output}[k,c,r,s]: the signed INT8 code. TensorRT has no zero-point; the toy uses Krishnamoorthi's "asymmetric, per-channel" variant, which changes the counts by about one bit.

The measured effect is large. In Krishnamoorthi's 8-bit tests, MobileNet-v1 on ImageNet scored 0.001 top-1 with one asymmetric scale per layer and 0.703 with one per channel. Wu found the same for EfficientNet b0 weights once batch norm is folded: 12.93% per-tensor against 76.72% per-channel. Activations stay per-tensor in all three papers, because their scale would not factor out of the sum.

Calibration: choosing the range for activations

Weights are known before inference, so their range is just their min and max. Activations depend on the input image. Calibration runs a few hundred representative images through the float network, records each activation tensor as a histogram, and picks one clip value per tensor. Wu used 512 images from the training set for each detector and segmenter; Nagel says between 500 and 1000 unlabeled images usually suffice.

Max keeps every value and pays in rounding error: the one largest value sets the spacing for all the others. The other three calibrators clip a handful of values in the tail, and the marks for everything else get closer together. The bars show the trade directly: clipping error appears, rounding error drops.

Make a guess before you press "entropy (KL)". Percentile 99.99 clips a fixed share of values no matter what the tail looks like. Will the KL method clip more or fewer? It clips fewer here, because it looks at the shape of the tail, not a fixed count. MSE clips fewer still: in squared error, one far outlier costs a lot, so MSE prefers to keep most of the tail.

The MSE calibrator is Nagel's range-setting objective. It chooses the grid ends that minimize the squared difference between a tensor and its quantized copy:

\htmlData{step=1, link=mse}{\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{q_{\text{min}}, q_{\text{max}}}} \quad \htmlData{step=2, link=hist}{\big\lVert \htmlClass{t-blue}{\mathbf{V}}} - \htmlData{step=3, link=grid}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{\hat{\mathbf{V}}(q_{\text{min}}, q_{\text{max}})}\big\rVert^2_F}

Eq. 16 in Nagel et al., arXiv 2106.08295v1 (solved by grid search, golden-section search or a closed-form approximation).

  • \mathbf{V}: the tensor being quantized, here the calibration activations (blue histogram).
  • \hat{\mathbf{V}}(q_{\text{min}}, q_{\text{max}}): the same tensor after quantizing with a grid from q_{\text{min}} to q_{\text{max}}. For ReLU outputs q_{\text{min}} = 0, so only the clip value is searched.
  • \lVert\cdot\rVert_F^2: the sum of squared differences, rounding and clipping error together.

The entropy calibrator compares distributions instead of values. Wu describes it in one sentence: use the KL divergence to minimize information loss between the floating-point values and what the quantized format can represent. It gives no equation, so the one below is our reading, in our notation:

T^\star = \htmlData{step=1, link=kl}{\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{T}\ \mathrm{KL}\big(}\htmlData{step=2, link=hist}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{P_T}} \,\big\|\, \htmlData{step=3, link=grid}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{Q_T}}\big), \qquad \mathrm{KL}(P\,\|\,Q) = \sum_j P_j \log\frac{P_j}{Q_j}

Our reading of the entropy calibration described in words in Wu et al. Sec. 3.4, arXiv 2004.09602v1, which credits it to TensorRT.

  • P_T: the activation histogram cut at T, with every value beyond T added to the last bin, which is what clipping does to them.
  • Q_T: the same bins merged into 128 groups, one per grid level, and spread back evenly. This is what the 8-bit grid can still tell apart.
  • T^\star: the clip value whose two histograms differ least.

The post-training toolkit

Post-training quantization (PTQ) converts a trained network to integers without retraining it, using no data or a small set of unlabeled images (a few hundred to a few thousand). Before anything is rounded, three tools rearrange the network so that it rounds better: move scale between layers, correct the shift that rounding causes, and choose up or down for each weight.

Each row is one channel. In the left panel a channel's weights are the ones that produce it in layer 1; in the right panel they are the ones that read it in layer 2. A per-tensor quantizer lays one ruler over the whole weight tensor, and the ruler must reach the widest channel. A narrow channel then falls between a handful of marks. In the toy, one layer-1 channel uses only three of the 255 levels of an 8-bit grid, so most of its information is rounded away.

The rescue rests on one property of ReLU: for any positive number s, \mathrm{ReLU}(s z) = s\,\mathrm{ReLU}(z). Divide every weight and the bias of channel i in layer 1 by s_i, multiply the weights that read channel i in layer 2 by the same s_i, and the network computes exactly the same function. Cross-layer equalization (CLE) uses this freedom to shrink wide channels and stretch narrow ones until each channel has the same range in both layers.

Make a guess before you press "Equalize". Some weights grow and some shrink by large factors. Will the FP32 output for the test input change? It does not, to the last printed digit: the two lines at the bottom of the figure stay identical. The integer output error, though, drops in the toy from about thirteen percent to about one percent of the output's spread. Note what "equalized" means: each channel gets the same range in layer 1 and in layer 2, so the two panels match row by row. Different channels still have different ranges.

Eq. 21 and Eq. 22 in Nagel et al. 2021, arXiv 2106.08295v1 (macros expanded); the same scale is Eq. 11 in DFQ, arXiv 1906.04721v3.

  • \mathbf{W}^{(1)}, \mathbf{b}^{(1)}: weights and bias of the first layer; \mathbf{W}^{(2)}, \mathbf{b}^{(2)}: the second layer, which reads the first layer's channels.
  • f: a ReLU-like activation; \hat f is the same function, re-parameterized when it is only piecewise linear (ReLU6).
  • \mathbf{S} = \mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{s}): one positive scale per channel. Layer 1 is divided by it and layer 2 is multiplied by it, so the product is unchanged.

Bias correction

Rounding errors are not always zero on average. A layer's error on its output is \Delta\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x}, and its mean is \Delta\mathbf{W}\,\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}]. After a ReLU, the inputs \mathbf{x} are never negative, so their mean is not zero and the whole output distribution shifts. The shift is a fixed vector, so it can be subtracted through the bias at no cost at inference time.

\htmlData{step=1, link=out}{\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}_{\text{corr}}]} = \htmlData{step=2, link=out}{\mathbb{E}[\htmlClass{t-yellow}{\widehat{\mathbf{W}}}\mathbf{x}]} - \htmlData{step=3, link=shift}{\htmlClass{t-red}{\Delta\mathbf{W}}\,\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}]} = \htmlData{step=4, link=out}{\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{y}]}

Eq. 26 in Nagel et al. 2021, arXiv 2106.08295v1 (macros expanded).

  • \widehat{\mathbf{W}}: the quantized weights; \Delta\mathbf{W} = \widehat{\mathbf{W}} - \mathbf{W}, the rounding error.
  • \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}]: the mean input to the layer. With calibration data it is measured (empirical bias correction, Eq. 27). Without data, DFQ computes it from the previous batch-norm layer's shift and scale (analytic bias correction, Eq. 28).

Press "On" under bias correction in the figure: the lighter bar, the mean shift, nearly disappears, and the total error falls by the same part. On MobileNetV2, bias correction alone lifts per-tensor INT8 from 0.12 to 52.02; after CLE and bias absorption it adds a little more, 70.92 to 71.19, with no data at all (Nagel Table 4).

AdaRound: up or down?

Rounding each weight to its nearest grid point minimizes each weight's own error. It does not minimize the error of the layer's output, because the errors of different weights can add up or cancel, depending on how their inputs move together.

The red ellipses are lines of equal output error. When the two inputs are independent, they are circles, and the nearest corner is always the best one. When the inputs are correlated, the ellipses stretch along the diagonal: errors of opposite sign cancel, and errors of the same sign add. The nearest corner rounds both weights down, so both errors have the same sign.

Make a guess before you press "to the best corner". Which corner wins for the correlated inputs? It is (1, 0): it rounds one weight up and one down, its errors partly cancel, and its output error is about 40 percent lower than that of the nearest corner. The nearest corner is only the third best of four.

AdaRound turns this picture into an algorithm. Start from the change in the task loss \mathcal{L} when the weights move by \Delta\mathbf{w}, expand it to second order, drop the gradient term (the trained network sits at a minimum), and ignore interactions between layers:

\htmlData{step=1, link=delta}{\arg\min_{\htmlClass{t-red}{\Delta \mathbf{w}}}\ \mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{w} + \Delta\mathbf{w}) - \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{w})\right]} \;\approx\; \htmlData{step=2, link=hess}{\arg\min_{\Delta \mathbf{w}^{(\ell)}}\ \mathbb{E}\left[{\Delta \mathbf{w}^{(\ell)}}^T \htmlClass{t-red}{\mathbf{H}^{(\mathbf{w}^{(\ell)})}} \Delta \mathbf{w}^{(\ell)}\right]}

Eq. 29 and Eq. 30 in Nagel et al. 2021, arXiv 2106.08295v1 (macros expanded); AdaRound, arXiv 2004.10568v2, derives them in Sec. 2-3.

The Hessian \mathbf{H} of a whole layer is too costly to compute, and choosing up or down for every weight is a hard combinatorial problem. AdaRound makes two more steps. It assumes the loss treats a layer's outputs independently, which turns the Hessian into the input correlation \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^T], as in the figure: minimize the error of the layer's output on calibration data. Then it relaxes the binary choice into a continuous variable that a regularizer pushes to 0 or 1:

\htmlData{step=1, link=loss}{\arg\min_{\mathbf{V}} \left\lVert f_a(\htmlClass{t-blue}{\mathbf{W}} \mathbf{x}) - f_a(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}}\, \hat{\mathbf{x}}) \right\rVert^2_F} + \htmlData{step=3, link=corners}{\lambda f_{\text{reg}}(\mathbf{V})}, \qquad \htmlData{step=2, link=delta}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}} = s \cdot \mathrm{clamp}\left(\left\lfloor \frac{\mathbf{W}}{s} \right\rfloor + h(\mathbf{V}); n, p \right)}

Eq. 35 and Eq. 33 in Nagel et al. 2021, arXiv 2106.08295v1 (macros expanded); Eq. 25 and Eq. 22 in AdaRound, arXiv 2004.10568v2.

  • \lfloor \mathbf{W}/s \rfloor: every weight rounded down; h(\mathbf{V}) \in [0, 1] adds 0 (keep down) or 1 (round up). It is a rectified sigmoid of a learned variable \mathbf{V}, one per weight.
  • f_{\text{reg}}(\mathbf{V}) = \sum_{i,j} 1 - |2 h(\mathbf{V}_{i,j}) - 1|^\beta (Eq. 34): zero only when every h is exactly 0 or 1; \beta is annealed so the choice hardens.
  • \hat{\mathbf{x}}: the layer input with all earlier layers already quantized, so each layer also corrects its predecessors' errors; f_a: the activation function; n, p: the grid limits.

The payoff is large at 4 bits. On ResNet18 with 4-bit weights in every layer, rounding to nearest gives 23.99 top-1 and AdaRound 68.60 (Nagel Table 5, ImageNet). On the segmenter DeeplabV3+ (MobileNetV2 backbone, Pascal VOC mIoU, FP32 72.94), 4-bit weights with 8-bit activations give 6.09 with nearest rounding and 70.86 with AdaRound (AdaRound Table 9). The default setup optimizes on 1024 unlabeled images for 10k iterations; the ImageNet comparison in its Table 7 doubled both, and the DeeplabV3+ run used fewer images because they are larger.

When PTQ falls short

Nagel et al. also give a debugging flow (Sec. 3.7, Fig. 9). The error is FP32 accuracy minus quantized accuracy, compared with the error you can accept:

  1. Run the quantized model with every bit-width set to 32. If the error is not about zero, the simulation itself has a bug: fix it first.
  2. Quantize only the activations, then only the weights. Whichever side fails, apply its fixes: for weights, AdaRound, per-channel scales, CLE and bias correction; for activations, better range setting, and a check that CLE did not unbalance the activation ranges.
  3. If one side still fails, quantize one layer at a time. Plot the failing tensor per channel, then fix it with a custom range or a higher bit-width for that tensor.
  4. Quantize the whole model again. If the error is still too large, go to quantization-aware training (the next chapter).

Wu et al. (arXiv 2004.09602v1) make step 3 systematic with partial quantization. They quantize one layer at a time, sort layers by the accuracy each one costs, and leave the most sensitive layers in floating point. On EfficientNet b0 (ImageNet, classification only; Wu's Table 6 has no detection rows), skipping 10 of 82 layers lifts INT8 top-1 from 72.06 to 76.35 (FP32 76.85). 7 of the ten most sensitive layers are depthwise convolutions, and the most sensitive is the block0 depthwise layer (Fig. 3). Training through the rounding: QAT and the straight-through estimator

Quantization-aware training (QAT) simulates the rounding in the forward pass, so the network can learn weights that survive it. The obstacle is the backward pass: rounding is a staircase, and a staircase has zero slope almost everywhere.

The top panel is the forward pass. The network keeps a full-precision copy of each weight, the shadow weight w (blue). Every forward pass rounds it onto the grid, \hat w (yellow), and uses only the rounded value. The bottom panel is what the backward pass needs: the slope of the staircase. Its true value is zero everywhere except at the jumps, where it is infinite. Press "true", then "1 step" a few times: the loss stays where it is, and the weight never moves.

The straight-through estimator (STE) ignores the staircase in the backward pass and treats rounding as the identity. Inside the grid's range, the gradient passes through unchanged; outside it, where the quantizer clips, it is zero. Now the weight moves, and the rounded value climbs the staircase toward the target.

\htmlClass{t-yellow}{\hat{v}} = \htmlData{step=3, link=round}{\Big\lfloor} \htmlData{step=2, link=clip}{\mathrm{clip}\big(} \htmlData{step=1, link=shadow}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{v} / s}\htmlData{step=2, link=clip}{,\, -Q_N,\, Q_P\big)} \htmlData{step=3, link=round}{\Big\rceil} \htmlData{step=4, link=grid}{\times s}

Eq. 1-2 in Esser et al. (LSQ), arXiv 1902.08153v3, written as one line (our notation). Nagel et al. Eq. 7 (arXiv 2106.08295v1) writes the same operation with a zero-point; Wu et al. Eq. 12 (arXiv 2004.09602v1) writes it as dequantize(quantize(x)).

  • v: the full-precision value, here the shadow weight; \hat v: the value the forward pass uses.
  • s: the step size, the distance between grid levels.
  • Q_N, Q_P: the number of levels below and above zero. For b-bit signed weights, Q_N = 2^{b-1} and Q_P = 2^{b-1} - 1.
  • \lfloor \cdot \rceil: round to the nearest integer. Multiplying back by s returns a float that lies on the grid: this is fake quantization, integer values carried in a float tensor.
\frac{\partial \hat{v}}{\partial v} = \htmlData{step=1, link=ste}{\begin{cases} \htmlClass{t-red}{1} & \text{if } -Q_N < v/s < Q_P \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}} \qquad \text{instead of} \qquad \htmlData{step=2, link=true}{\frac{\partial \lfloor y \rceil}{\partial y} = 0 \text{ almost everywhere}}

Eq. 5 in Esser et al., arXiv 1902.08153v3. The same clipped rule is Eq. 14-15 in Krishnamoorthi, arXiv 1806.08342v1, and Eq. 13 in Wu et al., arXiv 2004.09602v1. Bengio et al., arXiv 1308.3432v1 (Sec. 4, Eq. 13), named the straight-through estimator and define its plain form, identity with no clipping, crediting Hinton's lecture 15b.

Make a guess before you press "5 steps" with the straight-through gradient. The target lies above the top level of the grid. Where will the weight stop? It climbs to the top level and stops there, short of the target. Once the shadow weight passes the top of the range, the clipped STE passes no gradient, so nothing pulls the weight back and nothing moves the range.

The fix is to learn the range too. Learned step size quantization (LSQ, Esser et al.) makes the step s a trainable parameter and differentiates the quantizer with respect to it, again with the STE for the rounding:

\htmlData{step=1, link=step}{\frac{\partial \hat{v}}{\partial s}} = \htmlData{step=2, link=round}{\begin{cases} -v/s + \lfloor v/s \rceil & \text{if } -Q_N < v/s < Q_P \\ -Q_N & \text{if } v/s \le -Q_N \\ Q_P & \text{if } v/s \ge Q_P \end{cases}}

Eq. 3 in Esser et al., arXiv 1902.08153v3 (\sfrac written as a slash). Nagel et al. Eq. 38 is the same gradient in their notation.

Inside the range, the step's gradient is the distance from v/s to its rounded value, so it is largest near a jump. Outside the range it is the full level count, so a clipped value pushes the range outward. LSQ multiplies this gradient by g = 1/\sqrt{N Q_P}, where N is the number of weights in the layer, so the step changes about as fast as the weights do. Press "straight-through + learned step": the step grows until the top level reaches the target, and the loss goes to nearly zero.

Three papers set the range in three ways. Jacob et al. (arXiv 1712.05877v1) do not learn it: weight ranges are the minimum and maximum of the weights, activation ranges are a moving average of the values seen in training, and "backpropagation still happens as usual". The paper does not name the STE. It also turns activation quantization off at the start of training, for 50 thousand to 2 million steps (500 thousand in all its training protocols). PACT (Choi et al., arXiv 1805.06085v2) learns a clipping value for each layer's activations; its gradient comes only from the inputs that are clipped. LSQ learns the step for weights and activations. On the first and last layers they differ too: LSQ always keeps them at 8 bits, PACT leaves them unquantized in its accuracy runs (but assumes 8 bits in its hardware model), and Jacob et al. do not discuss them.

Inside one QAT step on a detector: forward, then backward

The same machinery runs at the scale of a detector. Every convolution gets a fake quantizer on its weights and on its output activations; the rest is ordinary fine-tuning.

Make a guess before you press "6 Gradients": does the gradient stop at the fake quantizers, or reach the weights in the first convolution? It reaches every shadow weight, because the STE lets it through each quantizer. Only values outside the clipping range block it.

Act IVFewer weights

Pruning and Deep Compression

Most weights in a trained network are close to zero, and many of them can be set to exactly zero with little loss, especially if the network is trained a little more afterwards. Whether the zeros make the network faster is a separate question: a GPU kernel can skip only zeros that come in a shape it was built for.

Each cell is one weight. A row is one output channel: the weights that produce one output feature map. A column is one input. A GPU computes this layer as a matrix multiply, and the inputs are the dimension it sums over. The sparsity of the layer is the share of its weights that are zero.

Magnitude pruning is the simplest rule: weights with a small absolute value contribute little to the output, so set them to zero. Han et al. (NIPS 2015) made this work on large networks with three steps. Train the network, remove every connection whose weight is below a threshold, then train the survivors again so they make up for the loss. Repeating prune and retrain does better than pruning once: AlexNet went from 5x fewer weights in one aggressive step to 9x with iterations.

\htmlData{step=1, link=kept}{\htmlClass{t-green}{m_{ij}}} = \mathbf{1}\big[\, \htmlData{step=2, link=mag}{|w_{ij}|} > \htmlData{step=3, link=thr}{t} \,\big], \qquad \htmlData{step=4, link=pruned}{\htmlClass{t-brown}{w_{ij} \leftarrow 0 \ \text{where}\ m_{ij}=0}}, \qquad \htmlData{step=3, link=thr}{t = q \cdot \sigma_{\text{layer}}}

Our transcription of Han et al., arXiv 1506.02626v3, Sec. 4, which gives the rule in words: the threshold is "a quality parameter multiplied by the standard deviation of a layer's weights". Deep Compression (arXiv 1510.00149v5, Sec. 2) builds on this method and removes "all connections with weights below a threshold"; it does not restate the rule.

  • m_{ij}: the mask, 1 if weight (i, j) is kept and 0 if it is pruned. \mathbf{1}[\cdot] is 1 when the condition holds.
  • |w_{ij}|: the weight's magnitude, its absolute value. The sign does not matter, only the size.
  • t: the threshold. \sigma_{\text{layer}} is the standard deviation of the layer's weights, and q is a hand-set quality parameter: larger q, more zeros.

The results were large. AlexNet on ImageNet went from 61M to 6.7M weights, and its top-1 error moved from 42.78% to 42.77%. VGG-16 kept one weight in 13, with top-1 error 31.50% before and 31.34% after. The cost was training time: the pruned AlexNet took 173 hours to retrain, against 75 hours for the original.

Make a guess before you press "2:4". Both the unstructured pattern and 2:4 set half of the weights to zero. Which one keeps more of the total weight magnitude, and which one can a GPU turn into speed? Unstructured pruning keeps the most magnitude, because it removes exactly the smallest weights. The 2:4 pattern must remove two of every four inputs, even in a group where three weights are large, so the histogram shows some large weights removed. Whole channels lose the most. For speed the order reverses. A dense kernel multiplies the scattered zeros like any other number; sparse tensor cores skip half of the multiplies in a 2:4 layer; removing channels leaves a smaller dense layer that any kernel runs faster.

Deep Compression: prune, share, Huffman-code

Deep Compression (Han, Mao and Dally, ICLR 2016) aims at storage, not speed: shrink a model until its weights fit in on-chip memory. It adds two steps after pruning, and their savings multiply.

Prune. The survivors are stored with their position. Deep Compression stores the gap to the previous survivor in a few bits instead of a full column index; if a gap is too long for those bits, it inserts a filler zero.

Share weights. Many surviving weights have nearly the same value, so give them exactly the same value. Within one layer, k-means clustering groups the weights into k clusters, and each weight stores only the index of its cluster: \log_2 k bits. A table of k full-precision centroids, the codebook, holds the shared values. This is quantization with a learned, uneven grid, unlike the evenly spaced integer grid of chapter 4. The paper initializes the centroids evenly between the smallest and largest weight, because that keeps a few centroids for the rare large weights. In the toy, one of those even starting points lands in the gap that pruning left, and it never gets a member.

The centroids are then trained. Every weight in a cluster is the same number, so the loss gradient for that number is the sum of the gradients of all its members:

\htmlData{step=1, link=centroid}{\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \htmlClass{t-yellow}{C_k}}} = \sum_{i,j} \htmlData{step=2, link=grad}{\htmlClass{t-red}{\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial W_{ij}}}} \frac{\partial W_{ij}}{\partial C_k} = \sum_{i,j} \htmlData{step=2, link=grad}{\htmlClass{t-red}{\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial W_{ij}}}} \, \htmlData{step=3, link=member}{\mathbf{1}(I_{ij} = k)}

Eq. 3 in Han, Mao and Dally, arXiv 1510.00149v5 (the indicator, a double-struck 1 in the paper, is written in bold).

  • C_k: the shared value (centroid) of cluster k, one codebook entry.
  • W_{ij}: the weight at position (i, j); its ordinary gradient \partial \mathcal{L} / \partial W_{ij} comes from normal backpropagation.
  • I_{ij}: the cluster index that weight (i, j) stores. \mathbf{1}(I_{ij} = k) is 1 for the members of cluster k and 0 otherwise, so the sum runs over the members only. The index assignment stays fixed; only the centroids move.

The storage gain of sharing alone is r = nb / (n \log_2 k + kb) (Eq. 1): n weights of b bits become n indices plus a codebook of k values. The paper uses 8-bit indices for convolutions and 5-bit indices for FC layers.

Huffman-code. Some clusters and some gap lengths are far more common than others. A Huffman code gives the common symbols short bit strings and the rare ones long strings, with no loss and no retraining. The paper reports a 20% to 30% saving from this step.

On a detector

Deep Compression's speed case is FC layers at batch 1. FC layers once took up to 38% of Fast R-CNN's time, but a modern detector with a feature pyramid is mostly convolutions, and a convolution reuses each weight at every pixel, like the batch-64 column above. So on a GPU, unstructured pruning makes a detector's file smaller, not its inference faster (our reading). For speed, the zeros need a structure the hardware skips: chapter 9 shows which ones, and the decision guide says when to use them.

Lottery tickets and structure a GPU can use

If a pruned network is as accurate as the dense one, why not train the small network from the start? Frankle and Carbin found that this works, but only if the small network starts from the same initial weights it had inside the big one, and finding those weights takes full training first.

Each round of iterative magnitude pruning (IMP) has three steps. Train the network. Remove the smallest 20% of the weights that are still alive. Then set every survivor back to the value it had before any training, its initial value. Repeat. After round 7 about a fifth of the weights are left; after round 16, under 3%.

The surviving subnetwork with its original starting values is a winning ticket. The paper's hypothesis, verbatim: "A randomly-initialized, dense neural network contains a subnetwork that is initialized such that -- when trained in isolation -- it can match the test accuracy of the original network after training for at most the same number of iterations."

\htmlData{step=1, link=net}{f(x;\,} \htmlData{step=2, link=mask}{\htmlClass{t-green}{m}} \odot \htmlData{step=3, link=init}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{\theta_0}}\htmlData{step=1, link=net}{)}, \qquad \htmlData{step=4, link=pm}{P_m = \frac{\lVert m \rVert_0}{|\theta|}}

Frankle and Carbin, arXiv 1803.03635v5: the masked network of Sec. 1 and the sparsity notation of Sec. 2 (unnumbered in the paper).

  • f(x; \theta): the network applied to input x with weights \theta.
  • m: the mask, one 0 or 1 per weight, found by IMP. \odot multiplies element by element, so masked weights are zero.
  • \theta_0: the initial weights, drawn at random before the dense network was trained. Resetting to \theta_0 is what makes a ticket; the control draws new random values instead.
  • P_m: the share of weights that remain. \lVert m \rVert_0 counts the ones in the mask and |\theta| is the total number of weights. P_m = 25\% means 75% are pruned.

Make a guess before you press "random reinit (control)" at round 7. The mask is the same; only the starting values change. Does the structure alone explain the result? In Frankle's LeNet it does not. At about a fifth of the weights, the ticket reaches its best validation loss 2.51x faster than the same mask started from new random values, and the reinitialized network is already less accurate than the dense one. The pruned structure needs the starting values it grew up with.

The convolutional results are similar. On CIFAR10, Conv-2 and Conv-4 tickets reached early stopping up to 3.5x sooner than the dense network, and Conv-6 up to 2.5x. Conv-4 gained up to 3.5 points of test accuracy, at Pm = 11.1%. The cost is the search: iterative pruning trains the network 15 or more times. So the result explains why pruning works more than it saves training.

Structure a GPU can use

Tickets, like Han's pruning, are unstructured: scattered zeros that a dense kernel still multiplies (chapter 8). For speed, remove whole pieces of the computation instead. Then what remains is an ordinary dense network, only thinner, and every kernel and compiler runs it as is.

Filters by L1 norm. Li et al. (ICLR 2017) score each convolution filter by the sum of the absolute values of its weights, s_j = \sum_{l=1}^{n_i} \sum |\mathcal{K}_l| (Sec. 3.1). They remove the filters with the smallest scores, the feature maps those filters produce, and the matching input kernels of the next layer, then retrain. The "Whole channels" button in chapter 8's figure uses this rule. On CIFAR-10, VGG-16 lost 34.2% of its operations and 64.0% of its parameters, with test error 6.75% before and 6.60% after. The paper also measured wall-clock time: inference over the test set went from 1.23 s to 0.73 s on a Titan X (Pascal) with cuDNN v5.1 at batch 128. In the authors' words, "the saved inference time is close to the FLOP reduction".

Channels by batch-norm scale. Network slimming (Liu et al., ICCV 2017) reuses a number every batch-norm layer already has. Batch norm multiplies each normalized channel by a learned scale \gamma, so a channel with a small \gamma contributes little. Slimming adds a penalty that pushes the scales toward zero during training:

L = \sum_{(x, y)} l\big(f(x, W), y\big) + \lambda \sum_{\gamma \in \Gamma} \htmlClass{t-green}{g(\gamma)}, \qquad g(s) = |s|

Eq. 1 in Liu et al. (network slimming), arXiv 1708.06519v1.

  • l(f(x, W), y): the normal training loss on input x with target y and weights W.
  • \Gamma: all batch-norm scales in the network; g(\gamma) = |\gamma| is an L1 penalty, which drives many scales to nearly zero.
  • \lambda: balances the two terms. After training, channels whose \gamma is below one global threshold are removed, and the thinner network is fine-tuned.

On CIFAR-10, a VGG network with 51.0% fewer operations and 2.30M instead of 20.04M parameters had 6.20% test error against 6.34% for the original.

Is it the weights or the shape? Liu et al. (ICLR 2019) took the thinner architectures these methods produce and trained them from random initial weights, with a fair training budget. For structured pruning, from scratch was as good as or better than fine-tuning the kept weights. So the value of structured pruning is the architecture it finds. Their detection row:

2:4: zeros in a shape the tensor cores skip

Mishra et al. (NVIDIA) describe a middle ground. In every group of four consecutive weights along the dimension a matrix multiply sums over, at least two must be zero: the 2:4 pattern, exactly 50% sparse. The kept values are packed into half the space, each with a 2-bit index of its position in the group. Because every group has the same shape, the sparse tensor cores of the A100 GPU read memory at full width and pick the matching inputs in hardware. Their peak rate doubles: from 312 to 624 trillion operations per second for FP16, and from 624 to 1248 for INT8 (Table 1). The paper prunes only layers whose summed dimension is a multiple of 16 for FP16 and 32 for INT8, and TensorRT from version 8.0 runs 2:4 weights automatically.

The paper measures speed only for one INT8 matrix multiply on the A100, with both outer dimensions 10240: larger multiplies get "nearly a 2x speedup" (Fig. 3). It reports no speedup for a whole network. So the honest summary is up to 2x faster math on the layers that are bound by arithmetic, and the end-to-end gain of a detector is something to measure.

The recipe is short (Sec. 4.1). Train the network dense. Prune each group of four to its two largest weights. Retrain with the same optimizer, learning-rate schedule and number of epochs, with the mask held fixed. For detection and segmentation (Sec. 4.3), train the backbone on ImageNet and the detector on COCO as usual, then prune backbone and heads together and repeat only the COCO fine-tuning: "the task-specific heads have to be fine-tuned before they can be pruned". INT8 calibration comes after the sparse retraining (Sec. 4.4).

Act VThe LLM era

Outliers: LLM.int8() and SmoothQuant

Large language models (LLMs) grow a few activation channels that are far larger than all the others, and those channels are large in almost every token. One INT8 scale for the whole tensor then spends nearly all of its 256 levels on those few channels, and every other channel rounds to almost nothing.

The front surface is the input of one linear layer: one row per token, one column per channel. Three ridges run through every token. With one scale for the whole tensor, the step between levels is set by the tallest ridge, and a typical channel spans only about 3 of the 256 levels. SmoothQuant computes the same quantity for a real layer and gets 2-3 levels for the non-outlier channels (Sec. 3). That is why a typical channel in the toy is off by almost half its own size, and why the floor of the surface is red.

How large are the outliers? The two papers measure different models and say it differently, so here are both, unmerged. Dettmers et al. find feature dimensions up to 20x larger than the others, which "usually have a range of [-3.5, 3.5]" (3-20x in their Table 4). Xiao et al. (SmoothQuant) write that outliers are "~100x larger than most of the activation values", and their Fig. 4 shows channels "greater than 70" in OPT-13B.

Dettmers et al. first make the scales finer. A matrix multiply is many independent dot products, one per row of X and column of W, so each token row gets its own scale and each output column of W gets its own scale. This is vector-wise quantization:

\begin{aligned} \htmlData{step=3, link=act wt}{\mathbf{C}_{f_{16}}} &\approx \frac{1}{\htmlData{step=1, link=act}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{\mathbf{c}_{x_{f16}}}}\otimes \htmlData{step=2, link=wt}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{\mathbf{c}_{w_{f16}}}}} \htmlData{step=3, link=act wt}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{\mathbf{C}_{i32}}} = S\cdot\mathbf{C}_{i32} \\ &= \mathbf{S}\cdot\mathbf{A}_{i8}\mathbf{B}_{i8} = \htmlData{step=4, link=act wt}{\mathbf{S}\cdot \htmlClass{t-yellow}{Q(\mathbf{A}_{f16})\phantom{.} Q(\mathbf{B}_{f16})}}, \end{aligned}

Eq. 7 in Dettmers et al., arXiv 2208.07339v2.

  • \mathbf{c}_{x_{f16}}: one scaling constant per row of X (per token), from that row's largest magnitude.
  • \mathbf{c}_{w_{f16}}: one scaling constant per column of W (per output).
  • \otimes: the outer product, which gives one rescaling factor for each entry of the output.
  • \mathbf{C}_{i32}: the integer result of the INT8 multiply, accumulated in 32 bits; Q is the absmax quantizer and \mathbf{S} undoes both scales.

Per-token scales do not help with the problem in the figure, though. The outliers live in columns of X (channels), and every token row contains them. So the second step splits the multiply by channel:

\mathbf{C}_{f16} \approx \htmlData{step=1, link=outlier}{\htmlClass{t-green}{\sum\limits_{h\in O} \mathbf{X}_{f16}^h \mathbf{W}_{f16}^h}} + \htmlData{step=2, link=act}{\mathbf{S}_{f16} \cdot \htmlClass{t-yellow}{\sum\limits_{h\not\in O} \mathbf{X}_{i8}^h \mathbf{W}_{i8}^h}}

Eq. 8 in Dettmers et al., arXiv 2208.07339v2 (the mixed-precision decomposition).

  • O: the set of feature dimensions h that hold at least one value larger than a threshold, 6.0 in the paper. They are multiplied in FP16 (green).
  • h \not\in O: all other dimensions, multiplied in INT8 with the vector-wise scales of Eq. 7 (yellow).
  • \mathbf{X}^h, \mathbf{W}^h: column h of X and row h of W, so each sum is one partial matrix multiply.

Press LLM.int8() in the figure. The three ridges turn green and leave the INT8 grid, and the rest of the tensor gets per-token scales. A typical channel's error falls from almost half its size to under 2%. In a real model the set O is tiny: at most 7 dimensions up to 13B parameters, about 0.1% of all dimensions. So more than 99.9% of the values are still multiplied in 8 bits.

The method is about memory first. It cuts the footprint of BLOOM-176B by 1.96x and lets OPT-175B run on 8x RTX 3090 consumer GPUs. The paper states no degradation up to 175B. Its own evidence at that size is a zero-shot accuracy plot (Fig. 1); its perplexity table stops at 13B, where LLM.int8() matches 32-bit exactly (12.45 against 12.45 on C4). An independent check comes from SmoothQuant's Table 3 on OPT-175B: average zero-shot accuracy 66.7 against 66.9 for FP16, so "near-zero" is the safer word.

Speed is the weak point. The FP16 side multiply and the splitting cost time. In the paper's own matrix-multiply benchmark (Table 5, GPU not stated), LLM.int8() runs at 0.86x the speed of FP16 for a layer of the 6.7B model's size, and is faster only from 13B-sized layers up (1.22x, then 1.81x at 175B size). SmoothQuant measured whole models: OPT-13B in PyTorch on an A100, batch 4, 256 tokens, takes 237.1 ms with LLM.int8() against 152.6 ms in FP16 (Table 11).

Fix two: move the difficulty into the weights (SmoothQuant)

If the trouble is that channels differ, why not give each activation channel its own scale, as chapter 5 did for weights? SmoothQuant's Table 1 shows that this would work: simulated per-channel INT8 activations keep OPT-175B at 71.4 average accuracy against 71.6 in FP16, where per-tensor activations fall to 32.3. The problem is the integer matrix multiply:

\mathbf{Y} = \htmlData{step=1, link=act}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{\text{diag}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{X}}^{\text{FP16}})}} \cdot \htmlData{step=3, link=act wt}{(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{\mathbf{\bar{X}}^{\text{INT8}}\cdot \mathbf{\bar{W}}^{\text{INT8}}})} \cdot \htmlData{step=2, link=wt}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{\text{diag}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{W}}^{\text{FP16}})}}

Eq. 2 in Xiao et al. (SmoothQuant), arXiv 2211.10438v7.

  • \mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{X}}: one step size per token (a row of X). It multiplies whole rows of the output, so it can be applied after the integer multiply.
  • \mathbf{\Delta}_{\mathbf{W}}: one step size per output channel (a column of W), applied after the multiply in the same way.
  • \mathbf{\bar{X}}^{\text{INT8}}\cdot \mathbf{\bar{W}}^{\text{INT8}}: the integer multiply. Each output sums over the input channels, so a different scale per input channel would sit inside that sum and cannot be pulled out.

So SmoothQuant changes the tensors instead of the scales. Divide each input channel j of X by a number s_j and multiply row j of W by the same number. The product does not change:

\mathbf{Y} = \htmlData{step=1, link=act}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{(\mathbf{X}\text{diag}(\mathbf{s})^{-1})}} \cdot \htmlData{step=2, link=wt}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{(\text{diag}(\mathbf{s})\mathbf{W})}} = \htmlData{step=3, link=act wt}{\hat{\mathbf{X}} \hat{\mathbf{W}}}

Eq. 3 in Xiao et al., arXiv 2211.10438v7.

\htmlData{step=3, link=s}{\mathbf{s}_j} = \htmlData{step=1, link=act}{\htmlClass{t-red}{\max(|\mathbf{X}_j|)^{\alpha}}} / \htmlData{step=2, link=wt}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{\max(|\mathbf{W}_j|) ^{1-\alpha}}}

Eq. 4 in Xiao et al., arXiv 2211.10438v7.

  • \mathbf{s}_j: the smoothing factor of input channel j, drawn as the line between the two surfaces. It is computed once, offline, and folded into the layer that produces X (for example the LayerNorm before it), so it costs nothing at run time.
  • \max(|\mathbf{X}_j|): the largest magnitude of channel j over calibration data (512 random sentences from the Pile in the paper).
  • \max(|\mathbf{W}_j|): the largest magnitude in row j of W, the weights that multiply channel j.
  • \alpha: the migration strength. At 1, all the difficulty goes to the weights (every channel of X ends with maximum 1); at 0, all of it stays with the activations (every row of W ends with maximum 1).

Make a guess before you drag alpha to 1. The weights started flat and easy. Where do the three ridges go? They move into the weights: the activation surface goes flat and blue, and three red ridges rise in the weight surface, because those rows now carry the large values. At 0.5 the two errors are both a few percent per channel. The paper uses 0.5 for all OPT and BLOOM models and finds a sweet spot of 0.4-0.6 on OPT-175B; other model families need more, for example 0.9 for Llama-2-70B. In the toy, the three big channels dominate the output, so the output error stays small in every setting; it still falls to under a third from no smoothing to alpha 0.5.

The rescaling algebra is the same one that cross-layer equalization uses for weights in chapter 6 (our reading); SmoothQuant applies it across the activation and the weight of one layer.

Because the scales fold away, every linear layer stays a plain INT8 matrix multiply, and the method needs no FP16 side path. On OPT-175B:

Vision transformers have a different problem

Vision transformers (ViTs) are not known to grow LLM-style ridges in the sources here. Their trouble is the shape of two distributions. After the softmax in attention, almost every value is near 0 and a few are near 1; after the GELU activation, the values are lopsided, with large positives and a small negative tail. One uniform grid fits neither. PTQ4ViT (Yuan et al., arXiv 2111.12293v3) gives each of these tensors two ranges with two scales. With it, DeiT-S at 8-bit weights and activations keeps 79.47% ImageNet top-1 against 79.80% in FP32, where a plain post-training baseline reaches 77.65%. The paper reports image classification only, with no detection results.

On a detector

None of these sources measures LLM-style outlier channels in a convolutional detector, so treat this as a diagnosis to run, not a result. Record the per-channel maximum of each layer input over your calibration images. If a few channels are tens of times larger than the rest, per-tensor INT8 activations will fail in that layer as they do here. The two fixes are the ones in this chapter: keep that layer in FP16, or fold a per-channel scale into the previous layer. For a ViT or transformer-based detector, check the softmax and GELU outputs first. The decision guide puts this into the INT8 debugging steps.

Four-bit weights: GPTQ and AWQ

When a language model writes text one token at a time, each layer reads all of its weights to do a little arithmetic on one token, so it waits on memory (the "LLM layer, 1 token" dot in chapter 1). Storing the weights in 4 bits cuts those bytes by four, and the two methods in this chapter make 4-bit weights accurate without any retraining.

GPTQ: round one column, repair the rest

Round to nearest treats every weight alone. GPTQ (Frantar et al.) asks what the layer's output needs, using real inputs from a small calibration set:

\htmlData{step=3, link=loss}{\text{argmin}_{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{\mathbf{\widehat{W}}}} \, ||}\htmlData{step=1, link=w}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{\mathbf{W} \mathbf{X}}} - \htmlData{step=2, link=q}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{\mathbf{\widehat{W}} \mathbf{X}}}\htmlData{step=3, link=loss}{||_2^2.}

Eq. 1 in Frantar et al. (GPTQ), arXiv 2210.17323v2.

  • \mathbf{W}: the layer's weights, one row per output; blue bars in the figure.
  • \mathbf{X}: the layer's inputs for the calibration samples, one column per sample.
  • \mathbf{\widehat{W}}: the quantized weights, restricted to the grid; yellow bars.
  • The squared norm is the total output error, the red line in the chart.

The algorithm walks the columns from left to right. It rounds one column onto the grid, measures the rounding error, and shifts every column not yet rounded so that the layer's output moves back toward where it was. Press the column buttons and watch the blue bars to the right of the frame move away from their outlines: that is the repair. The red bars above the grid say how much of the error each later column takes. They come from H, which records how the input channels move together; when two channels are correlated, one can stand in for the other. This is the same input Hessian that AdaRound uses to choose up or down in chapter 6.

The shift is the update of Optimal Brain Surgeon, applied to quantization by the earlier OBQ method (Frantar et al., arXiv 2208.11580v2), which GPTQ builds on:

\htmlData{step=3, link=err}{\htmlClass{t-red}{\boldsymbol{\delta}_F}} = - \frac{\htmlData{step=1, link=q}{\htmlClass{t-red}{w_q - \text{quant}(w_q)}}}{[\mathbf{H}_F^{-1}]_{qq}} \cdot \htmlData{step=2, link=u}{(\mathbf{H}_F^{-1})_{:, q}}

Eq. 2 in Frantar et al., arXiv 2210.17323v2 (the update half; the paper's Eq. 2 also picks which weight goes next, and GPTQ replaces that choice with a fixed left-to-right order).

  • w_q - \text{quant}(w_q): the rounding error of the weight just quantized.
  • \mathbf{H}_F = 2\mathbf{X}_F\mathbf{X}_F^\top: the Hessian of the output error over the weights F that are still in full precision. GPTQ adds a small damping term, 1% of its mean diagonal.
  • (\mathbf{H}_F^{-1})_{:, q}: how strongly each remaining weight should move to cancel that error.
  • \boldsymbol{\delta}_F: the change applied to all the remaining weights in that row.

Two observations make this cheap. Every row uses the same column order, so all rows share one sequence of inverse Hessians. And that whole sequence is one Cholesky factorization of H-1 (Sec. 4, Step 3). After line 3 of Algorithm 1, the symbol H-1 means that upper Cholesky factor U, not the inverse itself:

\begin{aligned} &\htmlData{step=1, link=q}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{\mathbf{Q}_{:, j} \gets \text{quant}(\mathbf{W}_{:, j})}} \\ &\htmlData{step=2, link=err}{\htmlClass{t-red}{\mathbf{E}_{:, j - i}} \gets (\mathbf{W}_{:, j} - \mathbf{Q}_{:, j}) \, / \, [\mathbf{H}^{-1}]_{jj}} \\ &\htmlData{step=3, link=u}{\mathbf{W}_{:, j:(i + B)} \gets \mathbf{W}_{:, j:(i + B)} - \mathbf{E}_{:, j - i} \cdot \mathbf{H}^{-1}_{j, j:(i + B)}} \end{aligned}

Algorithm 1, lines 6 to 8, in Frantar et al., arXiv 2210.17323v2. The paper uses blocks of B = 128 columns and applies the update to later blocks in one batch.

Everything here is forward passes and linear algebra: no gradient, no training. GPTQ feeds 128 random segments of 2048 tokens from C4 (generic web text) through the model one block at a time, and quantizes each layer from its own inputs. In the toy, GPTQ ends about 5 times below round to nearest on the same 4-bit grid. Reading H-1 in Algorithm 1 as the plain inverse instead of the Cholesky factor gives a different, wrong update.

At full scale, GPTQ quantizes OPT-175B in 4.2h and BLOOM-176B in 3.8h on a single A100 with 80GB. On OPT-175B, WikiText2 perplexity goes from 8.34 in FP16 to 8.37 with 4-bit GPTQ, where 4-bit round to nearest gives 10.54. At 3 bits, round to nearest collapses to 7.3e3 and GPTQ holds 8.68. The algorithm also works on vision models: ResNet-50 with 4-bit weights keeps 75.71% ImageNet top-1 against 76.13% in full precision (Table 1).

The speed numbers need their conditions. The 3-bit OPT-175B model takes 63GB and fits on one 80GB A100; the paper says a 4-bit version would not fully fit. Generating one token at a time (batch size 1), it takes 71 ms per token on that one GPU, against 230 ms for FP16 spread over 5x80GB GPUs: 3.24x. The custom kernel multiplies a quantized matrix by one FP16 vector, so the gain comes from moving fewer bytes, and the paper lists speedups in batched execution as outside its study.

Mixed precision is awkward for hardware, so AWQ protects those channels another way. The rounding error of a weight is at most half a step either way, so after dividing by s it shrinks by about s, as long as the scaled weight does not raise the group's maximum (AWQ Eq. 3 gives the error ratio as (\Delta'/\Delta)(1/s)). Eq. 4 in Lin et al. (AWQ), arXiv 2306.00978v6 (the source splits "diag" across an alignment mark; joined here).

  • \mathbf{s_X}: the average magnitude of each input channel's activation over calibration data, the top bars. SmoothQuant used the maximum; AWQ uses the mean.
  • Q: the weight quantizer, INT3 or INT4 with a group size of 128 in the paper.
  • \alpha: one exponent for the whole layer. At 0 nothing is scaled (round to nearest); at 1 the scale equals the activation size.
  • \mathcal{L}: the output error; the paper prints no norm subscript, and the toy uses the Frobenius norm. The paper searches 20 values of alpha on [0, 1].

FP8: floating point in eight bits

An 8-bit integer spaces its 256 levels evenly, so the gap between two levels is the same near zero and near the largest value. An 8-bit float spends some of its bits on an exponent instead, so its levels are dense near zero and sparse far away, and that suits tensors in which most values are small and a few are huge.

Read the number line from right to left. The dashed line is the tensor's largest magnitude, and every format is scaled so that its own largest value lands there. INT8's 127 positive levels are evenly spaced, so on a logarithmic axis they crowd into the top few doublings and stop at 1/127 of the maximum. Anything smaller rounds to zero. E4M3 puts the same number of marks, eight, into every doubling (a binade), so its relative precision is the same for small and large values. E5M2 has four marks per binade but covers almost twice as many binades.

Press Uniform first. Values fill the range evenly, so the even INT8 grid fits them best, at about 8 bits of accuracy in our computation. Press Normal: INT8 still wins, because a bell curve has no value far beyond four times its RMS.

Make a guess before you press "Student-t". This tensor has heavy tails: most values sit near 1, but a few reach about 20. Which format has the lowest error? The answer is E4M3, with about a third of INT8's mean squared error, which is less than one bit of accuracy more. The single largest value sets INT8's step to about 0.15, which is coarse for the values near 1 that make up most of the tensor. E4M3 keeps eight marks per binade wherever the values are. Van Baalen et al. find the same winners among these formats for their three test shapes: E4 is best on the Student-t, but with only 5 bits of accuracy, and INT8 reaches 8 bits on the uniform. Their FP8-E4 is a variant with 255 distinct values, not the E4M3 drawn here, so their numbers and ours agree in shape, not digit for digit.

How eight bits become a number

A float code has three fields: a sign bit, an exponent field that picks the binade, and a mantissa field that picks a mark inside it. E4M3 has 4 exponent bits and 3 mantissa bits; E5M2 has 5 and 2. The grids at the bottom of the figure lay out all 256 codes with the exponent field running left to right, so moving one column doubles the value.

\htmlData{step=1, link=sign}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{z} = (-1)^{s}} \cdot \htmlData{step=2, link=exp}{2^{\hat{e}-B}} \cdot \htmlData{step=3, link=mant}{\left(1+\frac{\hat{m}}{2^M}\right)} \qquad \htmlData{step=4, link=sub}{\text{if } \hat{e}=0:\;\; \htmlClass{t-yellow}{z} = (-1)^{s} \cdot \frac{\hat{m}}{2^M} \cdot 2^{1-B}}

Sec. 2 in van Baalen et al. 2023, arXiv 2303.17951v2, normal case and the subnormal footnote, with a sign factor added (our notation). The bit layouts are Table 1 in Micikevicius et al. 2022, arXiv 2209.05433v2.

  • s: the sign bit. The top half of each code grid has s = 0, the bottom half s = 1.
  • \hat{e}: the exponent field read as an unsigned integer, one grid column per value.
  • B: the exponent bias, 7 for E4M3 and 15 for E5M2. It shifts the binades so that half of them lie below 1.
  • \hat{m} and M: the mantissa field and its number of bits (3 or 2). The mantissa picks one of 2^M evenly spaced marks inside a binade.
  • The subnormal case: when the exponent field is zero, the leading 1 is dropped, so the marks continue evenly down to zero instead of halving forever. These codes are the faint first column of each grid.

Try the largest E4M3 code. Its pattern is S.1111.110: exponent field 15, mantissa 6, so 2^{15-7} \cdot (1 + 6/8) = 1.75 \cdot 2^8, which is 448. The largest E5M2 code, S.11110.11, gives 57,344.

Two encodings, one for each job

Micikevicius et al. propose two encodings: E4M3 for weights and activations, where precision matters, and E5M2 for gradients, where range matters. E5M2 follows the IEEE rules of FP16, with infinities and several NaN (not a number) codes in its top exponent column. E4M3 breaks them on purpose. It has no infinities and keeps only one NaN mantissa pattern, so the top exponent column holds real numbers. With IEEE-style special values its maximum would be 240; the reclaimed codes add the magnitudes 256, 288, 320, 352, 384, 416, 448.

A scale is still needed

Eighteen binades sound like a lot, but they are fixed in place: without a scale, the largest E4M3 value is always 448 and the smallest is always tiny. A real tensor may live anywhere. So FP8, like INT8 in chapter 4, multiplies each tensor by a scale first. Micikevicius et al. choose the scale "such that the maximum magnitude in the tensor becomes close to the maximum representable magnitude in the corresponding format" (Sec. 2). The figure does exactly that.

\htmlClass{t-yellow}{x_{q}} = \htmlData{step=3, link=cast}{\mathrm{castToFp8}\Big(} \htmlData{step=2, link=clip}{\mathrm{clip}\big(} \htmlData{step=1, link=scale}{\frac{\htmlClass{t-blue}{x}}{s}}, \htmlData{step=2, link=clip}{-448, 448\big)} \htmlData{step=3, link=cast}{\Big)}

  • s: the per-tensor scale, stored in FP32, FP16 or BF16. Dividing by it slides the tensor onto the format's binades.

How much the scale matters is measured on a language model. Micikevicius et al. cast GPT3 1.3B to E4M3 with exponent bias 7 and no scale, with both the matrix-multiply inputs and the residual-add inputs in FP8. Perplexity rose to 12.59 against a bfloat16 baseline of 10.19. With calibrated scales (per-channel for weights, per-tensor for activations) it was 10.44 (Sec. 4.3). The format alone is not enough.

Claim card: FP8 versus INT8 for inference (van Baalen et al. 2023, arXiv 2303.17951v2)

Claim
"If a distribution has very significant outliers, the FP8-E4/FP8-E5 format is more accurate, but if the distributions are well-behaved and more Gaussian-shaped, then INT8 or FP8-E2/FP8-E3 is expected to perform better" (Sec. 4.1). Their conclusion: FP8 may be good for training, but for inference it does not warrant dedicated hardware in place of INT8.
Measured
Bits of accuracy on uniform, normal and Student-t inputs (Fig. 5); post-training quantization of many networks, with the best of several range settings for each format (Table 1); and quantization-aware training with the same recipe for INT8 and FP8 (Table 2). Both formats get the same freedom of scale, and the whole network runs in 8 bits. After training, INT8 is best on every 2D vision row, for example ResNet18 ImageNet top-1 70.43 for INT8 against 69.35 for FP8-E4, and DeepLabV3 (MobileNetV2, Pascal VOC) mIoU 73.99 against 73.22.
Hardware cost
A count of two-input gates per multiply-accumulate unit, taken as a stand-in for area and power. An FP8-E4 unit needs 53% more gates than INT8 with an FP16 accumulator and 183% more with an FP32 accumulator (Fig. 3; the INT8 unit has a 27-bit fixed-point accumulator, our reading). This argues about dedicated accelerators. On an NVIDIA GPU the vendor has already fixed the FP8 and INT8 throughput, so the choice there depends on which formats the GPU and TensorRT support (our reading).
Their format
FP8-E4 reserves one code and has 255 values; it is not the E4M3 of Micikevicius et al. and TensorRT.
Falsifier
A near-Gaussian network in which FP8-E4 beats INT8 after training with the same range learning; a synthesized FP8-E4 unit at or below INT8 cost with the same accumulator; or an outlier-heavy model in which neither INT8 with 16-bit activations on the outlier layers nor quantization-aware training matches FP8-E4.

What Micikevicius et al. measured

Their main result is training, not post-training quantization. Networks trained with FP8 inputs to every matrix multiply match their 16-bit baselines within run-to-run noise, except MobileNet v2 (ImageNet top-1 71.04 against 71.65). ResNet-50 v1.5 reached 76.76 against 76.71 (Table 2). For post-training quantization they test only language models: BERT Base keeps SQuAD v1.1 F1 at 88.09 in E4M3 against 88.19 in 16 bits, where INT8 drops to 76.89 (Table 5). Language models have the large activation outliers of chapter 10, which is the case where the figure says FP8 should win. The paper has no detection or segmentation rows.

On a detector

FP8 on a GPU needs compute capability 8.9 or newer in TensorRT. A T4 (7.5) supports only FP32, FP16, INT8, and on A100 and A10 FP8 is N/A. On GPUs that have it, FP8 convolutions with kernel sizes larger than 32 (the docs give a 7x7 convolution as the example) fall back to slower FP16 or FP32 kernels on SM89, SM90, SM120 and SM121. In van Baalen's naive post-training test, YoloV5 on COCO (mAP 56.3) changed by -1.8 points with INT8 and by -2 with their FP8-E4. That is simulated quantization on their own tooling, and the paper does not state the YoloV5 variant or input size. Our reading: convolutional detectors rarely have the heavy tails where FP8 wins, so per-channel INT8 stays the first choice, and FP8 is worth a test for transformer blocks with outliers. What an inference compiler does

A trained network arrives as a graph of small operations, and running each one as its own GPU kernel wastes time on kernel launches and on writing every intermediate tensor to memory. An inference compiler such as TensorRT rewrites the graph into fewer, larger kernels, then times several implementations of each kernel on your GPU and keeps the fastest.

Step through the buttons from left to right. Blue boxes are full-precision operations, yellow boxes are Q and DQ nodes, and green boxes are fused kernels. The bars below the graph keep score: how many kernels the GPU launches for one image, and how many bytes they write to memory.

Make a guess before you press "Absorb Q/DQ". Folding batch normalization removed two kernels. Will absorbing the Q and DQ nodes save more memory traffic, or less? It saves more: 6.25 MiB becomes 3.91 MiB in the toy, while folding BN saved only 1.56 MiB. Each DQ node writes a full FP16 copy of an INT8 tensor that the next convolution could have read directly. Once the convolution reads INT8 and writes INT8, those copies are gone, and the tensor that crosses memory is half the size.

The build phase

TensorRT works in two phases. In the build phase, "a builder compiles your network and selects the fastest available kernel (a low-level GPU function) for each layer on your target GPU"; the result is a serialized engine, also called a plan file. In the runtime phase, the application loads the engine and runs it (architecture, how TensorRT works). Between those two sentences sits most of the work: "The builder eliminates dead computations, folds constants, reorders, and combines operations to run more efficiently on the GPU" (capabilities, the build phase).

Fusion helps in two ways. It removes kernel launches, which cost "typically 5-15 us per launch on the host", and it "avoids materializing intermediate tensors in DRAM" (performance, enabling layer fusion). The four steps in the figure are fusions from that documentation:

  1. Fold batch normalization. At inference, batch normalization is a fixed per-channel multiply and add, so it can be moved into the convolution's weights and bias. The docs say TensorRT "combines weights, such as convolution with batch normalization", and list a Conv+Scale fusion done by "adjusting the convolution weights".
  2. Fuse conv, bias and activation. Conv+ReLU is in the fusion catalog for any convolution, and a bias is part of the convolution itself.
  3. Absorb Q/DQ. "TensorRT expects a Q/DQ layer pair on each input of quantizable layers." A convolution fuses with the DQ on its input and the Q on its output and becomes a quantized layer that reads and writes INT8 (explicit quantization).
  4. Fuse the residual add. Conv+ElementWise is in the catalog, and a trailing activation fuses after it. For this to happen in INT8, the docs say to quantize the residual input of the skip connection. One more rule: "The precision of the first input to the element-wise addition layer determines the fusion output's precision."
\htmlData{step=1, link=bn}{\gamma\,\frac{(W * x + b) - \mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^2+\epsilon}} + \beta} \;=\; \htmlData{step=2, link=conv}{\underbrace{\htmlClass{t-blue}{\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{\sigma^2+\epsilon}}\,W}}_{W'} * x} \;+\; \htmlData{step=3, link=conv}{\underbrace{\htmlClass{t-blue}{\frac{\gamma\,(b-\mu)}{\sqrt{\sigma^2+\epsilon}} + \beta}}_{b'}}

Batch normalization after a convolution, folded into new weights and bias. Our notation and algebra; the TensorRT docs state only that the weights are combined.

  • W * x + b: the convolution, with weights W and bias b.
  • \mu, \sigma^2: the per-channel mean and variance that batch normalization stored during training. At inference they are constants.
  • \gamma, \beta: the learned per-channel scale and shift; \epsilon is a small constant that avoids division by zero.
  • W', b': the folded weights and bias. One convolution with these gives the same output as the convolution and the batch normalization together.

Explicit Q/DQ and strongly typed networks

A Q node and a DQ node are the two halves of the quantizer from chapter 4, with the zero-point fixed at zero. TensorRT defines them like this:

\htmlData{step=1, link=q}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{x_{q}} = \mathrm{quantize}(\htmlClass{t-blue}{x}, s) = \mathrm{roundWithTiesToEven}\big(\mathrm{clip}(\tfrac{\htmlClass{t-blue}{x}}{s}, -128, 127)\big)} \qquad \htmlData{step=2, link=dq}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{x} = \mathrm{dequantize}(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{x_{q}}, s) = \htmlClass{t-yellow}{x_{q}} \ast s}

TensorRT 11.4 documentation, quantized types schemes, INT8 (function names set upright).

  • x: a full-precision value; x_q: its 8-bit integer code in [-128,127].
  • s: the scale, an FP32, FP16 or BF16 number that a calibration tool wrote into the Q/DQ pair before the model reached TensorRT.

In the exported graph, a Q followed by a DQ only simulates quantization: the tensor goes to INT8 and straight back. TensorRT turns this simulation into real INT8 arithmetic. It moves Q nodes backward and DQ nodes forward, "so quantization happens as early as possible" and "dequantization happens as late as possible", and then fuses them into the neighbouring convolutions. The docs warn that this has a cost if it fails: "Performance can decrease if TensorRT cannot fuse the operations with the surrounding Q/DQ layers, so be conservative when adding Q/DQ nodes". They also recommend per-tensor scales for activations and per-channel scales for weights, as in chapter 5.

Since TensorRT 11.0.0, explicit Q/DQ is the only way in. The release notes say: "Implicit quantization has been removed. The IInt8Calibrator class and all subclasses (IInt8EntropyCalibrator, IInt8EntropyCalibrator2, IInt8MinMaxCalibrator, IInt8LegacyCalibrator), setInt8Calibrator(), setDynamicRange(), resetDynamicRange(), and all related calibration and dynamic-range APIs have been removed. Use explicit quantization with Q/DQ nodes instead. Models can be quantized using the TensorRT Model Optimizer or by manually inserting Q/DQ nodes." The same release made networks strongly typed: "Strongly typed networks are now the default: createNetworkV2() produces a strongly typed network by default in TensorRT 11.0.0. Weak typing is no longer supported."

Weak typing was the old convenience: builder flags such as kFP16 and kINT8 let TensorRT pick a lower precision layer by layer. Those flags are gone too. Now the model states the type of every tensor, so even an FP16 engine needs an FP16 model (the migration guide points to Model Optimizer's AutoCast for that conversion). TensorRT no longer calibrates. Calibration happens before export, in Model Optimizer or another quantization tool, and "the resulting scales are embedded directly into the Q/DQ nodes". The compiler's job is now fusion and kernel choice, not precision choice.

Tactic selection

Press "Time tactics". For every fused layer, TensorRT has several implementations, called tactics, that differ in tiling, data layout and other details. It does not predict which is fastest; it measures: "TensorRT runs through all the possible tactics in the engine-building phase and selects the fastest ones." By default each tactic runs for at least four iterations and the times are averaged. The search effort is set by the builder optimization level, whose default is 3.

Because the winners are measured, they belong to the GPU they were measured on. "By default, TensorRT engines are specific to both the TensorRT version and the GPU on which they were created." Timing is also noisy: "Running the builder in parallel with other GPU work can perturb the timings, resulting in poor optimization." A timing cache stores the winners so that a rebuild picks the same tactics, but the cache is itself specific to the device, the CUDA and TensorRT versions, and the builder settings. Across major versions, only TensorRT 10 version-compatible engines run on a TensorRT 11 runtime; a TensorRT 8 engine does not.

The open analogue: TVM

TVM (Chen et al. 2018, arXiv 1802.04799v3) is an open-source compiler that does the same two jobs in a different way. For fusion it sorts operators into four categories: injective (one-to-one, such as add), reduction (such as sum), complex-out-fusable (such as conv2d, which can absorb element-wise operations on its output) and opaque (such as sort, which cannot fuse). Conv+ReLU in TensorRT is the complex-out-fusable rule. On an NVIDIA Titan X, TVM's fused operators ran 1.2x to 2x faster than unfused ones (Sec. 3; the batch size is not stated). For kernel choice, TVM does not time every candidate. A learned cost model, gradient tree boosting by default, predicts which schedules will be fast, and only the promising ones are measured on the device. Unlike a library, TVM generates code for each fused operator itself, so it does not need a hand-written kernel for every combination.

On a detector

Build the engine on the same GPU model, with the same TensorRT version, that will run it, and on an otherwise idle GPU. Quantize the detector with a tool that writes Q/DQ nodes into the exported ONNX file (ONNX is the common exchange format for trained networks): per-channel for weights, per-tensor for activations, and on the residual inputs of skip connections so that the adds fuse. A calibration flow that relied on TensorRT's own calibrators has no equivalent in TensorRT 11, and TensorRT 11 also removed 16 older plugins, among them BatchedNMS_TRT, NMS_TRT and EfficientNMS_ONNX_TRT, so check the non-maximum-suppression step of an older export. The decision guide puts these steps in order.

If your pipeline uses TensorRT calibrators

TensorRT 11.0.0 removed implicit quantization: the INT8 calibrator classes (entropy, min-max, legacy) and the per-precision builder flags are gone. Quantization now means explicit Q/DQ nodes in the network, which NVIDIA's Model Optimizer can insert and calibrate. Engines are tied to a TensorRT major version: TensorRT 10 version-compatible engines run on a TensorRT 11 runtime, and TensorRT 8 engines do not. TensorRT 11 also removed 16 old plugins, including the EfficientNMS_ONNX_TRT plugin that some exported detectors use.

On a detector: the short version

For a ResNet-style detector or segmenter, INT8 post-training quantization with per-channel weights and a percentile or entropy calibrator is the first thing to try, and in the sourced rows it stays within a few tenths of a point. Depthwise-heavy models and 4-bit weights are where the real losses are; that is when to use equalization, sensitivity analysis, BRECQ-style PTQ or a short QAT run. For more speed after that, 2:4 sparsity with retraining and a distilled smaller backbone are the sourced options. For YOLO models and transformer detectors, no source here measures TensorRT INT8, so measure your own.

Summary

A layer's time on a GPU is set by whichever is slower: its arithmetic or its memory traffic. Four levers attack those two costs. Distillation trains a smaller student, which needs feature imitation, not only soft labels, when the student is a detector. Quantization stores numbers on a grid set by a scale and a zero-point. For convolutional detectors and segmenters, 8-bit weights per channel and 8-bit activations calibrated by percentile or entropy cost only tenths of a point in the sourced rows. Depthwise-heavy models and 4-bit weights are where post-training tools (equalization, AdaRound, BRECQ) and quantization-aware training earn their keep. Pruning only speeds up a GPU when its zeros come in a shape the hardware can skip, such as whole channels or the 2:4 pattern. The LLM-era methods (LLM.int8(), SmoothQuant, GPTQ, AWQ) fix outlier activations and weight-bound decoding, which matter for transformers more than for CNN detectors. A compiler such as TensorRT turns all of this into speed by fusing layers, timing kernels on your GPU and running the precision your network specifies. Since version 11, that precision comes from explicit Q/DQ nodes rather than built-in calibrators.
