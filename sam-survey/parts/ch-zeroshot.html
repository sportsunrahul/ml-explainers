What prompting buys

SAM was never trained on the datasets in this chapter. The authors gave it one click per object on datasets it had not seen and compared it with RITM, the strongest interactive model at one click.

Before you press Best of three masks, guess: on the datasets where SAM loses, is the right answer missing, or is SAM picking the wrong one of its three answers? The rings answer it. On every dataset, one of the three masks beats RITM. So most of SAM's losses are ranking errors on ambiguous clicks, not missing answers. For example (our reading, not the paper's), a click on a hand in an egocentric video (GTEA) could mean the hand, the arm, or the person.

Claim card: one point, 23 unseen datasets

Claim
From a single click, SAM returns a valid mask on image distributions it never trained on, better than a dedicated interactive model.
How measured
23 datasets, from microscopy and X-ray to paintings and fisheye driving. The first point sits at the center of the object (the maximum of its distance transform). The metric is mIoU against the dataset's own masks. RITM, FocalClick and SimpleClick are the baselines, at their largest public sizes. Datasets with more than 15k masks are subsampled to about 10k.
Result
SAM beats RITM on 16 of the 23, by as much as about 47 IoU. Because IoU cannot tell a wrong-but-valid mask from a bad one, the authors also asked people. Annotators rated 1000 masks per model on 7 of the datasets, from 1 (no object) to 10 (no visible errors). SAM's masks scored higher than RITM's on all seven (table below).
What would falsify it
RITM ahead on most datasets, or human raters preferring RITM's masks where IoU says SAM wins. Neither happened. At more clicks the picture changes: by 5 and 9 points the paper calls SAM "on par" with the dedicated methods, and its curves sit slightly below RITM and SimpleClick there.

Composing SAM with other parts

A promptable model is a part, not a finished system. The authors wired SAM into four other tasks with no further training, by choosing the prompts:

  • Edges. Prompt a 16x16 grid of points, take the boundaries of all masks, and thin them. On BSDS500, SAM reaches ODS .768. That beats the classic zero-shot method Canny (.600) and comes close to HED (.788), which was trained on BSDS500. It is below the newest trained model, EDETR (.840).
  • Object proposals. Prompt a 64x64 grid and keep up to 1000 masks. On LVIS v1, mask recall AR@1000 is 59.3 against 63.0 for ViTDet-H, a detector trained on LVIS. SAM is ahead on rare categories (65.8 vs 58.3).
  • Instance segmentation. Prompt SAM with ViTDet-H's boxes. Mask AP is lower than ViTDet-H's own masks: 46.5 vs 51.0 on COCO, 44.7 vs 46.6 on LVIS v1. Yet when both models were given ground-truth LVIS boxes, people rated SAM's masks higher (8.1 vs 7.9). The authors' reading: ViTDet learned the drawing habits of COCO's annotators, and AP rewards that.
  • Text to mask. Train SAM to take a CLIP image embedding of the masked object as its prompt; at test time, give it the CLIP text embedding instead, because CLIP puts both in one space. The paper calls this "exploratory and not entirely robust". SAM 3 makes text a first-class prompt (chapter 9).

Two LVIS versions appear in this chapter. The 23-dataset suite uses LVIS v0.5; proposals and instance segmentation use LVIS v1. Their numbers are not comparable.

What the authors say it cannot do

The limitations section (SAM v1, Sec. 8) is short and specific. SAM can "miss fine structures", it "hallucinates small disconnected components" at times, and it "does not produce boundaries as crisply" as slower methods that zoom in. Prompts run in real time, but the whole model is "not real-time when using a heavy image encoder". And it is "unclear how to design simple prompts that implement semantic and panoptic segmentation": SAM finds things, but it does not name them. Each of these became a later paper: HQ-SAM for boundaries, the efficient encoders for speed (chapter 11), SAM 3 for names (chapter 9). The next gap the authors left was time.
