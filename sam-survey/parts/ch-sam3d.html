SAM 3D: from a mask to an object

A mask tells you which pixels belong to an object. SAM 3D (submitted 2025-11-20, revised 2026-06-02) takes one image and one such mask and generates the whole object in 3D: its shape, including the hidden back, its texture, and where it sits in the scene.

SAM 3D is not part of SAM. It does not make masks; it assumes one, and its authors point to the SAM series as a fast way to get it. It belongs on this page for a different reason: it copies SAM's recipe for a field that had no data.

The model. A DINOv2 encoder turns the object crop, its mask, the full image and the full mask into 4 sets of conditioning tokens. A 1.2B-parameter geometry model, a flow-matching transformer, generates the coarse shape as 4096 latent tokens (a 64^3 voxel grid) and the layout as 1 more token: a rotation in 6D form, a translation and a scale per axis. A 600M-parameter model then refines the shape and adds texture, and decoders output a mesh or Gaussian splats. Predict before you switch tabs: which of the three outputs (shape, rotation, placement) is hardest to learn from one photo?

The geometry model learns all its outputs with one flow-matching loss: start from noise, and learn the velocity that moves it in a straight line to the true shape and layout.

\mathcal{L}_{\text{CFM}} = \htmlData{step=1, link=shape}{\sum_{m \in \mathcal{M}} \lambda_m} \, \mathbb{E}_{\tau, \mathbf{x}_0^m}\Big[ \big\| \htmlData{step=2, link=layout}{\mathbf{v}^m} - \htmlData{step=3, link=mask}{\mathbf{v}_\theta^m(\mathbf{x}_\tau^m, \htmlClass{t-green}{c}, \tau)} \big\|^2 \Big]

SAM 3D v2, Eq. 1 (App. C.2), with \mathbf{x}_\tau^m = \tau\mathbf{x}_1^m + (1-\tau)\mathbf{x}_0^m and \mathbf{v}^m = \mathbf{x}_1^m - \mathbf{x}_0^m.

  • m \in \mathcal{M}: the outputs, shape, rotation, translation and scale, each with a weight \lambda_m (rotation and scale get 0.1).
  • \mathbf{x}_0^m: noise; \mathbf{x}_1^m: the true value; \mathbf{x}_\tau^m: a point on the straight line between them at time \tau.
  • \mathbf{v}^m: the velocity along that line, the target; \mathbf{v}_\theta^m: the network's guess, conditioned on \htmlClass{t-green}{c}, the image and the mask.

The data engine, again

Nobody has millions of photos paired with true 3D models, and most people cannot build a 3D mesh. They can, however, pick the best of several candidates. SAM 3D's engine asks for exactly that. Annotators choose target objects (10 seconds per object, with SAM helping to segment), pick the best of several candidate shapes (N=8 in the method section, 6 to 10 in the annotation statistics; 80 seconds), and align the winner to a point cloud of the scene (150 seconds). Professional artists model the hardest failures. The losing candidates become negative examples for preference training.

As in SAM's engine, the model takes over the work: the best SAM 3D model eventually produced about 80% of the annotated data. The engine annotated almost 1 million images with 3.14 million untextured meshes and collected 7 million pairwise preferences.

The training follows a language-model recipe: pretraining on 2.7 million rendered synthetic meshes, mid-training on 61 million semi-synthetic samples (rendered objects pasted or swapped into real images, plus synthetic indoor scenes), then supervised fine-tuning and preference optimisation (DPO) on the engine's real data. Each step helps, measured as shape F1 on the new SA-3DAO benchmark of 1,000 artist-made meshes paired with natural images:

Limits (App. F): the 64^3 grid distorts thin structures; objects are predicted one at a time, with no reasoning about contact or stability; and texture is predicted without knowing the pose, so symmetric objects can get a rotated texture. Mask errors from the segmenter carry through, though the paper reports fair robustness to over- and under-segmentation.
