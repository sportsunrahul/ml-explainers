One space for pictures and words

How OpenAI's CLIP learned to connect images and language from 400 million captioned pictures on the internet, why that lets it recognise things it was never explicitly trained on, and what came after it.

The idea

Before CLIP, most vision models learned from a fixed list of labels, such as ImageNet's 1,000 classes. That makes them good at those classes and nothing else: to recognise a new category, you need new labelled data and retraining.

CLIP (Contrastive Language–Image Pre-training) learns from natural language instead. The internet is full of images with captions or alt text. CLIP trains an image encoder and a text encoder together so that an image and its caption map to nearby points in one shared embedding space, while unrelated images and captions are pushed apart.

The payoff is flexibility. Once images and text share a space, any sentence becomes a classifier. To ask “is this a photo of a pangolin?”, you embed that sentence and measure how close the image is to it. No pangolin-specific training is needed. This is called zero-shot classification.

The data

OpenAI built a dataset called WIT (WebImageText) of 400 million image–text pairs collected from the public internet. To cover a broad range of visual concepts, they searched for pairs whose text contained one of about 500,000 queries (built from frequent Wikipedia words and phrases, WordNet synonyms and more) and capped each query at roughly 20,000 pairs so no topic dominated. The text is noisy: alt text, titles and captions rather than careful descriptions. The dataset itself was never released, which later motivated open replications such as LAION.

Architecture

CLIP has two separate towers that never share weights. They only meet at the very end, where a dot product compares their outputs.

Image encoder

The paper trained two families. ResNets (ResNet-50, 101, and wider versions RN50x4, RN50x16, RN50x64 scaled in the EfficientNet style) with a few changes: anti-aliased pooling and an attention pooling layer instead of global average pooling. And Vision Transformers: ViT-B/32, ViT-B/16 and ViT-L/14, with an extra layer norm before the transformer. The best model, ViT-L/14@336px, is a ViT-L/14 trained for one more epoch at 336-pixel resolution.

Text encoder

A 12-layer, 512-wide transformer with 8 attention heads (about 63M parameters), using masked (causal) self-attention. Text is lower-cased and tokenised with byte-pair encoding (49,152-token vocabulary), capped at 76 tokens, and wrapped in start and end tokens. The activation at the end token of the final layer is the text feature.

Shared space

Two linear projections, Wi and Wt, map each tower's feature into the same embedding size, followed by ℓ2 normalisation. There is no non-linear projection head; the paper found a linear map was enough.

The contrastive loss

Take a batch of N image–caption pairs. Embed all N images and all N captions, and compute every image–caption cosine similarity. That gives an N×N matrix. The N entries on the diagonal are the true pairs; the other N² − N are mismatches. Training makes each diagonal entry the largest in both its row and its column.

Concretely, each row is treated as an N-way classification problem, “which caption belongs to this image?”, and each column as “which image belongs to this caption?”. The loss is the average cross-entropy of both directions, a symmetric form of the InfoNCE loss.

sij = ⟨ Ii, Tj ⟩ / τ   (I, T unit vectors)
L = ½ [ −(1/N) Σi log softmaxj(sij)j=i  −  (1/N) Σj log softmaxi(sij)i=j ] τ is a learned temperature, initialised at 0.07 and clipped so logits are never scaled by more than 100. The batch size was 32,768, so each positive competes with 32,767 negatives per direction.

What the gradient actually does

For one image, the loss pulls its embedding toward its own caption and pushes it away from every other caption in the batch. The push from each wrong caption is weighted by how probable the model currently thinks that caption is. Confusable negatives push hardest, easy ones barely matter. Below, the six pairs start in random positions and are trained with the real symmetric loss.

The training loop in code

# images: (N, 3, H, W)   texts: (N, 77) token ids
img = F.normalize(image_encoder(images) @ W_i, dim=-1)   # (N, d)
txt = F.normalize(text_encoder(texts)  @ W_t, dim=-1)    # (N, d)
logits = img @ txt.T * logit_scale.exp()                 # (N, N), scale = 1/τ
labels = torch.arange(N)
loss = (F.cross_entropy(logits, labels) +                # image → text
        F.cross_entropy(logits.T, labels)) / 2           # text → image

Why contrastive, not captioning?

The authors first tried to predict the exact caption of each image, like an image-captioning model. That was slow to learn, because there are countless valid ways to describe a picture. Predicting a bag of words was about 3× more efficient, and switching to the contrastive task, which only asks which caption goes with the image, was another 4× more efficient. Discriminating is far easier than generating, and it is all zero-shot transfer needs.

Training details

All models were trained from scratch (no ImageNet initialisation) for 32 epochs with the Adam optimiser, decoupled weight decay and a cosine learning-rate schedule, in mixed precision. The largest ResNet (RN50x64) took 18 days on 592 V100 GPUs; the largest ViT took 12 days on 256 V100 GPUs. Data augmentation was minimal: only random square crops.

Zero-shot classification

To classify an image into K classes, write one text prompt per class, embed them all with the text encoder, and pick the class whose embedding is closest to the image's. The text embeddings act as the weights of a linear classifier that was generated from words rather than learned from labelled examples.

Wording matters. A bare label like “boxer” is ambiguous (a dog or an athlete?) and looks nothing like a typical caption. The paper found the template “a photo of a {label}.” alone improved ImageNet accuracy by about 1.3 points, and averaging the embeddings of 80 different templates (“a blurry photo of a {label}”, “a drawing of a {label}” and so on), called prompt ensembling, added more, for roughly 5 points in total.

Results

Zero-shot: without seeing a single ImageNet training label, ViT-L/14@336px reached 76.2% top-1 accuracy, matching the original supervised ResNet-50. Across 27 datasets, zero-shot CLIP beat a fully supervised linear classifier on ResNet-50 features on 16 of them.

Linear probe: with a linear classifier trained on its frozen features, the best CLIP model outperformed the best ImageNet-trained models of the time on most of the 27 datasets, reaching about 85.4% on ImageNet itself.

Robustness: the most striking result. Standard ImageNet models lose much of their accuracy on shifted versions such as sketches, renditions and adversarially filtered images. Zero-shot CLIP closed up to 75% of that robustness gap, because it never overfit to ImageNet's particular photo style.

Strengths and limits

One subtle property is worth seeing. CLIP's image and text embeddings do not actually mix: they occupy two separate regions of the sphere, a phenomenon called the modality gap (Liang et al., 2022). It comes partly from each encoder starting in its own narrow cone at initialisation and partly from the contrastive loss with a low temperature, which only needs relative ordering and never forces the two clouds to overlap.

Strengths

  • Zero-shot classification from any list of class names, in plain language.
  • Far more robust to distribution shift than models trained on a fixed labelled dataset.
  • One embedding space serves image search, text search, deduplication, filtering and clustering.
  • Scales predictably with data and compute, and its encoders became standard building blocks for text-to-image and multimodal models.
  • Simple, stable objective with no labels and no captioning decoder.

Weaknesses

  • Weak at counting, spatial relations and fine-grained categories (car models, flower species); it only got about 88% on handwritten digits (MNIST).
  • Behaves somewhat like a bag of words: “a horse riding an astronaut” and “an astronaut riding a horse” embed similarly.
  • Vulnerable to typographic attacks: writing “iPod” on an apple can make it classify the apple as an iPod.
  • Inherits social biases from unfiltered web text, which can surface in harmful classifications.
  • Needs very large batches and compute; the training data was never released.
  • Global embeddings only, so it is weak at dense tasks like segmentation without extra work.

Improvements after CLIP

CLIP started a family. Later work improved it along four axes: more or cleaner data, a better loss, cheaper training, and added generative objectives.

ModelYearAxisWhat changed
ALIGN2021Data1.8B noisy alt-text pairs with minimal filtering, EfficientNet and BERT towers. Showed that scale can compensate for noise.
LiT2021TrainingLocks a strong pretrained image encoder and trains only the text tower to match it. Cheaper and better zero-shot accuracy.
CoCa2022GenerativeAdds a captioning decoder next to the contrastive loss, so one model both retrieves and describes.
OpenCLIP + LAION2022DataOpen reproductions on LAION-400M and LAION-5B, with published scaling laws. Made CLIP-style research reproducible.
FLIP2022TrainingRandomly drops 50 to 75% of image patches during training, speeding it up several times with little accuracy cost.
SigLIP2023LossReplaces the softmax with a pairwise sigmoid loss. No batch-wide normalisation, so it works well at smaller batches and scales more easily.
EVA-CLIP2023TrainingInitialises from a masked-image-modelling encoder and uses optimisation tricks for better accuracy per unit of compute.
MetaCLIP2023DataReconstructs and publishes CLIP's metadata-based curation, balancing counts per concept. Beats CLIP with the same data size.
DFN2023DataTrains a “data filtering network” to score pairs, then keeps the best. Filtering quality matters more than raw volume.

Where CLIP ended up

CLIP's encoders became infrastructure. Its text encoder conditions Stable Diffusion; DALL·E 2 generates images from CLIP embeddings; CLIP-style vision towers feed many multimodal chat models such as LLaVA; open-vocabulary detectors like OWL-ViT build on it; and CLIPScore is a common metric for how well an image matches a caption.

Which one should you use?

For zero-shot classification or retrieval today, a SigLIP or SigLIP 2 model, or a large OpenCLIP model trained on curated data, is usually more accurate than the original CLIP. For compatibility with tools built on the original (older Stable Diffusion versions, CLIPScore), use OpenAI's ViT-L/14. For dense tasks like segmentation, pair a CLIP-style model with a self-supervised backbone such as DINOv2 or DINOv3, or use SigLIP 2, which was trained with localisation in mind.

import torch, open_clip
model, _, preprocess = open_clip.create_model_and_transforms("ViT-B-32", pretrained="openai")
