SimCLR: strong augmentation, a big batch, a throwaway head

MoCo built a queue and a momentum encoder to get many negatives. SimCLR asks whether you need any of that. Its answer is no, if you get three plain choices right. The most important choice is how you make the two views.

Make a guess before you press "2nd: Color". The figure starts with crop alone. Which do you think trains better features: crop alone, or crop followed by color distortion? And what could the network learn from crops alone that would not help it later?

Lesson 1: crop alone lets the network cheat

Look at the histograms under the two views when only crop is on. Two crops of one photo share almost the same colors: the same beige wall, the same wooden table, the same mug. A crop of a different photo has different colors. So the network can find the partner by comparing color statistics alone, without ever learning what a mug looks like. That is a shortcut: a cheap feature that solves the training game and is useless afterwards.

Now press "2nd: Color". The partner's colors shift, and the green overlap shrinks. Color can no longer identify the partner, so the network must use what color distortion leaves alone: edges, shapes, the layout of the scene. The table shows the effect: crop alone gives 33.1, crop then color gives 56.3, and color then crop gives 55.8. The paper makes the same argument: color histograms alone suffice to tell its crops apart, so it composes cropping with color distortion.

You might expect each transformation to be good on its own. In fact the diagonal is weak everywhere: color alone gives 18.8 and blur alone 2.6. A good task needs views that differ in many ways at once, so that only the content of the photo connects them.

Check yourself: you train with crop only on product photos where every product has its own color. The training loss drops fast. Why might the features still be poor?

The loss can be solved by color matching: each product's crops share a color, and other products' crops do not. The encoder learns color statistics, not shapes, so a classifier on top of it has little to work with. Add color distortion (and grey-scale views) so that color no longer identifies the partner.

Lesson 2: the other views in the batch are the negatives

Now the loss. Take a batch of N photos and make two views of each, so you have 2N views. Pick one view as the anchor. Its partner is the positive. Every one of the other 2(N-1) views comes from a different photo, so each of them is a negative. There is no memory bank and no queue: the batch is the lineup.

Careful: N now counts photos, not candidates. Each row's lineup has 2N - 1 candidates.

Why does this work without MoCo's tricks? Every negative is computed in this step, by the encoder as it is now. A memory bank holds embeddings from earlier versions of the encoder, so they are stale; MoCo's momentum encoder keeps its queue consistent by changing slowly. In-batch negatives are never stale, so neither fix is needed. The price is the batch size: to see many negatives, you need a large batch, and that is the second ingredient.

The figure below draws the whole loss for a tiny batch. On the left, the views sit on the unit circle (each embedding scaled to length 1). On the right is the table of cosine similarities between every pair of views, with views 2k-1 and 2k from photo k, as in the paper.

You might expect the diagonal to be the positive, as in a table of images against captions. Here it is not: the diagonal compares a view with itself, so its cosine is always 1 and it teaches nothing. The loss removes it. The positive sits next to the diagonal, at the partner's column.

The same idea as an equation

Read one row of the table as one softmax: "of all the other views, which is my partner?". SimCLR calls this loss NT-Xent, the normalized temperature-scaled cross-entropy.

\ell_{i,j} = -\log \frac{\htmlData{step=1, link=pos}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{\exp(\htmlData{step=2, link=sim}{\mathrm{sim}(\bm z_i, \bm z_j)}/\htmlData{step=4, link=tau}{\tau})}}}{\htmlData{step=3, link=row}{\htmlClass{t-red}{\sum_{k=1}^{2N}}} \htmlData{step=5, link=mask}{\mathbb{1}_{[k \neq i]}}\htmlClass{t-red}{\exp(\mathrm{sim}(\bm z_i, \bm z_k)/\tau)}}

Eq. 1 in Chen, Kornblith, Norouzi and Hinton, "A Simple Framework for Contrastive Learning of Visual Representations", arXiv 2002.05709v3 (the indicator is written there with a blackboard-bold 1, macro \one).

  • \bm z_i, \bm z_j: the head's outputs for two views of the same photo. i is the anchor's row; j is the partner's column.
  • \mathrm{sim}(\bm u, \bm v) = \bm u^\top \bm v / (\lVert \bm u\rVert \lVert \bm v\rVert): the cosine similarity, one cell of the table. Dividing by the lengths is the "normalized" in NT-Xent.
  • \tau: the temperature. Dividing by a small \tau stretches the gaps between cosines before the softmax.
  • \htmlClass{t-red}{\sum_{k=1}^{2N}}: a sum over the whole row, all 2N views.
  • \mathbb{1}_{[k \neq i]}: 1 for every column except k = i, so the diagonal cell drops out. The row keeps the partner and the 2(N-1) negatives.

Try it with the numbers in the figure: N = 2 and \tau = 0.5 (our choice for the arithmetic; the paper does not give a default). Row 1 has cosines 0.8 with its partner, 0 and -0.6 with the two negatives.

  • Divide by \tau: 1.6,\ 0,\ -1.2.
  • Exponentiate: e^{1.6} \approx 4.95,\ e^{0} = 1,\ e^{-1.2} \approx 0.30. The row sum is 6.25.
  • The partner's probability is 4.95 / 6.25 \approx 0.79, so \ell_{1,2} = -\log 0.79 \approx 0.23.
  • At \tau = 1 the same row gives e^{0.8} / (e^{0.8} + 1 + e^{-0.6}) \approx 0.59 and a loss of about 0.53. Press the temperature buttons to see every row change.

Each row is one loss. The batch loss averages all of them, both directions of every pair:

\mathcal{L} = \htmlData{step=1, link=rows}{\frac{1}{2N} \sum_{k=1}^N} \left[ \htmlData{step=2, link=pos}{\htmlClass{t-red}{\ell(2k\!-\!1, 2k)}} + \htmlData{step=3, link=pos}{\htmlClass{t-red}{\ell(2k, 2k\!-\!1)}}\right]

Algorithm 1 in Chen et al., arXiv 2002.05709v3 (unnumbered there).

For our tiny batch at \tau = 0.5, the four row losses average to the "mean over all rows" printed under the table. Now press N = 4. Every row goes from 2 negatives to 6, and the anchor's loss rises, because there are more strangers who might look like the partner. At N = 4096, SimCLR's default batch, each row has 8190 negatives (our arithmetic, 2(N-1)). The paper itself gives 16382 negatives per positive pair at batch 8192.

What about \tau? The paper does not state one default. In its sweep (Table 5, 100 epochs, with the embeddings normalized), \tau = 0.1 gave the best linear-probe top-1, 64.4, against 59.7 at 0.05 and 58.0 at 1. Why a small temperature helps, and why too small hurts, is the subject of chapter 6.

Lesson 3: a head you train and then throw away

From here on, the embedding is what the loss sees (\bm z), and the representation is the encoder output you keep (\bm h). Before this chapter there was no head, so the two were the same vector.

The loss wants the two views to land on the same point. To do that, the network must remove everything the augmentation changed: the color shift, the crop position, the blur. That is what we asked for, but it removes too much. Color can matter for the next task: a ripe banana and a green one differ only in color.

SimCLR's fix is a small extra network, the projection head g, between the encoder and the loss. The encoder produces \bm h; the head turns it into \bm z = g(\bm h); the loss sees only \bm z. Now the head can do the forgetting. It learns to throw away color and orientation, so \bm z is invariant, while \bm h, one layer earlier, is free to keep them. After pretraining you delete g and keep \bm h.

Think of \bm h as your full notes and g as a cheat sheet written for one exam. The exam only asks "same photo or not?", so the cheat sheet leaves out the colors. For the next exam, you bring the notes.

The paper measures both halves of this story. A nonlinear head (one hidden layer with a ReLU) beats a linear head by 3 points, and beats no head by more than 10 points. Even with the nonlinear head, \bm h beats \bm z by more than 10 points under a linear probe (Sec. 4.2; the bars in its Fig. 8 carry no printed values). Check yourself: the loss is computed on z, so z is what training optimized. Why is it the worse representation?

Because the loss rewards invariance to the augmentations, and z is where that invariance is enforced. Anything the augmentations change, such as color or orientation, is information z is pushed to discard. The layer before the head is only asked to make that job possible, so it can keep more of the photo. A downstream task that needs color or orientation finds them in h, not in z.

Bigger batches and longer training

More negatives per step make the lineup harder, and a harder lineup teaches more. The paper's Table B.1 measures this directly: ResNet-50, linear probe, learning rate scaled linearly with the batch.

At 800 epochs the same change gains less than three, because training longer also shows the model more negatives over time. The paper frames this as contrastive learning benefiting more than supervised learning from big batches and long training, and explains it by the negatives. Its supervised check covers only the length of training (a supervised ResNet does not improve when trained longer, App. B.3); it has no supervised batch-size sweep.

Where this came from

SimCLR is by Chen, Kornblith, Norouzi and Hinton (2002.05709, submitted 2020-02-13). In-batch negatives were not new; the N-pair loss in chapter 1 already used them. The contribution was a careful ablation that found which simple parts matter, with no special architecture and no memory bank. Its ResNet-50 reaches 69.3 top-1 on ImageNet under a linear probe after 1000 epochs of pretraining, and a four-times-wider ResNet-50 reaches 76.5 under the same protocol.

What came next

InfoMin (Tian et al., 2005.10243) explains Lesson 1: good views share only the information the downstream task needs, so you reduce what the two views have in common but keep what matters for the label. Too little shared information makes the task impossible; too much lets shortcuts like color matching in.

SimCLRv2 (2006.10029) scaled the same recipe to bigger networks and used it to learn from a small fraction of labels. MoCo v2 borrowed SimCLR's MLP head and blur and kept its queue, so it needs no huge batch (chapter 4).

Where we go next

You now have a full recipe. But why should "pull the pair, push the rest" produce good features at all, and what does the temperature really control? Chapter 6 answers this on the sphere. First, the interlude runs one complete SimCLR step at full size.

You should now be able to explain

  • Why crop alone lets the network match color histograms, and why adding color distortion removes that shortcut.
  • How the other 2(N-1) views in a batch serve as negatives, why they are never stale, and what the masked diagonal is.
  • Why a projection head used only for the loss leaves the layer before it more useful, and why it is thrown away.
  • Why SimCLR needs a large batch, and what that costs.

In practice

Start from the augmentations, not the loss. Ask what your downstream task must ignore and what it must keep: if color matters (ripe or unripe, rust or paint), weaken color distortion or keep a head and use the features before it. Always evaluate the encoder output \bm h, not the head output. If you cannot afford a batch of thousands, use a queue (chapter 4) instead of shrinking the lineup. With data-parallel training, make sure batch normalization statistics are shared across devices, or the model may cheat.
