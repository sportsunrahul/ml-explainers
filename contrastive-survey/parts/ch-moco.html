Act IIThe recipes

Negatives at scale: a memory bank, then a momentum queue

The lineup from chapter 3 teaches more when it holds more strangers. So where do you find tens of thousands of negatives for every query, when a GPU holds only a few hundred images at once?

This chapter follows two answers. The first stores old embeddings and finds that they go stale. The second, MoCo, keeps a queue of recent ones and makes them from a slowly moving copy of the encoder.

The problem: a lineup needs many strangers

Recall the game. The network encodes one view of an image; call that vector the query. It also encodes a second view of the same image; call that the positive key. Then a softmax must pick the positive key out of a row of negative keys, the embeddings of other images. The query is chapter 3's anchor. The keys are its candidates: one positive key and many negative keys.

Why want many negatives? With five strangers in the lineup, they are probably all easy to tell apart from your partner. With fifty thousand, a few will look a lot like it: another mug, the same table. To pick the right one anyway, the encoder has to notice finer details. A harder lineup trains better features.

The obvious source of negatives is the rest of the batch: the other images you just encoded. The MoCo paper calls this end-to-end, because the gradient flows through every key. But then the number of negatives is tied to the batch size, and the batch is tied to GPU memory. On the authors' GPUs, the largest batch that fit was 1024 images. The MoCo v2 authors estimate that a batch of 4096 would need 93.0G of memory per GPU.

First fix: a memory bank

Wu et al. (2018) asked a different question: why compute negatives at all? Keep one stored embedding for every image in the dataset, in a table they call the memory bank. For ImageNet that is 1.28M vectors of 128 numbers, about 600 MB. Each step, draw negatives from the table instead of encoding them; Wu drew 4,096 per image. (Wu's paper calls this count m; it is unrelated to the momentum m below.)

The catch is in how the table is filled. An image's entry is rewritten only when that image comes round in training again, once per epoch. At a batch of 256, an epoch is about 5000 steps. So the negatives in one lineup were made by up to that many different versions of the encoder, some fresh and some an epoch old.

Picture trying to recognise a friend from today's photo by comparing it with a gallery of portraits. Each portrait was painted by the same artist, but years apart, and the artist's style kept changing. Two portraits that look different may differ only in when they were painted. Stale keys are like that: their differences mix up "different image" with "different encoder". The MoCo authors call such keys inconsistent.

MoCo's fix: a queue of recent keys from a slow copy

MoCo (Momentum Contrast) keeps two ideas apart. A queue holds the keys of the last few batches: each step, the new batch's keys go in at one end and the oldest batch falls out at the other. The queue length K no longer depends on the batch size. Because every key is at most a few hundred steps old, it is far fresher than a bank entry.

The second idea decides which network makes the keys. You cannot send gradients into the keys in the queue: the computation that made them finished many steps ago. So the keys come from a separate key encoder that gets no gradient at all. It is a slowly moving copy of the trained query encoder. Each step it moves a small fraction of the way toward the query encoder's current weights. The fraction is 1 - m, where m is the momentum (not the margin m of chapter 1).

The figure below makes this concrete in a toy. Imagine an encoder's millions of weights squeezed onto a plane. The query encoder (red ring: it is trained) jumps around with each noisy gradient step. The key encoder chases it. Every brown dot is the key encoder as it was when one batch now in the queue was encoded. The queue holds 16 batches, as a 4096-key queue does at batch 256.

Make a guess before you press "0". With m = 0, the key encoder becomes an exact copy of the query encoder at every step. The keys are then as fresh as they can be. Will training work better or worse?

Press it. The brown dots scatter as widely as the query encoder's own jumps, because each batch in the queue was encoded by a different network. The paper reports that at m = 0 "the training loss oscillates and fails to converge". Fresh is not enough: the keys must also agree with each other.

Now step through the chips. At m = 0.9 the cloud shrinks, and the sourced accuracy is 55.2. At 0.99 and 0.999 the queue's keys come from almost the same network, and accuracy peaks at 59.0. At 0.9999 the cloud is even tighter, but the key encoder now trails far behind the query encoder, and accuracy dips slightly, to 58.9. The paper does not test why; the lag in the toy is one possible reason (our reading).

The same idea as an equation: the loss

The loss is the lineup from chapter 3. Only the source of the strangers changes: they are the keys in the queue.

\htmlData{step=1, link=query}{\mathcal{L}_q} = -\log \frac{\exp(\htmlData{step=2, link=pos}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{q}{\cdot}\htmlClass{t-yellow}{k_+}} / \tau)}{\htmlData{step=3, link=queue}{\sum_{i=0}^{K}\exp(\htmlClass{t-blue}{q}{\cdot}\htmlClass{t-red}{k_i} / \tau)}}

Eq. 1 in He et al., "Momentum contrast for unsupervised visual representation learning", arXiv 1911.05722v3.

  • \htmlClass{t-blue}{q}: the query, the query encoder's output for one view. A 128-number vector scaled to length 1.
  • \htmlClass{t-yellow}{k_+}: the positive key, the key encoder's output for the other view of the same image.
  • \htmlClass{t-red}{k_i}, i = 0 \dots K: the K + 1 candidates, the positive key plus K negatives from the queue. In the first MoCo the negatives are the queue only, never the other images of the current batch.
  • q \cdot k: the dot product, which is the cosine similarity because both vectors have length 1.
  • \tau: the temperature, which sharpens the softmax. MoCo uses \tau = 0.07.
  • \mathcal{L}_q: the cross-entropy of a (K+1)-way softmax whose correct answer is the positive key.

Try it with numbers, at \tau = 0.07 and three negatives. Say q \cdot k_+ = 0.8. Divide every term by the positive's term, so each negative contributes \exp((q \cdot k_i - 0.8)/0.07).

  • Easy negatives at 0.3, 0.1 and -0.2 contribute e^{-7.1} \approx 0.0008 and less. The loss is \log(1 + 0.0008 + \dots) \approx 0.0008: almost nothing to learn.
  • Swap the last one for a look-alike at 0.75. It contributes e^{-0.05/0.07} = e^{-0.71} \approx 0.49, and the loss jumps to \log(1.49) \approx 0.40.

One look-alike carries almost the whole loss. With a small temperature, only near-misses teach. That is why a big lineup helps: the more strangers you draw, the more likely one of them is a near-miss.

The momentum update, and why it keeps keys consistent

Here is the rule that moves the key encoder. Read it as "keep most of where you are, and take a small step toward the query encoder".

\htmlData{step=1, link=key}{\htmlClass{t-brown}{\theta_\textrm{k}}} \leftarrow \htmlData{step=2, link=momentum}{m \htmlClass{t-brown}{\theta_\textrm{k}}} + \htmlData{step=3, link=query}{(1 - m) \htmlClass{t-blue}{\theta_\textrm{q}}}.

Eq. 2 in He et al., arXiv 1911.05722v3.

  • \htmlClass{t-brown}{\theta_\textrm{k}}: all the weights of the key encoder. No gradient ever reaches them.
  • \htmlClass{t-blue}{\theta_\textrm{q}}: all the weights of the query encoder. "Only the parameters \theta_\textrm{q} are updated by back-propagation."
  • m: the momentum, between 0 and 1. MoCo's default is 0.999.

Try it with one weight. Say the query encoder's weight is 1.0 and the key encoder's is 0, with m = 0.999.

  • After one step, the key weight is 0.999 \cdot 0 + 0.001 \cdot 1.0 = 0.001. It closed 0.1\% of the gap.
  • If the query weight stayed put, the remaining gap would shrink by a factor 0.999 per step. It halves after \ln 2 / 0.001 \approx 693 steps.
  • The oldest key in a queue of K = 65536 is 65536 / 256 = 256 steps old. Over those steps the key encoder closes at most 23% of its gap. The queue's keys were made by nearly the same network.
  • Compare the memory bank: an entry can be 5000 steps old. By then a key encoder with m = 0.999 would have only 0.7% of its gap left: a different network.

You might expect an encoder that moves a thousand times slower to learn a thousand times slower. It does not, because it follows the query encoder with a delay, not at a reduced speed. Each gradient step is noisy and jumps around. The key encoder averages roughly the last thousand of those jumps, so the noise cancels and the steady progress remains. Press "Run 500 steps" at m = 0.999: the key encoder glides along behind the query encoder's trail while the query encoder jitters.

Does it pay off?

The MoCo paper trained the same ResNet-50 three ways and changed only where the negatives come from. The chart below plots the results against the number of negatives K.

Three things to read off. Every curve rises with K: more strangers, better features. End-to-end and MoCo agree where both exist, so the queue costs nothing in quality at small K. And the memory bank sits below MoCo all the way, by 2.6 points at the largest K. That gap is the price of stale keys.

Check yourself: why can't MoCo just backpropagate into the key encoder, like it does into the query encoder?

The loss depends on every key in the queue, so a true gradient for the key encoder would have to flow into all of them. But most of those keys were computed in earlier steps, and their computation is gone. You could only backpropagate into the current batch's keys, which would make the key encoder a second trained network, changing fast, and the queue inconsistent again. MoCo instead gives the key encoder no gradient and moves it with Eq. 2.

Check yourself: what would m = 1 do?

The key encoder would never move from its starting weights. Its keys would be perfectly consistent, but they would come from a random network forever, and the query encoder would learn to match a random network's outputs. This is our reasoning; the paper's ablation stops at 0.9999.

Why shuffle batch norm?

Batch normalization (BN) rescales each feature using statistics of the samples on the same GPU. Without care, a query and its positive key are normalized with statistics from the same group of samples. That shared statistic is a "signature" the network can use to find its partner without looking at the content. Without the fix, the accuracy on the training game (picking the partner) climbs above 99.9% while the features get worse. MoCo shuffles the batch order before it splits the key batch across GPUs, and shuffles back after. Query and positive key then see different statistics.

Where this came from

Wu, Xiong, Yu and Lin (1805.01978, 2018) introduced instance discrimination: treat every image as its own class, and keep the memory bank so the softmax over all images is affordable. Their ResNet-50 reached 54.0 top-1 on ImageNet, measured with a different linear classifier on an earlier layer.

He, Fan, Wu, Xie and Girshick (1911.05722, 2019) replaced the bank with the queue and the momentum encoder. MoCo's ResNet-50 reached 60.6 linear-probe top-1 after 200 epochs. (A linear probe, as in chapter 3, trains one classifier layer on frozen features.) Because the two papers measured differently, the two numbers are not a controlled comparison; the chart above is.

What changed next: MoCo v2 and v3

MoCo v2 (2003.04297) kept the queue and the small batch, and took two things from SimCLR: a two-layer MLP head (a projection head, explained in chapter 5) and blur augmentation. It also added a cosine learning-rate schedule and raised the temperature. The table below lists what each change bought.

MoCo v3 (2104.02057) moved to Vision Transformers (ViT) and teaches two lessons.

The first: with a big enough batch, the queue is unnecessary, but the momentum encoder still helps. With a batch of 4096, the batch already holds enough negatives, so v3 drops the queue: each query's negatives are the other images' keys in the batch. You might expect m = 0 to fail, as it did in v1. It does not, because all keys now come from the current network and are consistent anyway (our reading; the paper notes that m = 0 is then close to SimCLR). Yet a momentum encoder with m = 0.99 still adds 2.2 points. v3 also adds a prediction head on the query side, as BYOL does (chapter 8).

The second: training ViTs this way is quietly unstable. It does not diverge; it loses a few points, and you may not notice. Gradient spikes appeared first in the patch projection, the ViT's first layer, which turns image patches into vectors. The fix is to freeze that layer at its random initialization, so it never changes.

Where we go next: no queue, just a huge batch

MoCo got many negatives cheaply by keeping old keys. SimCLR (chapter 5) took the opposite route: no queue and no momentum encoder, just a batch of thousands, with everything trained end to end. It also found which augmentations matter, and why a throwaway head helps.

You should now be able to explain

  • Why more negatives help, and why the batch alone cannot supply them.
  • Why memory-bank keys are inconsistent, and how a queue plus a momentum encoder fixes that.
  • What Eq. 2 does with m = 0.999: a 0.1\% step per update, about 693 steps to close half a gap.
  • Which parts of MoCo get gradients, and which change by momentum or enqueueing instead.

In practice

If your GPU memory limits the batch, a momentum queue gives you many negatives cheaply. In the MoCo v2 measurements, MoCo at batch 256 used 5.0G per GPU against 7.4G for end-to-end. With very large batches, as in MoCo v3, the queue adds little, but a momentum encoder still helps. Treat m as a delay in steps, about 1/(1-m), so about 1000 steps at m = 0.999, as in the worked example above: a short training run needs a smaller m (our reading).
