Image and text: CLIP's softmax, SigLIP's sigmoid

A label says "these photos belong together". A caption says much more: "a brown dog catching a frisbee on a beach". What if the partner of a photo is not a second crop but the sentence written under it? This chapter answers three questions. How does the lineup loss turn into CLIP? Why can CLIP then classify images it was never trained to label? And how did SigLIP make the same idea cheaper?

The game: a table of photos and captions

Collect pairs from the web: a photo and the text next to it. Put N pairs in a batch. Send the photos through an image encoder and the captions through a text encoder; both end in unit vectors of the same length, so you can take the cosine of any photo with any caption. That gives an N \times N table. Row i is photo i, column j is caption j, and the true pairs sit on the diagonal. Every other cell is a mismatched pair, a negative.

The figure is such a table for eight made-up pairs. The photos are blue row labels, the captions yellow column labels. We built the embeddings so that each photo has one look-alike caption: dog and "cat", car and "truck", and so on.

Before you press anything, read the raw cosines. Each photo is closest to its own caption, but the dog photo also scores well against "cat". Make a guess before you press "Softmax over the row". The dog row has cosine 0.91 for "dog" and 0.46 for "cat". What probability will the softmax give "cat"?

Press it. "Cat" gets about one percent. The softmax turns the row into a lineup: the probabilities must sum to 1 (see the "sum" column), so the clear winner takes almost everything. The red outline shows the catch. The number in the top-left cell depends on every score in its row.

CLIP: the lineup in both directions

CLIP (Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training) trains both encoders so that each row of the table picks out its own caption, and each column picks out its own photo. It is the lineup loss of chapter 3 run twice: once with the photo as the anchor and the captions as candidates, and once the other way round. The paper writes the loss as a few lines of code (its Figure 3). The heart of it:

logits = np.dot(I_e, T_e.T) * np.exp(t)
labels = np.arange(n)
loss_i = cross_entropy_loss(logits, labels, axis=0)
loss_t = cross_entropy_loss(logits, labels, axis=1)
loss   = (loss_i + loss_t)/2

Here I_e and T_e are the unit-length photo and caption embeddings, labels says "the answer for row i is column i", and the two cross-entropies run down the columns and along the rows. The paper prints no numbered equation for this. The SigLIP paper writes the same loss out in full, and we quote that form:

\htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{soft}}} = -\frac{1}{2|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{B}|}\left( \htmlData{step=2, link=row}{\log\frac{e^{\htmlData{step=1, link=diag}{t\,\htmlClass{t-blue}{\mathbf{x}_i}\cdot\htmlClass{t-yellow}{\mathbf{y}_i}}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{|\mathcal{B}|}e^{t\,\htmlClass{t-blue}{\mathbf{x}_i}\cdot\htmlClass{t-yellow}{\mathbf{y}_j}}}} + \htmlData{step=3, link=col}{\log\frac{e^{t\,\htmlClass{t-blue}{\mathbf{x}_i}\cdot\htmlClass{t-yellow}{\mathbf{y}_i}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{|\mathcal{B}|}e^{t\,\htmlClass{t-blue}{\mathbf{x}_j}\cdot\htmlClass{t-yellow}{\mathbf{y}_i}}}}\right)

Unnumbered, Sec. 3.1 of Zhai et al., "Sigmoid loss for language image pre-training", ICCV 2023, arXiv 2303.15343v4 (its labels "image to text softmax" and "text to image softmax" over the two terms are dropped here). It is the loss of the Figure 3 pseudocode in Radford et al., arXiv 2103.00020v1, with np.exp(t) written as t (our transcription).

  • \mathcal{B}: the batch of photo-caption pairs; |\mathcal{B}| is its size, the N above.
  • \htmlClass{t-blue}{\mathbf{x}_i}, \htmlClass{t-yellow}{\mathbf{y}_j}: the unit-length embeddings of photo i and caption j. Their dot product is the cosine in cell (i, j).
  • t: the scale, one over the temperature \tau of chapter 3. It is learned, as t = e^{t'}.
  • First log: the softmax along row i, "which caption is photo i's partner?". Its denominator sums over every caption.
  • Second log: the softmax down column i, "which photo is caption i's partner?". Its denominator sums over every photo.

CLIP learns the temperature instead of fixing it. It starts at the equivalent of 0.07, and the scale t is clipped so it never exceeds 100, to keep training stable.

Try one row with numbers. Take three pairs and t = 10, the figure's scale. Say the cosines are:

\begin{array}{c|ccc} & \text{"dog"} & \text{"cat"} & \text{"car"} \\ \hline \text{dog} & 0.8 & 0.5 & 0.1 \\ \text{cat} & 0.5 & 0.7 & 0.2 \\ \text{car} & 0.1 & 0.2 & 0.9 \end{array}

The dog row has scores 8,\ 5,\ 1. The softmax gives e^8 / (e^8 + e^5 + e^1) \approx 0.95 for "dog", 0.05 for "cat" and 0.001 for "car". The row's loss is -\log 0.95 \approx 0.05: this row is nearly solved.

Zero-shot: which caption is my partner?

Here is the payoff. After training, you can classify a photo into classes nobody trained on. Write one caption per class, such as "a photo of a dog." and "a photo of a cat.", and embed them with the text encoder. Then ask the question CLIP was trained on: which caption is this photo's partner? The class of the most similar caption is the answer. No labelled training images are needed, so this is called zero-shot classification.

CLIP was trained on 400 million photo-caption pairs from the web, with a batch of 32,768 pairs. Zero-shot on ImageNet, its best model (a ViT-L/14 at 336 pixels) reaches 76.2% top-1, and its ResNet-50 reaches 59.6%. The best model thus matches a ResNet-50 trained on all 1.28 million labelled ImageNet images, without using any of them. A different protocol, a linear classifier trained on the frozen image features with labels, gives 85.4% for the same ViT-L/14 at 336 pixels.

The trouble with the softmax: it needs the whole row

Look at the red outline again. To turn one cell into a probability, the softmax needs the sum over its whole row, and the column loss needs the whole column. At toy size that is nothing. At CLIP's batch the photos and captions are spread over many accelerators, called devices, each holding a slice of the batch.

Use the sliders under the figure. With the defaults, a batch of 32,768 pairs over 8 devices, one row's denominator needs all 32,768 scores, and 28,672 of the captions behind them live on other devices. So before any row can be scored, every device must collect every caption embedding from every other device (an all-gather), and the same again for photos for the columns. Make the batch bigger and the full table grows with its square.

SigLIP: one yes/no question per cell

SigLIP (Sigmoid loss for Language-Image Pre-training) drops the lineup. Each cell asks its own question: do this photo and this caption belong together? A diagonal cell should answer yes, every other cell no. Each answer is scored like any binary classifier, with a sigmoid and a log loss. No cell looks at any other cell.

Press "Sigmoid per cell". With the starting bias b = -10, every cell, even the true pairs, says "probably not". That looks wrong, but it is deliberate. In a batch of N pairs there are only N yes-cells and N^2 - N no-cells, so "no" is almost always right. Starting every cell near "no" means training does not first spend large steps learning that. Now drag the bias slider to -5. A cell now says yes once t \cos + b > 0, that is, once its cosine passes -b/t = 0.5. The diagonal turns green, and the dog photo still gives "cat" a fair 0.40, an honest "maybe" that the softmax would never allow.

\htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{sig}}} = -\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}\htmlData{step=4, link=cell}{\sum_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{j=1}^{|\mathcal{B}|}} \log\frac{1}{1+e^{\htmlData{step=1, link=diag}{\htmlClass{t-green}{z_{ij}}}\left(\htmlData{step=2, link=scale}{-t\,\htmlClass{t-blue}{\mathbf{x}_i}\cdot\htmlClass{t-yellow}{\mathbf{y}_j}} \htmlData{step=3, link=bias}{- b}\right)}}

Unnumbered, Sec. 3.2 of SigLIP, arXiv 2303.15343v4, with the sign of b taken from the paper's Algorithm 1, which computes logits = dot(zimg, ztxt.T) * t + b. The equation printed in Sec. 3.2 has +b inside the exponent, which would make the logit t\,\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y} - b. With b starting at -10, only the Algorithm 1 form starts every cell near "no", which is what the paper says the bias is for, so we follow Algorithm 1.

  • \htmlClass{t-green}{z_{ij}}: the label of cell (i, j): +1 on the diagonal (a true pair), -1 everywhere else.
  • t\,\mathbf{x}_i\cdot\mathbf{y}_j + b: the cell's logit. Each term of the loss is \log\sigma(z_{ij}(t\,\mathbf{x}_i\cdot\mathbf{y}_j + b)), with \sigma(u) = 1/(1+e^{-u}) the sigmoid.
  • t = e^{t'}: the learned scale. t' starts at log 10, so t = 10.
  • b: a learned bias shared by every cell, starting at -10. It sets the cosine where "no" turns into "yes".
  • The double sum runs over all |\mathcal{B}|^2 cells, each scored on its own, and is divided by |\mathcal{B}|.

Back to the three-pair example, dog row, t = 10. At the starting bias b = -10 the logits are -2,\ -5,\ -9 and the probabilities 0.12,\ 0.007,\ 0.0001: the "dog" cell pays -\log 0.12 \approx 2.1, the two no-cells almost nothing. With b = -5 the logits are 3,\ 0,\ -4 and the probabilities 0.95,\ 0.50,\ 0.02. Now "dog" pays 0.05, "cat" pays -\log(1 - 0.5) \approx 0.69, and "car" pays 0.02. The softmax was happy with this row; the sigmoid still pushes "cat" down, because it judges each cell against a fixed line rather than against its neighbours.

Check yourself: add a fourth caption, "puppy", with cosine 0.7 to the dog photo. Which of the dog row's probabilities change, under each loss?

Under the softmax, all of them. The new score e^7 joins the denominator, and "dog" drops from 0.95 to about 0.70, though nothing about the dog photo or its caption changed. Under the sigmoid, none of them. "Puppy" simply gets its own cell, and with b = -5 it reads \sigma(2) \approx 0.88. That independence is the whole point: a cell's loss never needs the rest of the batch.

What independence buys

Press "Sigmoid per cell" and look at the bar again. One cell's probability needs one score, its own. The total work is the same, all N^2 cells still get scored, but no device ever needs the whole row at once. SigLIP's implementation exploits this. Each device scores the block of cells for its own photos and the captions it currently holds, then passes those captions to its neighbour and repeats. There are no all-gathers, and the memory at any moment drops from |\mathcal{B}|^2 to B_d^2, where B_d is the per-device batch (our notation; the paper writes it b, the same letter as the bias). The VLM survey's sigmoid chapter animates this device-by-device schedule and trains the bias from both starting points.

The second gain is at small batches. Each row of the softmax is a lineup, and a lineup with few candidates is an easy test. Each sigmoid cell is a fixed question, so it does not depend on how many others are in the batch. The paper finds the sigmoid "significantly better" than the softmax below a batch of 16 k, and the two meet at large batches:

Bigger batches stop paying off: in the paper's runs the sigmoid peaked at 32 k, while the softmax still gained a little up to 98 k. And the cheapness pays off in another setting: with a frozen, already-trained ViT-g/14 image encoder and only the text encoder trained, SigLIP's sigmoid reaches 84.5% zero-shot on ImageNet, on four TPUv4 chips in two days.

Where this came from

CLIP was posted in February 2021. Its authors name the lineup losses of this page, the N-pair loss and InfoNCE, as its ancestors, and its big change was the data: captions scraped at scale instead of labels. ALIGN appeared the same month with the same loss on even noisier data. SigLIP followed in 2023 and kept everything but the loss.

Where it stands in 2025-26

Image-text contrastive training is the branch of this page that is still growing, and its lesson is simple. Since 2021 the loss has hardly changed; the gains came from data and scale. ALIGN (2102.05918v2) trained the CLIP loss on 1.8B noisy alt-text pairs with almost no cleaning and reached 76.4% zero-shot ImageNet. MetaCLIP (2309.16671v6) kept CLIP's recipe and only balanced the data per concept, reaching 70.8% zero-shot with a ViT-B/16. The OpenCLIP scaling laws (2212.07143v2) showed accuracy rising as a power law with model and data size, and Perception Encoder (2504.13181v2) scaled the plain CLIP loss to 85.4% zero-shot ImageNet. SigLIP 2 (2502.14786v1) keeps this chapter's sigmoid and adds extra training signals on top. For dense, pixel-level features, self-distilled image-only encoders are now the usual choice; the decision guide covers when to pick which.

For how CLIP-style encoders feed vision-language models, see the VLM survey; for how they let detectors find objects named in free text, see the open-vocabulary detection survey.

You should now be able to explain

  • How CLIP turns a batch of photo-caption pairs into a table and trains a lineup along every row and every column.
  • Why zero-shot classification is just "which caption is my partner?".
  • Why the softmax needs the whole row, and what that costs across devices.
  • How SigLIP's per-cell sigmoid and its bias of -10 remove that need, and why it helps most at small batches.

In practice

Reach for a pretrained CLIP- or SigLIP-family encoder when you want to search images with text, or to sort images into classes you can name but have few examples of. Write captions the way web text reads ("a photo of a ..."), and test a few phrasings, since the wording moves accuracy. If you fine-tune one yourself on a modest number of GPUs, the sigmoid loss is the safer choice: it does not need a huge batch, and it does not need every device to see every caption. For pixel-level tasks such as segmentation, compare against a self-distilled encoder such as DINOv2 or DINOv3 before you commit.
