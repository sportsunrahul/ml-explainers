Act IVBeyond pairs

Without negatives: clusters, redundancy, collapse

Negatives stop collapse, but they are expensive, and some of them are not really negatives. Can you stop collapse some other way? This chapter shows two ways that need no negatives at all, and then a quieter kind of collapse that even negatives do not prevent.

Why look for another way

Go back to the student from chapter 1 who answers "same" to every question. Negatives fix the student by also asking "are these two different?". That fix has a price. MoCo keeps a queue of 65,536 old embeddings (chapter 4). SimCLR's best results use a batch of 4096 images (chapter 5). And without labels, some "negatives" show the same kind of object as the anchor, so the loss pushes apart things that belong together (chapter 7).

So the real job of the negatives is to make collapse expensive. Any other rule that makes "everything is the same" a bad answer would do the same job. Here are two such rules.

First answer: sort the views into clusters

Give the network a small set of learnable vectors called prototypes. Think of them as bins. For each view, the network scores how well its embedding matches each prototype. A soft assignment of a view to the bins is its code: for example, "70% bin 1, 30% bin 3".

Now the game: compute the code of view 1, and ask the network to predict that code from view 2. Then swap the roles. This is swapped prediction. If the network can predict one view's bin from the other view, it must have kept what the two views share.

You can probably see the trap. If every image goes into bin 1, every code is "100% bin 1", and the prediction is always right. That is collapse again, in a new form. So SwAV adds one rule: within each batch, every bin must be used equally. A batch of 8 images and 4 bins must put 2 images' worth of mass into each bin. Now "everything in bin 1" breaks the rule, so it is not allowed.

How do you force equal use? The figure below starts from a score matrix where one prototype is winning almost everything. It has 4 prototypes as rows and 8 images as columns. Each cell is how much of an image's mass goes to a prototype; darker brown means more.

Make a guess before you press "1 iteration". One iteration rescales the rows, then the columns. Will every row then sum to exactly 0.250?

Press it. The columns are exact, because they were fixed last. The rows are close but not exact: fixing the columns undid a little of the row fix. Press "2 iterations" and "3 iterations": the row sums close in on 0.250 fast. Watch the top choices too. Before balancing, prototype 1 wins 6 of the 8 images. After balancing, all 4 prototypes are someone's top choice.

You might expect the loss in the side panel to fall as the codes get balanced. In this toy it rises. Balancing changed the answer key: image 1's code no longer says "almost all prototype 1", but its second view still predicts prototype 1. Training then moves the network to match the new key. That is how the equal-use rule reaches the encoder.

The same idea as equations

First the loss. Each view t has a normalized embedding \mathbf{z}_t; its code is \mathbf{q}_t. The loss asks each view to predict the other view's code.

\begin{array}{l} \displaystyle L(\mathbf{z}_t, \mathbf{z}_s) = \htmlData{step=1, link=loss}{\htmlClass{t-red}{\ell(\mathbf{z}_t, \mathbf{q}_s) + \ell(\mathbf{z}_s, \mathbf{q}_t)}} \\[4pt] \displaystyle \ell(\mathbf{z}_t, \mathbf{q}_s) = - \sum_{k} \htmlData{step=2, link=code}{\htmlClass{t-brown}{\mathbf{q}_s^{(k)}}} \log \htmlData{step=3, link=pred}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{\mathbf{p}_t^{(k)}}}, \quad \text{where} \quad \mathbf{p}_t^{(k)} = \frac{ \exp \left ( \frac{1}{\tau} \mathbf{z}_t^\top \mathbf{c}_k \right ) }{\sum_{k'} \exp \left ( \frac{1}{\tau} \mathbf{z}_t^\top \mathbf{c}_{k'} \right ) } \end{array}

Eq. 1 and Eq. 2 in Caron et al., "Unsupervised Learning of Visual Features by Contrasting Cluster Assignments" (SwAV), 2006.09882v5.

  • \mathbf{c}_k: prototype k, a learnable unit vector. SwAV uses 3000 of them, each 128-d.
  • \htmlClass{t-brown}{\mathbf{q}_s^{(k)}}: the share of prototype k in the code of view s. Codes are computed with no gradient; the paper is careful to say it does not treat them as a target, only as a way to make the two views map consistently.
  • \htmlClass{t-yellow}{\mathbf{p}_t^{(k)}}: the softmax probability that view t belongs to prototype k, with temperature \tau = 0.1.
  • \htmlClass{t-red}{\ell}: the cross-entropy between a code and a prediction. Its gradient trains both the encoder and the prototypes.

Try it with two prototypes. Say view s has code \mathbf{q}_s = (0.8, 0.2), and view t scores 0.6 and 0.4 against the prototypes. Divide by \tau = 0.1 to get 6 and 4. The softmax gives \mathbf{p}_t \approx (0.881, 0.119). The loss is -(0.8 \ln 0.881 + 0.2 \ln 0.119) \approx 0.102 + 0.425 = 0.527. Most of it comes from the second prototype: the code gives it 20\%, but the prediction gives it only 12\%.

Next, the equal-use rule. Stack the codes of a batch into a matrix \mathbf{Q} with K rows (prototypes) and B columns (images), exactly as the figure draws it. K here is the number of prototypes, not MoCo's queue length (chapter 4). The rule says which matrices are allowed:

\mathcal{Q} = \left \{\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}_+^{K\times B} ~|~ \htmlData{step=1, link=rows}{\mathbf{Q} \mathbf{1}_B = \frac{1}{K} \mathbf{1}_K}, \htmlData{step=2, link=cols}{\mathbf{Q}^\top \mathbf{1}_K = \frac{1}{B} \mathbf{1}_B} \right \}

Eq. 4 in SwAV, 2006.09882v5.

  • \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{1}_B = \frac{1}{K} \mathbf{1}_K: every row sums to 1/K. Each prototype gets an equal share of the batch. In the toy, that is the 0.250 target to the right of the matrix.
  • \mathbf{Q}^\top \mathbf{1}_K = \frac{1}{B} \mathbf{1}_B: every column sums to 1/B. Each image counts equally. In the toy, that is the 0.125 target below the matrix.

Among the allowed matrices, SwAV wants the one that best agrees with the scores, kept a little soft. The answer has a closed form, which the figure computes:

\mathbf{Q}^*= \htmlData{step=3, link=rows}{\text{Diag}(\mathbf{u})} \htmlData{step=1, link=kernel}{\htmlClass{t-brown}{\exp\left(\frac{\mathbf{C}^\top \mathbf{Z}}{\htmlData{step=2, link=eps}{\varepsilon}} \right)}} \htmlData{step=4, link=cols}{\text{Diag}(\mathbf{v})}

Eq. 5 in SwAV, 2006.09882v5. It solves Eq. 3, \max_{\mathbf{Q}\in\mathcal{Q}} \text{Tr}(\mathbf{Q}^\top \mathbf{C}^\top \mathbf{Z}) + \varepsilon H(\mathbf{Q}).

  • \mathbf{C}^\top \mathbf{Z}: the score of every image against every prototype (K \times B).
  • \varepsilon: how soft the codes are. SwAV uses 0.05. A small \varepsilon makes \exp(\cdot / \varepsilon) very peaked; the paper reports that a high \varepsilon leads to a trivial solution where all samples collapse.
  • \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}: one scale per row and one per column. The Sinkhorn-Knopp algorithm finds them by the alternating rescaling you just watched.

Try one iteration by hand with two prototypes and two images. Say \exp(\cdot/\varepsilon) gives rows (6, 1) and (2, 1). Divide by the total, 10: rows (0.6, 0.1) and (0.2, 0.1). Both images prefer prototype 1.

Rescale each row to sum 0.5: (0.429, 0.071) and (0.333, 0.167). Rescale each column to sum 0.5: (0.281, 0.150) and (0.219, 0.350). Image 2 now leans to prototype 2. The rows sum to 0.431 and 0.569, so one iteration is not yet balanced.

Check yourself: why does SwAV need the equal-use rule, if the loss already compares two views?

Because comparing two views is exactly the pull from chapter 1, and pull alone collapses. Without the rule, sending every image to one prototype makes every code identical and every prediction perfect. The rule makes that answer illegal: within a batch, each prototype must take an equal share. So the rule plays the role that negatives play in InfoNCE.

Second answer: make every feature say something different

Barlow Twins looks at the embedding from a different side. Do not compare images with images. Compare features with features.

Imagine a committee of eight people, each watching both views of every photo. Each member must give the same answer for both views of a photo: that is invariance. But no two members may give answers that move together across photos: each must report something the others do not. A committee where everyone repeats the same thing is useless, even if each member is consistent.

In numbers: take a batch, compute every feature for view A and view B, and measure how feature i of view A correlates with feature j of view B across the batch. That gives a square cross-correlation matrix. The goal is the identity matrix: 1 on the diagonal (each feature agrees across the views), 0 everywhere else (no two features carry the same information). The figure trains a small linear encoder toward that goal.

Make a guess before you press "lambda = 0". This trains with the diagonal term only. The diagonal will turn green. What will the off-diagonal cells do?

Press it. The diagonal goes to almost 1, but the off-diagonal cells stay red. Every feature learned to ignore the view noise, and most of them did it the same way: they became near-copies of each other. Nothing collapsed to a point, yet the eight features hold far less than eight features' worth of information. Now press "0.005". The off-diagonal cells fade to almost 0, and the diagonal still rises. The matrix becomes the identity.

The diagonal ends a little lower with a larger lambda. That is the trade the weight controls: the off-diagonal term is satisfied first, and the invariance term has less room. Try "0.020" to see the effect grow.

Barlow Twins as an equation

\mathcal{L_{BT}} \triangleq \htmlData{step=1, link=diag}{\htmlClass{t-green}{\underbrace{\sum_i (1-\mathcal{C}_{ii})^2}_\text{invariance term}}} + ~~\htmlData{step=2, link=lambda}{\lambda} \htmlData{step=3, link=offdiag}{\htmlClass{t-red}{\underbrace{\sum_{i}\sum_{j \neq i} {\mathcal{C}_{ij}}^2}_\text{redundancy reduction term}}}

Eq. 1 in Zbontar et al., "Barlow Twins: Self-Supervised Learning via Redundancy Reduction", 2103.03230v3.

\htmlData{step=1, link=cell}{\mathcal{C}_{ij}} \triangleq \frac{\sum_b \htmlData{step=2, link=za}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{z^A_{b,i}}} \htmlData{step=3, link=zb}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{z^B_{b,j}}}}{\sqrt{\sum_b {(z^A_{b,i})}^2} \sqrt{\sum_b {(z^B_{b,j})}^2}}

Eq. 2 in Barlow Twins, 2103.03230v3. The embeddings are mean-centered over the batch first.

  • b: indexes the images in the batch. i, j: index the features.
  • \htmlClass{t-blue}{z^A_{b,i}}, \htmlClass{t-yellow}{z^B_{b,j}}: feature i of view A and feature j of view B, for image b.
  • \mathcal{C}_{ij}: their correlation over the batch, between -1 and 1. The matrix is square, one row and one column per feature.
  • \htmlClass{t-green}{(1-\mathcal{C}_{ii})^2}: zero when feature i agrees perfectly across the two views.
  • \htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{C}_{ij}^2}, i \neq j: zero when two different features are uncorrelated. Note what is missing: no image is ever compared with another image, so there are no negatives.
  • \lambda: the off-diagonal weight. The paper uses 0.005, found by search.

Try Eq. 2 on a batch of three images. Feature i of view A is (1, 0, -1) and feature j of view B is (1, -1, 0); both already have mean zero. The top is 1 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot (-1) + (-1) \cdot 0 = 1. Each norm is \sqrt{2}, so \mathcal{C}_{ij} = 1/2 = 0.5: the two features move together half the time.

Try it with two features. Say \mathcal{C} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.9 & 0.6 \\ 0.5 & 0.8 \end{pmatrix} and \lambda = 0.005. The invariance term is (1-0.9)^2 + (1-0.8)^2 = 0.01 + 0.04 = 0.05. The redundancy term is 0.6^2 + 0.5^2 = 0.61, times \lambda: 0.00305. Total: 0.05305.

Here the off-diagonal part barely counts. That looks wrong until you count entries. The paper's projector outputs 8192 features, so the matrix has 67,100,672 off-diagonal entries against 8192 diagonal ones (our arithmetic). A small weight on a huge number of terms balances the two parts. That is why the toy, with only 56 off-diagonal entries, has to scale lambda up.

Both terms are needed. In the paper's 300-epoch ablation (ResNet-50, ImageNet linear probe, top-1), the full loss gives 71.4. The invariance term alone gives 57.3, and the redundancy term alone gives 0.1 (Table 5). The paper sweeps lambda from 0.002 to 0.020 and calls the result not very sensitive (Fig. 5).

Check yourself: why does the redundancy term alone score almost zero?

Without the diagonal term, nothing asks the two views to agree. Features that are perfectly uncorrelated with each other are easy to get, for example from random noise, and they need not say anything about the image. Decorrelation stops the features from copying each other; invariance makes them about the image. You need both.

A third answer: asymmetry and self-distillation

One more family uses no negatives and no balancing rule. It breaks the symmetry between the two branches instead. BYOL (2006.07733) and SimSiam (2011.10566) use two tools.

  • A predictor: a small extra network on one branch only. That branch must predict the other branch's embedding, instead of simply matching it.
  • A stop-gradient on the other branch: its output is used as the answer, but no gradient flows back through it. On this page brown always means "no gradient", like MoCo's key encoder in chapter 4.

The intuition: without the stop-gradient, both branches are pulled toward each other, and the quickest way to agree is one shared constant. With it, the learning branch must predict an answer it cannot move. The constant is still a valid answer, yet in practice training does not fall into it. Why exactly is subtle; the JEPA survey's collapse chapter works it out with a live toy.

BYOL also makes the answer branch a slow-moving average of the learning branch. That gives the idea its name. In self-distillation, a student network learns to match a slow-moving teacher copy of itself, as MoCo's key encoder does. DINO (2104.14294) is self-distillation without negatives: the student matches the teacher's softmax output over many classes it invents, and two simple tricks on the teacher's output (centering and sharpening) stop collapse instead of a Sinkhorn step. VICReg (2105.04906) goes back the other way and writes Barlow's idea as explicit variance, invariance and covariance terms.

A quieter failure: dimensional collapse

So far, collapse meant every image landing on one point. There is a milder version. The embeddings stay spread out, but only along a few directions. The other directions are flat: every image has almost the same value there. The embedding has many dimensions on paper and few in practice. This is dimensional collapse. You already saw a form of it: with lambda = 0, most of the eight Barlow features crowded into the same direction.

Jing et al. found that contrastive methods with negatives suffer from it too, and they explained one cause. The figure shows it in three dimensions. Each blue point is one input passed through a trained linear layer. A few points also show their two views (blue and yellow, joined by a green line), so you can see how far apart the views land.

Press "Healthy" first. The cloud stays round, and all three bars stay tall. Then press "Complete collapse": with the pull term alone, every direction shrinks and the cloud becomes a point, just as in chapter 1.

Make a guess before you press "Dimensional collapse". Now the views differ a lot along axis 3: the view noise there has variance 2, twice the data's own variance of 1. The loss rewards the network for making the two views agree. What is the cheapest thing it can do with axis 3?

Press it. The network squashes axis 3. The cloud becomes a flat pancake. Two bars stay tall; the third drops about 30 times below them, but not to zero. The negatives still keep the cloud wide along axes 1 and 2, so this is not complete collapse. It is a dimension thrown away.

Why does that happen? Along axis 3, most of the difference between two views is augmentation noise, not a difference between images. Squashing axis 3 removes more view disagreement than it removes useful spread. Along axes 1 and 2 the opposite holds, so those axes grow.

The same idea as equations

To see dimensional collapse in a real model, measure how spread out the embeddings are in each direction. Take the covariance matrix of the embeddings of many images, and look at its singular values in sorted order. That sorted list is the spectrum.

\htmlData{step=1, link=cov}{C = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N (\textbf{z}_i-\bar{\textbf{z}})(\textbf{z}_i-\bar{\textbf{z}})^T}, \qquad C = U \htmlData{step=2, link=sv}{\htmlClass{t-green}{S}} V^T

Eq. 1 in Jing et al., "Understanding Dimensional Collapse in Contrastive Self-supervised Learning", 2110.09348v3; the singular value decomposition is from the same paragraph (Sec. 3).

  • \textbf{z}_i: the embedding of image i; \bar{\textbf{z}}: the mean embedding; N: the number of images.
  • \htmlClass{t-green}{S}: a diagonal matrix of singular values, one per direction. A singular value near zero is a direction in which every image looks the same: a collapsed dimension. The bars in the figure are these values.

Try it with three 2-d embeddings: (1, 0), (-1, 0) and (0, 0). The mean is (0, 0). The covariance is \tfrac13 \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, so the singular values are 2/3 and 0. The second direction is collapsed: every image has the same second coordinate.

The cause is a matrix Jing et al. call X. In a single linear layer trained with InfoNCE, the weights evolve as \dot W = W X (their Eq. 5 with Lemma 1), and X splits into two parts:

X = \htmlData{step=1, link=cov}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{\hat{\Sigma}_0}} - \htmlData{step=2, link=aug}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{\hat{\Sigma}_1}}

Eq. 7 (Lemma 2), Sec. 4.2 in Jing et al., 2110.09348v3.

  • \htmlClass{t-blue}{\hat{\Sigma}_0}: a weighted covariance of the differences between different images. The data's spread.
  • \htmlClass{t-yellow}{\hat{\Sigma}_1}: a weighted covariance of the differences between the two views of the same image. The augmentation's spread.
  • Where X has a negative eigenvalue (a direction in which X shrinks vectors instead of growing them), the weights shrink along that direction. Their Theorem 1: "With fixed matrix X ... and strong augmentation such that X has negative eigenvalues, the weight matrix W has vanishing singular values."

Check it against the toy, ignoring the weights inside the two sums (our simplification). Along axis 1, the data spread is 1 and the view noise is 0.1: 1 - 0.1 > 0, so the axis grows. Along axis 3 in the "dimensional" run, it is 1 - 2 < 0, so the axis shrinks. Jing's own toy sweeps the augmentation strength from 0.1 to 2 on a 16x16 layer. Its augmented singular values drop sharply but stay above zero, like the third bar here. The theorem's "vanishing" is the limit.

Strong augmentation is not the only cause. With two or more linear layers, small singular values also grow more slowly than large ones, so weak directions fall behind even with mild augmentation. The paper calls this implicit regularization (Sec. 5).

Where the collapse lives: the throwaway head

Jing et al. measured SimCLR on ImageNet after 100 epochs. In the 128-d embedding that the loss sees, a number of singular values drop to zero; the cliff sits near rank about 95, read off their Fig. 2. But the 2048-d representation before the projector stays healthy (their Fig. 7b). The projector takes the collapse, and the layer below keeps its dimensions.

This is one explanation of a finding from chapter 5: a small head used only for the loss, and thrown away after training, makes the features under it better. Jing et al. test it with ResNet-50, 100 epochs, linear probe top-1. SimCLR without a projector gets 51.5; with its usual two-layer projector, 66.5. Their DirectCLR drops the projector and applies the loss to a fixed slice of the representation instead, and gets 62.7 (Table 1).

Where this came from

SwAV is by Caron et al. (NeurIPS 2020). It learns the prototypes together with the encoder and computes codes only from the full-resolution crops. The paper reports that 3 Sinkhorn iterations are "fast and sufficient", taking 35ms to align 4K features to 3K codes. With 1 iteration, training does not work: Table 14 lists it as "fail", against 73.9 with 3 iterations (an ablation run, ResNet-50, ImageNet linear probe). Barlow Twins is by Zbontar et al. (ICML 2021); its subtitle names the principle, redundancy reduction. Jing et al.'s analysis appeared at ICLR 2022.

The self-distillation line from the step above then took over image-only pretraining: iBOT, DINOv2 and DINOv3 scale it to large vision transformers and web-scale data (2304.07193, 2508.10104).

Where we go next: labels

Every method on this page so far has guessed which pairs belong together from augmentation alone. That guess is why false negatives exist, and why codes and decorrelation were needed. If you have labels, you can simply tell the loss which images share a class. Chapter 9 does that with the same InfoNCE machinery.

You should now be able to explain

  • How SwAV stops collapse with an equal-use rule on cluster codes, and what the Sinkhorn iterations do to the rows and columns.
  • Why Barlow Twins drives the cross-correlation matrix to the identity, and why it needs both the diagonal and the off-diagonal term.
  • What dimensional collapse is, why augmentation noise larger than the data's spread in one direction causes it, and why it shows up in the projector's output rather than in the representation below it.

In practice

Check the spectrum of your embeddings, not only the loss. Embed a few thousand held-out images, compute the covariance, and plot its sorted singular values on a log scale: a cliff means collapsed dimensions. If you fine-tune with a contrastive or no-negative loss, take features from below the projection head, and be careful with augmentations that change one property much more than the data itself varies. For pretrained general-purpose features today, the self-distillation descendants of this line (DINOv2 and later) are the usual starting point.
