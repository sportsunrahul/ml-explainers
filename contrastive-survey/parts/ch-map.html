The field at a glance

How big is this field, and which branches does this page follow? This chapter gives you a map: a count of papers by year, and a family tree you can come back to.

Optional on a first read: skip to chapter 3 for the mechanics.

Counting papers

One cheap way to see a field is to count how often its name appears. The chart below asks the arXiv preprint server two questions for each year. First, how many papers have the word "contrastive" in the title? Second, how many have the phrase "contrastive learning" in the abstract, the summary paragraph at the top of a paper?

Why 2020 is the turning point

Look at the solid bars first. The phrase "contrastive learning" was almost absent from abstracts before 2020: 17 papers in 2019. Then it jumped to 241 in 2020 and 690 in 2021.

Two papers sit right at the jump. MoCo (first posted 2019-11-13) and SimCLR (2020-02-13) turned the idea of chapter 1 into a recipe that anyone could copy: two augmented views, a standard network, and many negatives. Chapters 4 and 5 open each recipe up. Once a recipe is simple and works, other people start to use it, and the counts follow.

A word becomes a tool

Now compare the two bar colors. Before 2022, more papers put "contrastive" in the title than put "contrastive learning" in the abstract. From 2022 on it is the other way round: 1203 abstracts against 1197 titles that year, and the gap grows through 2025.

Here is our reading of that. A title names what a paper is about. An abstract also names what a paper uses. When the abstract count overtakes the title count, more papers use contrastive learning as one part of a larger method than study it for its own sake. The idea has become a tool.

A warning about the early title bars: "contrastive" is an ordinary English word. In 2019, 183 titles contained it, but only 17 abstracts said "contrastive learning". Many of those early titles are likely about other things, such as contrastive explanations or the contrastive study of languages. The abstract phrase is the cleaner signal.

Where the word spread

The method was born in vision. You might expect images to dominate the counts. Make a guess before you press "image or visual": what share of all "contrastive" titles also name images, and did that share rise or fall after 2020?

Press it. The image count keeps rising: 49 titles in 2019, 240 in 2025. But the dashed share line falls from about a quarter to under a fifth and then stays flat. Now press "text", "graph" and "recommendation". Each of them starts near zero before 2020 and climbs after. The image count grew; the rest of the field grew faster.

That is why a raw count can mislead you. To compare one slice across years, divide it by the whole.

\htmlData{step=3, link=share}{s(y)} = \frac{\htmlData{step=1, link=slice}{n_{\text{slice}}(y)}}{\htmlData{step=2, link=all}{n_{\text{all}}(y)}}

Derived here, in our notation: a plain share, not an equation from a paper.

  • y: the year of first submission to arXiv.
  • n_{\text{slice}}(y): the titles that contain "contrastive" and also a topic word, such as image or graph (the solid bars in the topic view).
  • n_{\text{all}}(y): all titles that contain "contrastive" (the grey bars).
  • s(y): the slice's share of the whole, between 0 and 1 (the dashed line).

Try it with the image slice:

  • In 2019, 49 of 183 titles named images, so s = 49 / 183 \approx 0.27: about 27%.
  • In 2021, 142 of 853, so s \approx 0.17: about 17%.
  • In 2025, 240 of 1324, so s \approx 0.18: about 18%.

So the image slice is a steady minority, near a fifth of the titles, while the word spread to text, graphs, recommendation and medicine. This page stays with images, because that is where the ideas were found and tested.

The arXiv search API's total-result count, one query per year of first submission, 2017 to 2026: "contrastive" in the title, "contrastive learning" in the abstract, and "contrastive" in the title together with one topic word. All of arXiv, every category.

A hand-labelled sample showing that most matching titles are not about the learning method, or a full-text search showing that image papers use the method far more often than their titles say.
Check yourself: a 2024 paper fine-tunes a detector on top of a SigLIP image encoder, and never writes "contrastive" in its title or abstract. Which bars count it?

None of them. The scan only matches words. That paper depends on contrastive pretraining, but the count cannot see it. This is the main blind spot of the chart: the more a method becomes a standard part, the less often papers name it.

The family tree

Counts tell you how much. The tree tells you who built on whom. Each pill is one paper, placed in a lane: the problem it works on. Time runs down the page. Inside a lane the order is exact; across lanes, papers on the same band share only the year. Solid pills are taught in depth on this page, and dashed pills are named in passing. Press any pill.

Do not worry about the names yet; each solid pill gets a chapter. Come back to this map after chapter 8.

How to read the lanes

Each lane is a question the field had to answer, and each one gets a chapter:

  • Negatives: how do you get many negatives cheaply? Memory bank, CPC, MoCo, SimCLR. This lane is the spine of chapters 3 to 5.
  • Theory: what does the loss actually optimize? Mutual information first, then alignment and uniformity on the sphere (chapter 6).
  • Hard negatives: which negatives teach the most, and which are secretly positives (chapter 7)?
  • No negatives: can you stop collapse without negatives at all (chapter 8)?
  • Labels and Language: what changes when the positive is a same-class image (chapter 9) or a caption (chapter 10)?
  • Dense: can you contrast regions or pixels instead of whole images, for detection and segmentation? This page does not teach it; the guide and chapter 10 point to it.

Now read the bottom half of the tree. After 2021 the image-only lanes add self-distillation papers (DINOv2, DINOv3, Web-SSL), which use no negatives. The lane with new contrastive work is Language: SigLIP, MetaCLIP, SigLIP 2 and Perception Encoder. Our reading: image-only contrastive pretraining peaked around 2020 and 2021, and the image-text form is the one that lives on. The idea of pulling the pair and pushing the rest did not go away; it moved to pairs of images and captions.

You should now be able to explain

  • Why 2020 is the turning point, and which two recipes made the idea easy to copy.
  • Why a slice's share, not its raw count, shows whether it is growing relative to the field, and what a word count cannot see.
  • Which lane of the family tree each later chapter follows, and why the image-text lane is the one still active.

In practice

If you need an image encoder today, you will usually start from a pretrained one, not train your own. In our reading, most such encoders come from two lanes of the tree: Language (CLIP, SigLIP and their successors) and No negatives (DINOv2, DINOv3). The Negatives lane is where the ideas behind both were worked out, which is why the rest of this page starts there. When you search for prior work in your own domain, search for the mechanism as well as the word: many papers that use it never say "contrastive".
