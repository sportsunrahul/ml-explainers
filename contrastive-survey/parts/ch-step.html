Interlude

One SimCLR training step, end to end

When the loss goes down, what does each negative actually do to the anchor? Chapter 5 walked SimCLR's step box by box; this interlude runs the same step on drawn photos and stops where that question is answered, at the gradient.

Click a stage or press Next. The stages before the gradient are a quick recap; the shapes and settings are explained in chapter 5, and the equations in chapter 3 and chapter 5. New batch draws four other photos and new random views.

Then the next batch arrives and the step repeats, for 100 epochs in the paper's ablations and 1000 for its best models. When training ends, the head is deleted and the ResNet-50's \bm h is what you keep.

Check yourself: in one row at N = 4096, how many cells get a push, and which one gets a pull?

The row has 8192 cells. The diagonal (the anchor against itself) is masked and gets nothing. The partner view, the other view of the same photo, gets the pull. The remaining 8190 cells are negatives, and each gets a push weighted by its softmax probability divided by the temperature, so most of the push comes from the few negatives that look most like the anchor.

You should now be able to explain

  • Which cells of a row push the anchor, which one pulls it, and why close negatives push hardest.
  • Where the negatives come from, how many each row has, and why the diagonal is removed.
  • Which weights the gradient reaches, and what is thrown away after training.

In practice

The cosine table grows with the square of the batch, so in common multi-device implementations it is split. (This is our note; the paper does not describe it.) Each device computes the rows for its own views against all views gathered from every device. If you reimplement this, gather the embeddings across devices before the matrix product, or each row will see only its own device's negatives. Pool batch-norm statistics across devices too.
