InfoNCE: find your partner in a lineup

Chapter 1 ended with a police lineup: show the network one view of a photo and a row of candidates, and ask it to point at the true partner. This chapter turns that game into a loss called InfoNCE, shows what temperature does to it, and explains what the loss measures and what it cannot.

The game: one anchor, N candidates

Take one photo and make two views of it, as in chapter 1. Call one view the anchor. The other view is its partner, also called the positive. Now add views of other photos. These are the negatives. The partner and the negatives together are the candidates, and there are N of them: one partner and N-1 negatives.

The encoder turns every view into an embedding. To compare two embeddings we use the cosine similarity: the dot product of the two vectors after each is scaled to length 1. It is 1 when they point the same way, 0 when they are at right angles, and -1 when they point in opposite directions. In the figure, each candidate's similarity to the anchor is the first bar under its card.

First attempt: turn the scores into a choice

A good encoder gives the partner the highest score. But "highest" is not a loss yet. We want one number that is small when the partner clearly wins and large when it does not.

You already know a tool for this: the softmax you use for classification. Exponentiate each score, then divide by the sum, and you get N probabilities that add up to 1. The second row of bars shows them. The loss is minus the log of the partner's probability. That is ordinary cross-entropy. The only new thing is what the classes are: not "cat" or "dog", but the N candidates in this lineup, with the partner as the correct answer. Every batch brings a new lineup, so there is no fixed list of classes.

This also fixes collapse. If the encoder sent every photo to one point, every candidate would score the same, each would get probability 1/N, and the loss would be \log N. That is the loss of a blind guess, the worst a sensible model can do. The only way down is to score the partner above the strangers.

Make a guess before you press "Hard (close)". The negatives become views of other mugs, so they score almost as high as the partner. Will the loss go up or down, and how close will it get to the dashed \log N line?

Press it. From the starting state, the partner's probability falls from 0.40 to 0.29 and the loss rises from 0.90 to 1.24, close to \log 4 \approx 1.39. The encoder has not changed; the lineup got harder. You might expect hard negatives to be bad news. In fact they are the useful ones: a negative that already scores far below the partner barely moves the probabilities, so it gives almost no gradient. Chapter 7 returns to this.

The same idea as an equation

Here is the loss for one anchor embedding u, with cosine similarity as the score and a temperature \tau (our notation). Read it as "minus the log of the partner's softmax probability".

\htmlData{step=1, link=loss}{\htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}}} = -\log \frac{\exp\big(\htmlData{step=2, link=partner}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{\mathrm{sim}(u, v^{+})}} / \htmlData{step=4, link=temp}{\tau}\big)}{\htmlData{step=3, link=lineup}{\sum_{j=1}^{N} \exp\big(\mathrm{sim}(u, v_j) / \tau\big)}}

Our notation for InfoNCE (CPC, arXiv 1807.03748v2, Eq. 4), with the cosine score and temperature of Wu et al. 2018 and SimCLR (chapter 5).

  • \htmlClass{t-blue}{u}: the anchor's embedding. v_1, \dots, v_N: the candidates' embeddings; \htmlClass{t-yellow}{v^{+}} is the partner, one of them.
  • \mathrm{sim}(u, v) = u^T v / (\lVert u \rVert \lVert v \rVert): the cosine similarity, between -1 and 1.
  • \tau: the temperature, a positive number that divides every score before the softmax. The next step explains it.

Try it with the figure's starting numbers. Four candidates, \tau = 1, and similarities 0.8 for the partner and 0.3, 0.1, -0.2 for the negatives.

  • Exponentiate: e^{0.8} = 2.23,\; e^{0.3} = 1.35,\; e^{0.1} = 1.11,\; e^{-0.2} = 0.82. The sum is 5.50.
  • The partner's probability: 2.23 / 5.50 = 0.40. The negatives get 0.25, 0.20, 0.15.
  • The loss: -\log 0.40 = 0.90. A blind guess would cost \log 4 = 1.39.

Notation on this page

N here is the number of candidates in one lineup: one partner and N-1 negatives. Later chapters follow their papers and use N for the number of photos in a batch; chapter 5 says so where it switches. \tau always means the temperature.

Temperature: how sharp is the pick?

Look at that example again. The partner wins clearly on similarity, yet it gets only 40\% of the probability. The reason is that cosine similarity lives between -1 and 1, so the exponentials can differ by a factor of at most e^2 \approx 7.4. Even a perfect encoder, with the partner at 1 and three negatives at -1, would get only 1/(1 + 3e^{-2}) = 0.71. With 63 negatives that drops to 0.10.

The temperature fixes this. Divide every similarity by \tau before the softmax. A small \tau stretches the gaps between scores, so the softmax becomes sharp. Try \tau = 0.1 on the same four numbers: the scores become 8, 3, 1, -2, the partner gets 0.99, and the loss drops to 0.008. Drag the \tau slider in the figure and watch the probability bars go from flat to peaked.

You might think the smallest \tau is always best. It is not. When \tau is tiny, the softmax looks almost only at the highest-scoring negative, so nearly all the push lands on the negatives that score closest to the partner. Set the figure to hard negatives, N = 64 and \tau = 0.05. Now the probability the partner loses goes almost all to the closest negatives. The rest get almost nothing. Chapter 6 shows what this does to the shape of the embedding.

CPC itself has no temperature. Its score z^T W_k c is not normalized, so the network can make scores as large as it needs. The temperature comes with unit-length embeddings: Wu et al. (2018) used \tau = 0.07, and SimCLR (chapter 5) made it a standard knob.

What the loss measures: a bound on shared information

CPC gives the loss a second meaning. Its symbols c_t (the anchor) and x_{t+k} (the partner) are drawn in "Inside CPC" below. The mutual information I between two views is how much knowing one tells you about the other, here measured in nats (natural-log units). If the network can pick the partner out of a lineup, the views must share information. CPC turns this into an inequality:

\htmlData{step=1, link=bound}{\htmlClass{t-green}{I(x_{t+k}, c_t)}} \geq \htmlData{step=2, link=ceiling}{\log(N)} - \htmlData{step=3, link=loss}{\htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L_{\text{N}}}}}

CPC (arXiv 1807.03748v2), Sec. 2.3: the unnumbered display just after Eq. 5. It is derived in App. A.1 through a step that is an approximation (Eq. 8). Poole et al. prove the bound without that approximation (Eq. 10, arXiv 1905.06922v1, Sec. 2.3).

  • \htmlClass{t-green}{I(x_{t+k}, c_t)}: the mutual information between the context and the true future piece. Nobody can compute it directly, so we estimate it from below: the bound is a floor under it.
  • \log(N): the loss of a blind guess among N candidates, the dashed line in the figure.
  • \htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{N}}}: the InfoNCE loss. Every nat you take off it raises the floor by one nat.

Read it as "how much better than a blind guess". In the worked example, \log 4 - 0.90 = 0.48 nats: the two views share at least that much. The green bracket in the figure is this number.

Now the catch. The loss can never go below 0, so the bound can never go above \log N (derived here; Poole et al. state the same cap). Pick the partner perfectly out of 4 candidates, and you have shown only \log 4 = 1.39 nats, even if the two views share far more. In the lineup picture: one correct pick out of four people is evidence, but it cannot prove more than "one of four". CPC notes that the bound "becomes tighter as N becomes larger". Its ceiling is log N, which is one reason contrastive methods want many negatives. Press "Easy (far)", set \tau small, and watch the loss hit 0 while the bracket stops at the dashed line.

Check yourself: you train with 256 candidates per lineup and the loss sits at 5.55 for hours. What is the model doing?

Nothing useful. \log 256 = 5.55, so the loss equals that of a blind guess: every candidate gets about the same probability. The bound \log N - \mathcal{L} is 0. Suspect collapse, a bug that feeds the wrong partner, or a learning rate that is too high.

A caveat: more information is not the point

The bound makes a tidy story: InfoNCE works because it maximizes mutual information. Tschannen et al. (1907.13625) tested that story and found it incomplete. Mutual information does not change under any invertible transformation of the features. An encoder that scrambles its features in a reversible way keeps all the information, yet a linear classifier on top may find them useless. So information alone cannot explain why the features are good. In their words, maximizing tighter bounds on mutual information "can result in worse representations". Success depends strongly on the encoder architecture. It also depends on how the score function, called the critic, is built.

How do you score an encoder that never saw a label? Freeze it, train one linear layer on top with the labels, and report how often its top guess is right (top-1 accuracy). This is a linear probe; every accuracy on this page uses it unless it says otherwise.

Claim card: Tschannen et al., "On mutual information maximization for representation learning" (ICLR 2020)

Claim
The success of InfoNCE-style methods "cannot be attributed to the properties of MI alone"; it depends on the inductive bias of the encoder and of the critic. A tighter bound can give worse features.
Setup
MNIST digits cut in two: the top half is one view, the bottom half the other. Quality is the linear-probe test accuracy on the top-half features, with batch 128, averaged over 20 runs. Reference points: a linear classifier on raw pixels reaches about 85%, a supervised network about 94%.
Test 1: invertible encoders
An invertible encoder (one whose input can always be rebuilt from its output) keeps all the information, so the true mutual information cannot change. Yet the estimated bound and the linear accuracy both rise during training. An invertible encoder trained adversarially (no MI estimator) ends up worse than raw pixels, although its true mutual information stays at the maximum.
Test 2: critics
Three score functions on the same encoders: a simple bilinear one (x^T W y) and two small networks. Measured with the NWJ bound (another estimate of mutual information), the MLP critic is tightest but barely beats raw pixels; the bilinear critic is loosest and gives the best features.
Test 3: same bound, different features
An MLP and a ConvNet encoder are trained to hold the bound at a target t of 2 or 4. After 7k iterations both hit the target, but the ConvNet gives clearly higher linear accuracy. Same bound, different features.
Result
Mostly shown in plots, not tables. With batch 128 the InfoNCE estimate is capped at \log 128 \approx 4.85 nats, and it ends near that cap for every critic, so InfoNCE alone cannot rank them.
What would falsify it
Our reading: a tighter bound that always gave better accuracy across critics, or two encoders at the same bound with the same accuracy.
Scope
Small networks on MNIST halves (CIFAR-10 in the appendix), linear evaluation only. Not tested at ImageNet scale.

The same paper gives a more practical reading of InfoNCE (Sec. 4). Take one encoder for both inputs, and score a pair by the dot product of its two embeddings. Then InfoNCE is the N-pair metric-learning loss from chapter 1, up to constants. Think of InfoNCE as a ranking loss on embeddings, and of the bound as a side fact, not the reason it works.

Inside CPC: forward, then backward

Here is how CPC plays the lineup game on images. It cuts each image into a grid of overlapping patches, encodes every patch, and reads the grid from the top down. From the rows it has seen, it predicts the embeddings of patches up to five rows below. Each prediction is an anchor; the true patch below is its partner.

Make a guess before you press "7 Gradients". Which boxes will the gradient reach, and is anything frozen?

Step 6 is the lineup loss of this chapter. This is the same loss, written in CPC's own notation: the anchor is the context c_t, and the partner is the patch x_{t+k} that lies k rows below.

\htmlData{step=1, link=loss}{\htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L_{\text{N}}}}} = - \mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}_{X}\left[\log \frac{\htmlData{step=2, link=partner}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{f_k(x_{t+k}, c_{t})}}}{\htmlData{step=3, link=lineup}{\sum_{x_j \in X} f_k(x_j, c_{t})}}\right]

Eq. 4 in van den Oord, Li and Vinyals, "Representation learning with contrastive predictive coding", arXiv 1807.03748v2, Sec. 2.3.

  • X = \{x_1, \dots, x_N\}: the lineup. One sample is the true future piece; the other N-1 are negatives.
  • \htmlClass{t-yellow}{f_k(x_{t+k}, c_t)}: the score of the partner. CPC uses f_k = \exp(z_{t+k}^T W_k c_t) (its Eq. 3), where z_{t+k} is the encoded piece and W_k is a learned matrix, one per step k.
  • \sum_{x_j \in X} f_k(x_j, c_t): the scores of all N candidates, the partner included. Dividing by it is the softmax.
  • \htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{N}}}: minus the log of the partner's probability, averaged over lineups. The paper calls it "the categorical cross-entropy of classifying the positive sample correctly".

Where this came from

  • Gutmann and Hyvarinen (NCE, AISTATS 2010) learned a model by telling real data from noise, a yes-or-no question that InfoNCE turns into a multiple choice.
  • Wu et al. (1805.01978) treated every image as its own class, with a temperature and a memory bank of all 1.28M ImageNet embeddings (chapter 4).
  • CPC (1807.03748) named InfoNCE and its bound.
  • CPC v2 (1905.09272) rebuilt CPC for images and reached 71.5% top-1 (linear probe, ResNet-161, 200 epochs).
  • CMC (1906.05849) made the views different channels of one image, such as lightness and color.
  • Poole et al. (1905.06922) proved the InfoNCE bound exactly, beside other bounds on mutual information.

Where we go next: more candidates

The bound and the figure point the same way: a lineup with more strangers is a harder test, and its ceiling \log N is higher. But a batch only holds so many images. Chapter 4 shows two ways to get tens of thousands of negatives without a huge batch: a memory bank, then a momentum queue.

You should now be able to explain

  • Why InfoNCE is softmax cross-entropy where the classes are the candidates in the lineup.
  • What the temperature does to the softmax, and why cosine similarity needs one.
  • Why the mutual-information bound \log N - \mathcal{L} can never exceed \log N.
  • Why a tighter bound is not the same as better features.

In practice

Track \log N next to your InfoNCE loss. A loss stuck at \log N means the model is guessing. A loss near 0 means the lineups are too easy to teach anything, so add negatives or make the views harder. Treat the temperature as a hyperparameter to tune, not a constant to copy, and do not read the mutual-information bound as a measure of feature quality.
