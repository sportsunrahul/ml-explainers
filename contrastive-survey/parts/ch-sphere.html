Act IIIWhy it works

Alignment and uniformity on the sphere

You now know how to train with InfoNCE, but what does it actually do to the embeddings? This chapter shows that it does two jobs at once, pulling partners together and spreading everything out, and that the temperature decides how the two trade off.

The picture: points on a ball

Almost every method on this page divides each embedding by its length before it compares two of them. Every embedding then has length 1, so it is a point on the surface of a ball: a sphere. In three dimensions you can see it; real models use many more dimensions, but the picture is the same. On the sphere the dot product of two embeddings is their cosine similarity, a number between -1 and 1, and only directions matter.

Why normalize at all? Without it, the encoder could make the softmax confident by just making vectors longer, without learning anything new. With unit length, the only way to raise a score is to point in a better direction. The temperature \tau then sets the scale that the softmax sees.

The figure trains a small encoder that maps 90 made-up images onto the sphere. Each image has two views: a blue dot (view A) and a yellow dot (view B), joined by a green line. The images come from six hidden classes that the loss never sees; the 15 images of one class are drawn as big dots, so you can follow them.

Make a guess before you press "Alignment only". This loss only asks the two views of each image to land close together. Where will the 90 images end up?

Press it. Everything slides to a single spot on the sphere. You have seen this in chapter 1: if every image gets the same embedding, every pair agrees perfectly and the loss is zero. It is collapse again, now on a sphere.

Now press "InfoNCE". The same pull is still there, but the negatives in the denominator push every image away from every other one. The dots spread over the whole sphere, and each green line stays short.

Two forces: alignment and uniformity

Wang and Isola gave these two effects names. Alignment means the two views of an image land close together. Uniformity means all embeddings together cover the sphere evenly, with no crowded spots and no empty ones.

Think of people arriving in an empty waiting room. Strangers sit as far from each other as they can, so the room fills evenly: that is uniformity. Couples still sit side by side: that is alignment. Nobody wants a crowd, and no couple wants to be split up.

Why is an even spread good? Points that are spread out can be told apart. If many different images crowd into one region, their embeddings are nearly the same, and whatever made them different is lost. An even spread uses all the room the sphere has, so it keeps the most information about the images.

The two forces as equations

Both forces can be measured with a short formula. Alignment is the average distance between the two views of an image:

\mathcal{L}_\mathsf{align}(f; \alpha) \triangleq \htmlData{step=2, link=align}{\mathbb{E}_{(x, y) \sim p_\mathsf{pos}}} \left[ \htmlData{step=1, link=align}{\htmlClass{t-green}{\|f(x) - f(y)\|_2^\alpha}} \right], \quad \alpha > 0.

Unnumbered, Sec. 4.1.1 in Wang and Isola, "Understanding contrastive representation learning through alignment and uniformity on the hypersphere", ICML 2020, arXiv 2005.10242v10.

Uniformity looks at two images picked independently, not a pair. It scores how close they are with a bump that is 1 when they touch and falls toward 0 as they move apart, then averages over all such pairs:

\mathcal{L}_\mathsf{uniform}(f; t) \triangleq \htmlData{step=2}{\log \mathbb{E}_{x, y \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} p_\mathsf{data}}} \left[ \htmlData{step=1, link=uniform}{\htmlClass{t-red}{e^{-t \|f(x) - f(y)\|_2^2}}} \right], \quad t > 0.

Unnumbered, Sec. 4.1.2 in Wang and Isola, arXiv 2005.10242v10 (written there with a Gaussian potential G_t(u, v) = e^{-t\|u - v\|_2^2}).

  • \triangleq means "is defined as"; i.i.d. means drawn independently from the same distribution.
  • f: the encoder; f(x) is a unit-length embedding.
  • (x, y) \sim p_\mathsf{pos}: a positive pair, the two views of one image.
  • x, y \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} p_\mathsf{data}: two images drawn independently from the data, usually two different images.
  • \htmlClass{t-green}{\|f(x) - f(y)\|_2^\alpha}: the distance between partners, raised to the power \alpha. The paper's code uses \alpha = 2.
  • \htmlClass{t-red}{e^{-t\|f(x) - f(y)\|_2^2}}: the bump, the push between two images. The larger t, the faster it falls, so the more it only cares about near neighbours. The code uses t = 2.
  • \log \mathbb{E}: the log of the average bump. Both losses are lower when better.

Try \mathcal L_\mathsf{uniform} with t = 2 on three points on a circle of radius 1. On the unit sphere, \|u - v\|^2 = 2 - 2\,u^\mathsf{T} v, so you only need the angles. Average over the three distinct pairs, as the paper's code does.

  • All three on one spot: every distance is 0, every bump is 1, and \mathcal L = \log 1 = 0. This is the worst value, the collapsed sphere you just saw.
  • Two on one spot, the third opposite (squared distance 4): the bumps are 1, e^{-8} and e^{-8}, so \mathcal L = \log\big((1 + 2e^{-8})/3\big) \approx -1.10.
  • Evenly spaced, 120 degrees apart: each squared distance is 2 - 2\cos 120^\circ = 3, each bump is e^{-6}, and \mathcal L = -6. The even spread wins by far.

Alignment is simpler: two pairs whose views are 0.2 and 0.4 apart give \mathcal L_\mathsf{align} = (0.2^2 + 0.4^2)/2 = 0.10 with \alpha = 2.

The theorem: InfoNCE is these two forces

So far these are two nice measurements. The surprise is that InfoNCE optimizes exactly these two things. Wang and Isola take the loss with M negatives and let M grow without limit. The loss grows like \log M, so they subtract that, and what remains splits into two terms:

\htmlData{step=1, link=tau}{\lim_{M \rightarrow \infty} \mathcal{L}_\mathsf{contrastive}(f; \tau, M) - \log M} = \htmlData{step=2, link=align}{\htmlClass{t-green}{-\frac{1}{\tau} \mathbb{E}_{(x, y) \sim p_\mathsf{pos}}\left[f(x)^{\mathsf{T}} f(y)\right]}} + \htmlData{step=3, link=uniform}{\htmlClass{t-red}{\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_\mathsf{data}}\left[\log \mathbb{E}_{x^- \sim p_\mathsf{data}}\left[e^{f(x^-)^{\mathsf{T}} f(x) / \tau}\right]\right]}}

Eq. 2 (Theorem 1), Sec. 4.2 in Wang and Isola, arXiv 2005.10242v10; the proof is in App. A.2.

  • \mathcal L_\mathsf{contrastive}(f; \tau, M): InfoNCE with one positive and M negatives (their Eq. 1, the loss of chapter 3).
  • \htmlClass{t-green}{-\tfrac{1}{\tau}\mathbb{E}[f(x)^\mathsf{T} f(y)]}: the alignment term. It is smallest when every pair of views lands on the same point.
  • \htmlClass{t-red}{\mathbb{E}_x[\log \mathbb{E}_{x^-}[\cdots]]}: the uniformity term. For each image it measures how crowded its neighbourhood is. The paper proves that a perfectly uniform encoder, if one exists, minimizes it.
  • Careful: M here is the number of negatives, the N - 1 of chapter 3.
  • The paper also shows that, with M negatives, the gap to this limit shrinks like M^{-1/2}. More negatives bring InfoNCE closer to this clean split.

Try the right side with numbers. Take \tau = 0.5 and partners at cosine 0.9: the alignment term is -0.9/0.5 = -1.8. If every negative sits at cosine 0, the uniformity term is \log e^{0} = 0. Move half of the negatives to cosine 0.5 and it becomes \log\big((1 + e^{1})/2\big) \approx 0.62: crowding raises the loss.

Why are these the same two forces? Use the identity from the worked example, u^\mathsf{T} v = 1 - \tfrac12\|u - v\|^2 for unit vectors (derived here):

\htmlData{step=1, link=align}{\htmlClass{t-green}{-\tfrac{1}{\tau}\, u^\mathsf{T} v = \tfrac{1}{2\tau}\|u - v\|^2 - \tfrac{1}{\tau}}} \qquad \htmlData{step=2, link=uniform}{\htmlClass{t-red}{e^{u^\mathsf{T} v / \tau} = e^{1/\tau}\, e^{-\frac{1}{2\tau} \|u - v\|^2}}}

Derived here from Eq. 2 of Wang and Isola, using unit-length embeddings.

The first term is \mathcal L_\mathsf{align} with \alpha = 2, scaled and shifted. The second is the same bump as in \mathcal L_\mathsf{uniform}, with t = 1/(2\tau); the only difference is where the log sits, which the paper says does not change the best encoder. Take \tau = 0.5: then t = 1. Take \tau = 0.1: then t = 5, a much narrower bump. That one line already tells you what temperature does.

Check yourself: what would you get if you trained with \mathcal L_\mathsf{uniform} alone?

An even spread with no meaning. Nothing would ask the two views of an image to agree, so the encoder could scatter them anywhere, and the crop or the colors could decide where an image lands. When Wang and Isola fine-tune a trained encoder with only one of the two losses, its accuracy drops; with both, it rises (their Fig. 8). You need both forces.

Temperature: how local the push is

The bridge above says that a small \tau gives a narrow bump: an image only feels the neighbours right next to it, and it feels them strongly. A large \tau gives a wide bump: every image feels a gentle push from almost everyone. Chapter 7 shows the same thing from the gradient: each negative's share of the push grows like e^{s/\tau}, where s is its similarity to the anchor.

Now press the temperature buttons, then "InfoNCE", and watch the big dots. At \tau = 0.1 the sphere is covered most evenly, but the big dots, one hidden class, are spread far apart. At \tau = 1.0 they stay in a tight group, and the sphere is covered less evenly. The readout gives the numbers.

You might expect the most uniform sphere to be the best one. It is not, because of what sits next to an image. Its nearest neighbours are often images of the same kind: another mug, another dog. A small \tau pushes exactly those neighbours hardest, so it tears apart groups that you wanted to keep. Wang and Liu call this the uniformity-tolerance dilemma. Tolerance is the average similarity of two images from the same class. It needs labels, so it is only a check, never part of training.

In short, a lower \tau buys uniformity, and a higher one buys tolerance (Wang and Liu, Sec. 4.2). In the toy, most of the change happens between \tau = 0.5 and 1.0; below that the toy hardly changes. The toy has no accuracy to peak, so it cannot tell you the best \tau. For real networks the claim card below gives it.

Where this came from

Two papers built this view: Wang and Isola (ICML 2020) for the two forces, and Wang and Liu (CVPR 2021) for the temperature. Here is what each one claims, and how strong the evidence is.

Claim card: alignment and uniformity predict feature quality (Wang and Isola)

Claim
Encoders with lower \mathcal L_\mathsf{align} and \mathcal L_\mathsf{uniform} transfer better, and training on these two losses alone gives features as good as InfoNCE or better.
How
They trained many encoders per dataset (304 on STL-10, 45 on ImageNet-100), sweeping loss weights, \tau, \alpha and t, and report the best of each family with a linear probe (picked by 5-fold cross-validation; for STL-10 among the 150 encoders with 128-d output and batch 768).
Result
STL-10, small AlexNet-style encoder, linear probe on the output: InfoNCE 80.46%, align + uniform 81.15% (Table 1). ImageNet-100, ResNet-50 trained with MoCo for 240 epochs (an unofficial MoCo implementation), linear top-1: 72.80% vs 74.60% (Table 3). ImageNet-1K, ResNet-50 with MoCo v2 for 200 epochs, linear top-1: 67.5% from the official MoCo v2 code vs 67.69% (Table 5).
Caveat
On text (BookCorpus sentence embeddings, Table 4) the two losses alone are 3 to 4 points worse than InfoNCE, for example InfoNCE 77.51% vs align + uniform 73.76% on movie-review sentiment. The claim holds for the vision tables only.
Would fail if
Under a matched sweep, the align + uniform encoders lost consistently, as they do on text.

Claim card: temperature trades uniformity for tolerance (Wang and Liu)

Claim
A smaller \tau gives a more uniform but less tolerant embedding, and linear accuracy peaks in between.
How
ResNet-18 on CIFAR10, CIFAR100 and SVHN, ResNet-50 on ImageNet100; negatives from a memory bank (a stored feature for every training image, see chapter 4), not from the batch; 200 epochs, batch 128; linear probe. They sweep \tau from 0.05 to 1.0. Their uniformity column prints -\mathcal L_\mathsf{uniform}, so larger means more uniform there.
Result
CIFAR10 (Table 1) below: uniformity falls and tolerance rises as \tau grows. The best accuracy comes at \tau = 0.2 or 0.3 (Sec. 5.3).
Would fail if
Accuracy rose steadily with uniformity at every \tau, so that the most uniform model were also the best.

A different route to the same question: HaoChen et al. (NeurIPS 2021, 2106.04156) replace the softmax with a spectral contrastive loss, a linear pull on partners plus a squared push on random pairs, and prove that a linear probe on its features works well when augmentations link images within a class well and rarely link images of different classes.

Where we go next: which negatives push?

Temperature decides how the push is shared among the negatives. The next chapter looks at that share one negative at a time, and at a problem hiding in it: some of the nearest "negatives" are not negatives at all.

You should now be able to explain

  • Why normalized embeddings live on a sphere, and what alignment and uniformity measure there.
  • Why InfoNCE with many negatives is alignment plus uniformity (Theorem 1), and why alignment alone collapses.
  • How temperature sets the width of the push, and why the most uniform embedding is not the best one.

In practice

Both measurements cost two lines of code, so log them on held-out data while you train. A \mathcal L_\mathsf{uniform} that drifts toward 0 is a cheap early sign of collapse (our reading). Treat \tau as a real hyperparameter and sweep it: a small value suits tasks that must tell near-duplicates apart, a larger one suits tasks that group similar images together (our reading of the dilemma).
