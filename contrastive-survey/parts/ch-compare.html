Act IThe idea

Learning by comparing

Suppose you have a million photos and no labels at all. Can a network still learn something useful from them? This chapter answers yes, and shows the one trap you must avoid on the way.

The game: two views of one photo

Take one photo of a mug. Crop it in two different places and change the colors a little. You now have two views. They look different pixel by pixel, but they show the same mug. Now take a photo of a bicycle. Its views share nothing with the mug's views.

Here is the game you give the network: put the two views of one photo close together, and keep views of different photos apart. No one has to label anything, because you made the pairs yourself. To play well, the network must ignore what you changed (the crop, the colors) and keep what you did not change (the mug). That is exactly what we want a good feature to do.

A few words you will see on every page from here on. The network that turns an image into a vector is the encoder. Its output vector is the embedding. Two views of the same photo form a positive pair. Views of different photos form negative pairs.

First attempt: only pull

The simplest loss you could write is "the distance between the two views of each photo". Minimize it, and the views of each photo pull together. The figure below trains a small encoder this way. Eight made-up photos each have two views; each blue dot is one view, its yellow ring is the other, and a green line joins them.

Make a guess before you press "Pull only". What is the cheapest way for the encoder to make every pair close?

Press it and watch. Every point slides into the middle. The encoder found a trick: if it sends every image to the same point, every pair has distance zero and the loss is perfect. But now a mug and a bicycle get the same embedding, so the encoder has learned nothing. This failure is called collapse.

Think of a student who answers "same" to every question "are these two the same?". The student is never wrong on the positive pairs, and is useless.

Keep this picture in mind. Every method on this page is, at heart, a way to stop collapse.

The fix: also push

The student cheats because we never ask about different photos. So ask. For every negative pair, add a cost that pushes the two views apart. Give each point a personal-space bubble of radius m, called the margin: a negative pair inside the bubble pays a cost, and a negative pair outside it pays nothing.

Now press "Pull and push". Collapse has become the most expensive answer, because when everything sits on one point, every negative pair is deep inside the bubble. The cheapest answer is now the one we wanted: partners close, different photos apart.

The same idea as an equation

Here is the whole loss for one pair of inputs. Read it as "if the pair is positive, pay for distance; if it is negative, pay for closeness inside the margin".

L = \htmlData{step=1, link=pull}{(1-Y)\,\tfrac12\,\htmlClass{t-green}{D_W^2}} + \htmlData{step=2, link=push}{Y\,\tfrac12\,\htmlClass{t-red}{\{\max(0,\; \htmlData{step=3, link=margin}{m} - \htmlData{step=4, link=dist}{D_W})\}^2}}

Eq. 4 in Hadsell, Chopra and LeCun, "Dimensionality reduction by learning an invariant mapping", CVPR 2006 (written there as L(W, Y, \vec X_1, \vec X_2)).

  • D_W: the distance between the two outputs of the encoder with weights W, one output per input of the pair.
  • Y: 0 if the pair is similar (two views of one image), 1 if it is dissimilar (views of different images). Only one of the two terms is active for any pair.
  • \htmlClass{t-green}{D_W^2}: the pull. It is zero only when partners coincide.
  • \htmlClass{t-red}{\max(0, m - D_W)^2}: the push. It is zero once a dissimilar pair is at least m apart, so far-away pairs cost nothing and only near ones are pushed.

Try it with numbers. Say the margin is m = 1.

  • A positive pair at distance 0.3 pays \tfrac12 \cdot 0.3^2 = 0.045. It still wants to get closer.
  • A negative pair at distance 0.3 pays \tfrac12 (1 - 0.3)^2 = 0.245. It is inside the bubble, so it gets pushed.
  • A negative pair at distance 1.5 pays 0. It is already far enough, so the loss leaves it alone.

That last line matters more than it looks. The push only spends effort on negatives that are close. Far-away negatives teach nothing. You will meet this idea again in chapter 7, on hard negatives.

Inside the siamese network: forward, then backward

The loss needs two outputs, one per input of the pair. The network that makes them is called siamese: two copies of one encoder that share every weight. Step through one training pair from the figure above. Make a guess before you press "5 Gradients": there are two encoder copies, so how many sets of weights does one step update?

The answer to the guess: one. The two copies are the same weights, so their gradients add up into a single update. This is why the method is called siamese, after twins who share one body.

Check yourself: what would happen if you trained with the push term only?

Different photos would spread apart, but nothing would pull the two views of one photo together. The encoder would have no reason to ignore the crop and the colors, so two views of the same mug could land far apart. You need both forces: the pull teaches what to ignore, and the push stops collapse.

Where this came from

This loss is from Hadsell, Chopra and LeCun (CVPR 2006), who call it the contrastive loss, the name the whole field now carries. A year earlier, Chopra, Hadsell and LeCun had trained a siamese network for face verification. It is two copies of one network with shared weights, one per input, scored on the distance between their outputs. FaceNet (1503.03832) later used a triplet of anchor, positive and negative, and asked the positive to be closer than the negative by a margin.

Where we go next: from a margin to a lineup

The margin loss has two weak spots. It looks at one pair at a time, so each step sees very few negatives. And you must pick the margin m by hand, in units of distance.

Now picture a police lineup instead. Show the network one view (the anchor) and a row of candidates: its true partner and many strangers. Ask it to point at the partner. One question now checks many negatives at once, and "most similar of all" needs no distance scale: a softmax over the candidates measures it.

Sohn's N-pair loss (NeurIPS 2016) made exactly this change for faces and products, using class labels to form the pairs. Later work showed that N-pair is the same loss as InfoNCE, which chapter 3 builds, up to constants, when both inputs go through one encoder and the score is a dot product (Tschannen et al., 1907.13625, Sec. 4). The figure at the top of the page already plays the lineup game, and chapter 3 builds it step by step.

You should now be able to explain

  • Why two augmented views of one photo give a training signal with no labels.
  • Why pulling partners together on its own makes the encoder collapse.
  • How a push with a margin makes collapse expensive, and why far-away negatives cost nothing.

In practice

Comparison is the only signal in self-supervised pretraining, so something must always stop collapse. On this page that something is negatives (Acts I to III), a balancing constraint (chapter 8), labels (chapter 9), or a caption (chapter 10). If a contrastive model you train gives almost the same embedding for very different inputs, suspect collapse first.
