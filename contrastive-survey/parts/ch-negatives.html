Hard negatives and false negatives

InfoNCE pushes every negative away. Does it push them all equally, and what if some "negatives" are not negatives? This chapter finds out which negatives get the push, and what goes wrong when the closest ones are not really negatives.

The setup: one anchor, 24 negatives

Take one slice through the sphere of chapter 6: a circle. On it sit an anchor (blue), its positive (yellow, the other view of the same image) and 24 negatives (red). The loss treats all 24 as "not my partner". But four of them secretly show the same kind of object as the anchor, and they carry a yellow ring. The loss cannot see the rings; you can.

Make a guess before you press "Plain InfoNCE". Which negatives will get most of the push: the far ones, which the loss could still move a lot, or the near ones? And is that good?

Press it. The six near negatives get about 70% of it; each far negative gets only a sliver, though the 18 of them still add up to about 30%. And the four negatives nearest the anchor, which take the biggest bars, are the ones with yellow rings.

Who gets pushed: the gradient

Here is why. Write s_{i,j} for the similarity of anchor i to candidate j, with s_{i,i} for its positive. InfoNCE for anchor i is \mathcal L = -s_{i,i}/\tau + \log \sum_k e^{s_{i,k}/\tau}, where the sum runs over the positive and all negatives.

Take the derivative with respect to one score. The first term only touches the positive. The log-sum touches every score, and its derivative is that candidate's softmax probability P_{i,j}, divided by \tau (derived here; this is the usual softmax derivative). Wang and Liu print the result:

\htmlData{step=1, link=pull}{\htmlClass{t-green}{\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}(x_i)}{\partial s_{i,i}} = - \frac{1}{\tau} \sum_{k \neq i} P_{i,k}}}, \quad \htmlData{step=2, link=push}{\htmlClass{t-red}{\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}(x_i)}{\partial s_{i,j}} = \frac{1}{\tau} P_{i,j}}}

Eq. 4 in Wang and Liu, "Understanding the behaviour of contrastive loss", CVPR 2021, arXiv 2012.09740v2.

Divide each negative's push by the total push, and the temperature and the positive cancel out. What is left is each negative's share of the push:

\htmlData{step=2, link=share}{r_{i}(s_{i,j})} = \frac{\htmlData{step=1, link=share}{\htmlClass{t-red}{{\rm exp}(s_{i,j}/\tau)}}}{\htmlData{step=3}{\sum_{k \neq i} {\rm exp} (s_{i,k}/\tau)}}, \quad i \neq j

Eq. 5 in Wang and Liu, arXiv 2012.09740v2 (the sum runs over the negatives only).

  • P_{i,j}: the softmax probability that the loss gives candidate j of being anchor i's partner (their Eq. 2).
  • \htmlClass{t-green}{\partial\mathcal L / \partial s_{i,i}}: the pull on the positive. It is negative, so gradient descent raises the positive's similarity. Its size equals the sum of all the pushes: the loss pulls exactly as hard as it pushes.
  • \htmlClass{t-red}{\partial\mathcal L / \partial s_{i,j}}: the push on negative j. It is proportional to e^{s_{i,j}/\tau}, so it grows exponentially with how similar the negative already is.
  • r_i(s_{i,j}): negative j's share of the push, a softmax over the negatives alone. The red bars in the figure are these shares.

Try it with three negatives at similarities 0.9, 0.5 and 0.0.

  • At \tau = 0.5 the terms are e^{1.8} \approx 6.05, e^{1.0} \approx 2.72 and e^{0} = 1. The shares are 0.62, 0.28 and 0.10.
  • At \tau = 0.1 the terms are e^{9} \approx 8103, e^{5} \approx 148 and 1. The shares are 0.982, 0.018 and 0.0001. The nearest negative takes nearly everything.

This is the soft version of the margin from chapter 1. There, a negative outside the bubble got no push at all. Here, a far negative still gets some, but exponentially little. The temperature sets how sharply the push falls off, which is the "narrow bump" of chapter 6 seen from the gradient. Wang and Liu call this a hardness-aware loss: a hard negative is one the encoder already finds similar to the anchor, and those get the push.

Hard negatives: the useful ones

That is mostly a good thing. A far negative is already handled; pushing it teaches nothing. A near negative is where the encoder is still confused, so that is where learning happens. It is also one reason big batches and long queues help (chapter 4): more negatives give more chances that a few are hard (our reading).

So why not push the hard ones even harder? Robinson et al. do this with a concentration \beta \ge 0: they reweight each negative by e^{\beta s/\tau} (in our notation), divided by the average weight in the batch. Put that weight into Eq. 5, holding the weights fixed, and the share becomes e^{(1+\beta)s_{i,j}/\tau} over its sum (derived here). That is the same as using a lower temperature, \tau/(1+\beta), for the negatives only. Press "Hardness weights" to see \beta = 1: the bars near the anchor grow and the far ones vanish.

The twist: false negatives

Look again at which bars grew. Most of the extra push went to the ringed points. Without labels, the "negatives" are just other images from the batch, and some of them show the same kind of object as the anchor: another mug, another dog. These are false negatives. The loss pushes them away as hard as any stranger, and harder, because they sit close.

How many are there? If the data has C classes of equal size, a random image shares the anchor's class with probability 1/C. So about 1/C of all negatives are false (our arithmetic; the papers call this probability the class prior). We write it \pi^+, and \pi^- = 1 - \pi^+. The paper writes this prior as \tau^+. It is not a temperature; we write \pi^+ to keep \tau for temperature. With 10 classes and 510 negatives per anchor, that is about 51 false negatives. The toy has 4 of 24, which is 1/6, as if there were 6 classes, like the sphere of chapter 6.

A bigger batch does not help with this: the fraction stays at 1/C. And it is worst exactly where hard-negative weighting looks: the nearest images are the most likely to share the anchor's class. Robinson et al. see this in their results: on vision data, raising \beta does not keep improving the features (their Sec. 6.1).

Check yourself: a dataset has 5 classes of equal size. About what fraction of the negatives are false?

About one in five, 20%. With few classes, false negatives are a large part of every batch, so the problem matters most on narrow datasets with few kinds of object.

The fix: debiasing

Chuang et al. found a way to correct for false negatives without knowing which ones they are. The idea is a piece of bookkeeping. A random image is a true negative with probability \pi^- = 1 - \pi^+ and a same-class image with probability \pi^+. So the average over all images mixes the two: (mean over all) = \pi^- (mean over true negatives) + \pi^+ (mean over same-class images).

Solve for the part you want: subtract \pi^+ times the same-class average, then divide by \pi^-. You do not have same-class images, but you do have one: the positive. So the positive stands in for them. That gives the estimator that replaces the plain sum of negatives:

g = \max \Big\{ \htmlData{step=3}{\frac{1}{\pi^-}} \Big( \htmlData{step=1, link=negsum}{\htmlClass{t-red}{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N e^{f(x)^T f(u_i)}}} - \htmlData{step=2, link=subtract}{\htmlClass{t-green}{\pi^+ \frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^M e^{f(x)^T f(v_i)}}} \Big),\;\; \htmlData{step=4}{e^{-1/\tau}} \Big\}

Eq. 7 in Chuang et al., "Debiased contrastive learning", NeurIPS 2020, arXiv 2007.00224v3, written there as g(x, \{u_i\}_{i=1}^N, \{v_i\}_{i=1}^M) with temperature t and prior \tau^+; we write \tau and \pi^+ (our notation). The loss (their Eq. 8) puts N g in the denominator in place of the sum over negatives.

  • f(x)^T f(u): the score of the anchor against u. These papers fold the temperature into f, so it plays the role of s/\tau on this page.
  • Careful: here N counts negatives and M counts positives. In chapter 6, M counted negatives.
  • \htmlClass{t-red}{u_1, \dots, u_N}: the N negatives from the batch, some of them false. The red term is their average.
  • \htmlClass{t-green}{v_1, \dots, v_M}: positives that stand in for same-class images. Usually M = 1 and v_1 is the anchor's own positive. The green term is the expected share of false negatives, which is subtracted.
  • \pi^+: the class prior, the chance that a random image shares the anchor's class; \pi^- = 1 - \pi^+. The paper writes it \tau^+; we write \pi^+ (our notation). It is a hyperparameter: the paper uses 0.1 for CIFAR10 and 0.01 for ImageNet-100.
  • e^{-1/\tau}: a floor, the smallest value a single term can take (similarity -1). Without it the estimate could go negative, and the log in the loss would break.

Try it with N = 10 negatives, \pi^+ = 0.1, negative terms that add up to 30 and a positive term of 5. The plain average is 3. Subtract 0.1 \times 5 = 0.5 and divide by 0.9: g \approx 2.78, so the denominator counts N g \approx 27.8 for the negatives instead of 30. The loss has removed what it expects the false negatives to add.

Now press "Debiased". You might expect the ringed bars to shrink. They do not move at all. The estimator has no idea which negatives are false; it only knows how many to expect, so it cannot single them out. What changes is the denominator on the right: the dashed box is the subtracted part, about the size of the false negatives' block. The debiased loss lands close to the loss you would get with true negatives only, and the loss never saw the labels.

The floor matters in practice. With the low temperature of their ImageNet-100 setup (\tau = 0.07), the estimate often fell below it, and the authors then trained on the plain loss for that example instead (App. B).

Both at once: hard and debiased

Robinson et al. (the same group) combine the two ideas. They sample negatives from a distribution q_\beta that favours similar images, and correct it for false negatives with the same bookkeeping. They need no new sampler: the expectation under q_\beta becomes a reweighted average over the batch they already have.

\mathbb{E}_{x^- \sim q_\beta} [e^{f(x)^T f(x^-)}] = \htmlData{step=1, link=hard}{\mathbb{E}_{x^- \sim p} [\htmlClass{t-red}{e^{(\beta+1) f(x)^T f(x^-)}} / Z_\beta]}, \qquad \htmlData{step=2, link=hard}{\widehat{Z}_\beta = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N e^{\beta f(x)^\top f(x_i^-)}}

Unnumbered, Sec. 3.1 in Robinson et al., "Contrastive learning with hard negative samples", ICLR 2021, arXiv 2010.04592v2. The same reweighting is applied to the positive term, and the result goes into Chuang's formula.

  • q_\beta(x^-) \propto e^{\beta f(x)^\top f(x^-)} p(x^-): the hardness-tilted distribution over negatives; p is the plain data distribution.
  • \htmlClass{t-red}{e^{(\beta+1) f(x)^T f(x^-)}}: each negative's term, sharpened by \beta. This is the (1+\beta) of the share above.
  • \widehat Z_\beta: the average weight in the batch, so the weights average to 1. \beta = 0 gives Chuang's loss back; \pi^+ = 0 gives hardness without debiasing.

Try it with two negatives whose scores f(x)^T f(x^-) are 0.9 and 0.1, and \beta = 1. The plain terms e^{0.9} \approx 2.46 and e^{0.1} \approx 1.11 give shares of 0.69 and 0.31. The weights e^{\beta s} are the same two numbers; their mean is 1.78, so the normalized weights are 1.38 and 0.62. The weighted terms become e^{1.8}/1.78 \approx 3.39 and e^{0.2}/1.78 \approx 0.69, and the shares move to 0.83 and 0.17. The near negative gained, the far one lost.

The paper's own 2 x 2 test on STL10 asks whether each half helps. All four runs are SimCLR with a ResNet-50 for 400 epochs, scored by a linear readout:

Not hard (\beta = 0)80.15 (plain SimCLR)84.26

 Robinson et al., Fig. 4 (right), printed values. Hardness alone and debiasing alone help about equally here; together they help most.

Each half helps, and the two together help most. The same order holds on other datasets, but the gain shrinks when there are many negatives: on CIFAR10 it disappears at large N (Sec. 5.1).

Where this came from

Wang and Liu (CVPR 2021) wrote down the gradient and named the hardness-aware property. Chuang et al. (NeurIPS 2020) introduced debiasing; in Chuang's own runs it raised ImageNet-100 top-1 from 73.58 to 74.6 (CMC's code, \pi^+ = 0.01, Table 1) and improved SimCLR on STL10 "by 4.26%" (Sec. 5.1). Robinson et al. (ICLR 2021), from the same group, added the hardness weights. Each paper's numbers compare methods inside that paper only, so do not line them up against each other.

A related fix targets small batches instead. Decoupled contrastive learning (Yeh et al., ECCV 2022, 2110.06848) removes the positive from the denominator. In SimCLR with batch 256 and 200 epochs, it raises ResNet-50 ImageNet-1K linear top-1 from 61.8% to 65.9% (Sec. 4.2 text).

Where we go next: drop the negatives?

Negatives cost memory, need big batches, and some of them are false. A natural question follows: can you prevent collapse without any negatives at all? Chapter 8 shows two ways.

You should now be able to explain

  • Why each negative's share of the push is a softmax of its similarity, and why far negatives get almost none.
  • What a false negative is, and why about 1/C of the negatives are false when there are C equal classes.
  • How debiasing corrects the denominator without knowing which negatives are false, and why hardness weighting needs it.

In practice

False negatives matter most when your data has few kinds of object, because then 1/C is large. If you know roughly how many balanced classes there are, \pi^+ \approx 1/C is a starting point (our reading). If you have labels, use them: chapter 9 turns same-class images into positives instead of negatives. Turn hardness up gently: on CIFAR100 the best was \beta = 0.5, and the paper suggests that much larger values pick many false negatives (Sec. 6.1).
