Act IThe problem and the yardstick

Learn normal, measure surprise

You cannot train a defect classifier on defects you do not have. So flip the problem: learn only what good parts look like, and flag whatever is far from that.

Start with the scratched cap: its worst patch sits far outside the blue cloud, and the scratch lights up on the part. Now inspect a good cap and predict: with only one good cap in memory, will a second good cap pass? Press 1 and see. It fails. Nothing is wrong with the cap; memory simply has gaps, and normal patches that fall into a gap look surprising. Five caps fill most of the gaps, and twenty leave a wide margin. This is the first lesson of the field: a detector is only as good as its picture of normal.

The setting

On a real line almost every part is good, and the bad ones carry defects of kinds you cannot list in advance: a scratch today, a crack or a missing screw next month. A supervised classifier needs many examples of each kind. Anomaly detection instead trains on good parts only. This is called the one-class or unsupervised setting, and it is the main setting of this page.

The model then answers two questions for each new image. Is this part defective? That is image-level detection, one score per image. Where is the defect? That is pixel-level localization, one score per pixel, shown as a heat map. Most methods compute scores for small patches and get both answers from them: the image score is the worst patch, and the heat map is the patch scores laid back over the image.

\htmlData{step=1, link=test}{s(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{p})} = \htmlData{step=2, link=dist}{\min_{\htmlClass{t-blue}{m} \in \htmlClass{t-blue}{\mathcal{M}}} \big\lVert \phi(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{p}) - \htmlClass{t-blue}{m} \big\rVert}, \qquad \htmlData{step=3, link=max}{S(\text{image}) = \max_{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{p}\,\in\,\text{image}} s(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{p})}
  • \htmlClass{t-yellow}{p} is one patch of the image under inspection, and \phi(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{p}) is its feature vector (here a toy one; from chapter 4 on, a pretrained network's).
  • \htmlClass{t-blue}{\mathcal{M}} is the memory of normal: features of patches from good parts. \htmlClass{t-blue}{m} is one of them.
  • s(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{p}) is the patch's anomaly score: the distance to its nearest normal neighbor. The heat map is these scores, laid over the image.
  • S is the image score: the worst patch. A threshold on S gives the verdict, pass or reject.

The threshold trades two errors. Set it low and good parts get rejected (false alarms); set it high and scratched parts pass (missed defects). Drag the slider in the figure and watch both happen. Chapter 2 shows how benchmarks score a detector without picking a threshold at all.

MVTec AD, VisA, and how a detector is graded

Every method on this page reports its scores on the same benchmarks, mostly MVTec AD and VisA. To read those scores you need to know what each metric counts, and what it ignores.

The threshold slider is the detector's one free choice, and every curve shows what happens at every possible threshold at once. Now predict: if the detector misses the tiny hole completely, how much does pixel AUROC fall? And AU-PRO? Press "Miss the tiny defect". Pixel AUROC barely moves, because the hole is a few dozen pixels among thousands of defect pixels. AU-PRO drops by about a tenth, because the hole is one of ten defect regions and each region counts the same. Then press "Miss the large defect": now pixel AUROC falls sharply, and AU-PRO drops by about the same tenth as before.

Three ways to grade one score map

Image AUROC grades the image score alone. Sweep the threshold from high to low; each threshold gives a true positive rate (defective parts rejected) and a false positive rate (good parts rejected). The ROC curve plots one against the other, and AUROC is the area under it. A standard identity gives the area a plain meaning:

\htmlData{step=1, link=image}{\text{AUROC}} = \htmlData{step=2, link=pair}{P\big(S(\htmlClass{t-red}{x_{\text{defective}}}) > S(\htmlClass{t-blue}{x_{\text{good}}})\big)}
  • Pick one defective part and one good part at random. AUROC is the chance that the defective one gets the higher score. In the figure, it is the share of (red, blue) dot pairs in which the red dot sits to the right.
  • An AUROC of 1 means some threshold separates every good part from every defective part. It says nothing about which threshold that is.

Pixel AUROC is the same area, computed over pixels instead of images. The MVTec AD paper defines its true positive rate as "the percentage of pixels that were correctly classified as anomalous" (pixel ROC AUC, Sec. 4.3). The trap is the denominator. A large defect holds most of the defect pixels, so finding it lifts the curve, and a tiny defect hardly counts. MVTec AD 2 puts it directly: the true positive rate is "dominated by large anomalies" and overestimates localization quality (Sec. 4.2.1).

PRO, the per-region overlap, fixes the weighting. Split the ground truth into connected defect regions, measure what fraction of each region the prediction covers, and average over regions. Uninformed Students introduced the integrated form for MVTec AD: it integrates PRO up to a pixel false positive rate of 30%, because at high false-positive rates large parts of the image would be wrongly flagged and even a perfect PRO would mean nothing, and normalizes the area to a maximum of 1 (Sec. 4.2). MVTec AD 2 writes the formula out (Eq. 1 in MVTec AD 2 v1):

\mathrm{PRO} = \htmlData{step=1, link=region}{\frac{1}{k} \sum_{i=1}^{|D_{\text{test}}|} \sum_{j=1}^{k_i}} \frac{\htmlData{step=2, link=pred}{\lvert \htmlClass{t-red}{P_{\text{ano}}} \cap C_{i,j} \rvert}}{\htmlData{step=3, link=region}{\lvert C_{i,j} \rvert}}, \qquad \htmlData{step=4, link=fpr}{\text{AU-PRO}_{0.30} = \frac{1}{0.3}\int_0^{0.3} \mathrm{PRO}\;d\,\mathrm{FPR}}
  • C_{i,j} is the set of pixels of defect region j in test image i (the dotted outlines). k_i regions are in image i, and k is the total number of regions (the paper leaves k undefined; this is our reading).
  • \htmlClass{t-red}{P_{\text{ano}}} is the set of pixels predicted anomalous at the current threshold (red in the figure). The fraction is how much of one region the prediction covers.
  • Lowering the threshold raises PRO and also raises the false positive rate on defect-free pixels. AU-PRO integrates PRO over that rate from 0 to 0.3 and rescales to a maximum of 1 (the rescaling written as 1/0.3 is ours).

MVTec AD 2 shows the difference with three defect regions of 4, 2 and 20 pixels (Fig. 4). Find half of every region: PRO and TPR are both 0.5. Miss only the large region: PRO is 0.67 but TPR is 0.23. Find only the large region: PRO is 0.33 but TPR is 0.77. Pixel metrics reward big defects; PRO rewards finding every defect.

A fourth metric: area under the precision-recall curve

VisA reports AU-PR next to AU-ROC because AU-ROC flatters rare classes. Its appendix builds a toy test set with 100 anomalous samples and 100,000 normal ones. A detector that pays 10 false alarms for every true hit scores 99.5% AU-ROC but only 10.5% AU-PR. A second detector scores a lower 98.5% AU-ROC and a far better 90.6% AU-PR (VisA v1, App. B). When normal samples outnumber defective ones a thousand to one, as normal pixels outnumber defect pixels in a segmentation test, a high AU-ROC is cheap.

MVTec AD

Claim
A real-world benchmark for unsupervised anomaly detection: train on defect-free images only, then classify and segment defects in a test set with pixel-precise masks (MVTec AD, CVPR 2019).
How measured
15 categories: 5 textures (carpet, grid, leather, tile, wood) and 10 objects. 5,354 images: 3,629 defect-free for training and 1,725 for testing (467 good, 1,258 defective). 73 defect types, on average five per category, in 1,888 annotated regions. Image sides from 700 to 1024 pixels (Table 1).
Result
No baseline was good on every category. The paper graded with image accuracy at an estimated threshold, per-region overlap at that threshold, and pixel ROC AUC. It reports no image AUROC and no AU-PRO; those came with later papers, and they are what every method below reports.
What would falsify it as a benchmark
Saturation, when the best methods differ by less than run-to-run noise. MVTec AD 2 cites a best MVTec AD mean AU-PRO of 97.8%, and EfficientAD notes that 99.9% of MVTec AD defects lie at least partly inside a center crop of 76.6% of the image, so a method can ignore the borders and lose almost nothing.

VisA

Claim
Industrial inspection is harder than MVTec AD shows: complex structures, several objects per image, and changing positions and poses (VisA, ECCV 2022, arXiv 2207.14315v1).
How measured
12 subsets in 3 domains: complex structure (four printed circuit boards), multiple instances (capsules, candles, two kinds of macaroni), and single instance (cashew, chewing gum, two kinds of fryum). 10,821 images: 9,621 normal and 1,200 anomalous, with up to 9 anomaly classes per object. The one-class split puts 90% of the normal images in training, and the other 10% plus all anomalous images in the test set.
Result
In the VisA authors' own re-run, PaDiM with a ResNet-50 backbone reaches 94.5 image AU-ROC on MVTec AD but 87.8 on VisA, and only 11.4 pixel AU-PR on VisA (Table 2).
What would falsify it
Methods that score the same on VisA as on MVTec AD. They did not, at first; chapter 10 shows how far the gap has closed since.

After saturation: logical defects and harder scenes

MVTec LOCO adds logical anomalies: every piece looks normal, but the arrangement breaks a rule, with missing, misplaced, or surplus objects, or an incorrect number of elements in a box. It has five scenarios, among them splicing connectors, a juice bottle, a breakfast box and a box of pushpins. Localization is graded with AU-sPRO, a generalization of AU-PRO, and the official script gives logical and structural anomalies equal weight. These facts come second-hand from EfficientAD and MVTec AD 2: we could not download the LOCO paper (IJCV 2022), which has the image counts, the fifth scenario and the sPRO formula. Local methods find logical defects harder. In EfficientAD's re-run, PatchCore reaches 75.8 image AUROC on logical anomalies against 84.8 on structural ones, and EfficientAD-M 86.8 against 94.7 (EfficientAD Table 2). Chapter 10 shows what EfficientAD adds to close that gap.

MVTec AD 2 (arXiv 2503.21622v1, 2025-03-27) raises the bar again: eight scenarios and 8,004 images of 2.6 to 5 megapixels, with transparent and reflective objects, bulk goods, defects at the image borders, at least four lighting conditions per scene, and a private test set graded only on a server. Its benchmark of established methods shows the drop in one row: AU-PRO up to FPR 0.3, averaged over its seven methods, is 92.4 on MVTec AD and 52.6 on MVTec AD 2, and the best method reaches 58.7% (Table 1). It also tightens the metric to an integration limit of 0.05: for a 10-pixel defect in a 5-megapixel image, even a 5% false positive rate allows a wrong area more than 25,000 times larger than the defect (Sec. 4.2.1). At that limit, every method stays below 31% (Table 7).

Keep three things in mind for the rest of the page. Image AUROC grades ranking, not a working threshold. Pixel AUROC flatters detectors that find large defects. And a score of 99 on MVTec AD says little about a production line that looks like MVTec AD 2.

Six families on one map

The field split into families, and each family answers "what is the model of normal?" in its own way. They did not replace each other: they grew side by side, and the newest growth is in one model for many products and in vision-language models.

Predict before you press: which family started latest, and which grew fastest after it started? Press "Vision-language". It has no papers before 2023 and then becomes the largest family. "Multi-class" follows the same curve one step behind. "Normalizing flow" stays small throughout. "Memory bank" grows slowly and never fades: the oldest idea on the page is still in use.

Act IIRemember normal

Pretrained features already know what normal looks like

Chapter 1 used hand-made toy features. SPADE takes its features from a network pretrained on ImageNet photos. It then finds and outlines defects by nearest-neighbor search alone, with no training at all.

Select a patch on the scratch: its best match among the retrieved caps is still far away, so the scratch lights up. Now inspect the cap with a hole and predict: will SPADE flag the hole? It does not. The hole patch finds an almost perfect match, and the line shows where: a bolt hole on another cap. SPADE asks only "does this patch look like some normal patch?", and never "does it belong here?". Chapter 5 adds that second question.

Why ImageNet features work on defects

A convolutional network trained to classify ImageNet photos learns a general vocabulary of edges, textures and parts. Its feature maps describe every region of an image with a vector. Two clean patches of brushed metal get similar vectors; a scratch gets a vector that no clean patch has. The network never saw a defect, but it does not need to: it only needs to describe patches well enough that "unlike anything normal" shows up as distance. DN2 (arXiv 2002.10445) showed this for whole images. SPADE (Cohen and Hoshen, first posted 2020-05-05) extends it to every pixel.

Stage 1: find the most similar normal images

SPADE runs each image through a Wide-ResNet50x2 pretrained on ImageNet, after resizing to 256 pixels and cropping to 224. The output of the last block is averaged over all positions into one 2048-dimensional vector per image. For a test image, SPADE finds the K nearest normal images by this vector (K = 50 on MVTec AD). The image score is the mean squared distance to them:

\htmlData{step=1, link=img}{d(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{y})} = \htmlData{step=2, link=knn}{\frac{1}{K} \sum_{\htmlClass{t-blue}{f} \in N_K(f_{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{y}})}} \htmlData{step=3, link=dist}{\big\lVert \htmlClass{t-blue}{f} - f_{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{y}} \big\rVert^2}

Eq. 2 in SPADE v3.

  • \htmlClass{t-yellow}{y} is the test image and f_{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{y}} its pooled feature vector.
  • N_K(f_{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{y}}) is the set of the K nearest pooled features of normal training images; \htmlClass{t-blue}{f} is one of them.
  • The score is their mean squared Euclidean distance. A high score flags the whole image. This is exactly DN2's score.

This image score comes from pooled features, not from the pixel map. PaDiM and PatchCore later take the image score as the maximum of the map instead.

Stage 2: match every pixel against the retrieved images

For localization, SPADE keeps a feature vector for every pixel position, taken from the ends of the first three blocks, at 56, 28 and 14 positions per side. The fine block sees small detail; the coarse blocks see more context. All pixel vectors of the K retrieved images form a gallery G. Each test pixel is scored against that gallery only, which keeps the search small:

\htmlData{step=1, link=pix}{d(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{y}, p)} = \htmlData{step=2, link=gallery}{\frac{1}{\kappa} \sum_{\htmlClass{t-blue}{f} \in N_\kappa(F(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{y}, p))}} \htmlData{step=3, link=match}{\big\lVert \htmlClass{t-blue}{f} - F(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{y}, p) \big\rVert^2}

Eq. 3 in SPADE v3.

  • F(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{y}, p) is the pyramid feature of the test image at pixel p.
  • N_\kappa picks its \kappa nearest features in the gallery G. The paper uses \kappa = 1 in all experiments, so the score is the squared distance to the single nearest gallery feature: the red segment in the figure.
  • The map is then smoothed with a Gaussian filter of width 4 pixels.

The nearest gallery feature is a correspondence: it names one pixel in one normal image that looks like the test pixel. That is the "deep pyramid correspondences" of the title, and it is what the figure's line draws. Using all three blocks matters. With only the finest block the PRO score on MVTec AD falls to 87.74, against 91.7 with all three (Table 6).

How well it works, and what it costs

On MVTec AD, SPADE reaches a pixel AUROC of 96.0 and a PRO of 91.7 (Tables 2 and 3). Its image AUROC, from stage 1, is 85.5 (Table 1). Chapter 2 explains these three metrics. The weak spot is cost: every test image needs a search over pixel features of normal images. PaDiM's authors re-ran SPADE and measured 7.10 seconds per image on a CPU, an Intel i7-4710HQ (the PaDiM paper reports no GPU timing; chapter 6 gives the PatchCore authors' GPU re-run) and 1.4 GB of memory on MVTec AD (PaDiM, Tables VII and VIII).

Related work in this branch: Patch SVDD (arXiv 2006.16067) trains patch features with a patch-level SVDD loss plus a self-supervised task and scores each patch by its distance to the nearest normal patch; it reports an image AUROC of 0.921 on MVTec AD (Table 1). One Gaussian per patch position

When parts sit in the same place in every image, the patch at a given position always shows the same thing: a bolt hole here, plain metal there. PaDiM fits one Gaussian per patch position, so each patch is judged against what normally appears at its own position.

Start at the position where the hole is. Normal patches at this position are plain metal and look alike, so they form a tight cluster, and the dark hole lands far outside it, by either distance. Now predict: what did chapter 4's nearest-neighbor search say about this same patch? The readout gives it: a near-perfect match, because the patch looks like a bolt hole, and bolt holes are normal elsewhere on the cap. On the toy cap, judging each patch at its own position lifts the image AUROC for holes from 0.573 to 0.950 (toy numbers, 20 holed and 20 good test caps).

Distance in units of normal spread

Euclidean distance treats every direction alike. But normal variation is rarely round: at a rim position, the edge direction of a patch varies a lot from cap to cap while its brightness hardly varies. A patch that differs a little in brightness is then more surprising than one that differs a lot in edge direction. Press "A patch that fools Euclidean distance": the probe sits on the first Euclidean ring, no farther from the mean than many normal patches, yet it lies far outside the narrow side of the bell. The Mahalanobis distance measures distance in units of the normal spread along each direction:

\htmlData{step=1, link=maha}{M(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{x_{ij}})} = \sqrt{\htmlData{step=2, link=test}{(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{x_{ij}} - \htmlClass{t-blue}{\mu_{ij}})^T}\, \htmlData{step=3, link=cov}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{\Sigma_{ij}}^{-1}}\, (\htmlClass{t-yellow}{x_{ij}} - \htmlClass{t-blue}{\mu_{ij}})}

Eq. 2 in PaDiM v1.

  • \htmlClass{t-yellow}{x_{ij}} is the test patch's feature vector at position (i, j).
  • \htmlClass{t-blue}{\mu_{ij}} and \htmlClass{t-blue}{\Sigma_{ij}} are the mean and covariance of the normal features at that position.
  • Multiplying by \htmlClass{t-blue}{\Sigma_{ij}}^{-1} divides each direction by its normal variance, so directions where normal patches barely vary count the most.
  • The anomaly map is M at every position, resized and smoothed with a Gaussian of width 4. The image score is the maximum of the map.

Fitting: a mean and a covariance per position

PaDiM (Defard et al., first posted 2020-11-17, accepted at an ICPR 2020 workshop) runs the N normal images through a pretrained CNN once. At every position (i, j) it computes the sample mean of the N feature vectors, and their covariance:

\htmlData{step=1, link=cov}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{\Sigma_{ij}}} = \htmlData{step=2, link=normals}{\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{k=1}^{N} (\htmlClass{t-blue}{x^k_{ij}} - \htmlClass{t-blue}{\mu_{ij}})(\htmlClass{t-blue}{x^k_{ij}} - \htmlClass{t-blue}{\mu_{ij}})^T} + \htmlData{step=3, link=eps}{\epsilon I}

Eq. 1 in PaDiM v1.

  • \htmlClass{t-blue}{x^k_{ij}} is the feature vector of normal image k at position (i, j); \htmlClass{t-blue}{\mu_{ij}} is their mean.
  • The sum is the ordinary sample covariance: how the feature dimensions vary, and vary together, across normal images.
  • \epsilon I adds a small constant, \epsilon = 0.01, to every variance. The paper says it makes the covariance "full rank and invertible".

That last term is not cosmetic. An MVTec AD category has about 240 training images, but PaDiM's features have hundreds of dimensions. A sample covariance from fewer samples than dimensions is singular and cannot be inverted (our reading of why the paper needs the term). Nothing here is learned by gradient descent: fitting is averaging.

Which features

Each position's vector joins the outputs of the first three layers of the CNN. Fine and coarse layers see different things, and a full covariance can also capture how they vary together. Modeling that correlation adds 1.1 points of pixel AUROC and 1.8 points of PRO over adding up three separate layer scores (Table I, ResNet18). With ResNet18 the joined vector has 448 dimensions. PaDiM then keeps a random subset of them, chosen once before fitting: 100 for ResNet18 and 550 for Wide-ResNet-50-2 (out of 1792, our reading of the layer widths). Surprisingly, random dimensions beat the principal components:

The paper's explanation: PCA keeps the directions with the most variance in normal data, and those are not necessarily the directions that separate defects. Our gloss on that: a defect often shows up in a direction where normal data barely varies, which is exactly what PCA throws away and what the Mahalanobis distance weights most.

How well it works

With Wide-ResNet-50-2 and 550 random dimensions, PaDiM reaches a pixel AUROC of 97.5, a PRO of 92.1 and an image AUROC of 95.3 on MVTec AD (Tables III and V). With an EfficientNet-B5 backbone (layers 7, 20, 26) the image AUROC is 97.9; the paper gives no localization numbers for that backbone. On the texture categories, PaDiM beats SPADE by 4.0 points of pixel AUROC and 4.8 points of PRO.

The model assumes that position (i, j) shows the same part of the object in every image. The paper tests this directly. It builds a shifted copy of MVTec AD, Rd-MVTec AD, with a random rotation of up to 10 degrees and a random crop applied to training and test images, and compares pixel AUROC:

What it costs

The test cost no longer depends on the training set: one matrix product per position, whatever N is. PaDiM's measured inference time is 0.95 seconds per image against SPADE's 7.10, both on a CPU (Table VII; the paper reports no GPU timing). Fitting takes about 150 seconds per MVTec AD category, on a different CPU (a 72-thread server processor, serial implementation). The price is memory: Table VIII gives 3.8 GB for the Wide-ResNet model. Our computation explains the number: each position stores a 550-dimensional mean and a 550 by 550 covariance, 303,050 numbers or about 1.2 MB in 32-bit floats, and 3,136 positions (a 56 by 56 grid) make 3.80 GB. The memory grows with image resolution and feature size, not with the number of training images.

An earlier paper in this branch, Gaussian AD (arXiv 2005.14140), fitted a Gaussian to the pooled features of whole images at each level of the network and summed the Mahalanobis distances. It scores images well, an image AUROC of 95.8 on MVTec AD with EfficientNet-B4 (its best configuration, Table V of that paper), but has no map. PaDiM's step is to fit the Gaussians per position.

A memory bank, thinned to a coreset

PatchCore keeps every normal patch feature in one memory bank that any test patch may search, so it assumes no shape and no fixed position. To keep the search fast, it then keeps only a small subset of the bank that still covers the whole cloud.

Press Run greedy selection and watch the rule work: every new point is the bank point that is farthest from everything kept so far, and the red coverage radius shrinks with each pick. Now predict: how few points can we keep before the radius jumps? Slide down to 1%. The radius grows past the scratch's own distance, the good cap's worst patch scores as high as the scratch, and the two caps can no longer be told apart. Above a few percent, the coreset covers the cloud almost as well as the full bank, at a fraction of the search cost. In the paper's real setting the bank is far larger, so even 1% keeps enough points.

Why keep every patch?

PaDiM (chapter 5) fits one Gaussian per patch position. That assumes the parts are aligned, and it forces every position's normal variation into one ellipse. SPADE (chapter 4) keeps raw features, but its pixel memory is too large to search, so it first picks the nearest training images with a coarse global feature. PatchCore drops both shortcuts. It stores every patch feature of every normal image in one bank, and each test patch searches all of it. A scratch at any position can match normal metal from any position; a patch matches only if something like it was seen somewhere.

The features come from the middle of a frozen ImageNet network: the outputs of blocks 2 and 3 of a WideResNet50, on images resized to 256 and center-cropped to 224 pixels. The paper argues that the deepest layers have low resolution and are biased toward ImageNet's object classes, while the earliest layers are too generic and too local; its layer ablation (Fig. 4) finds the best balance in the middle. Each feature is also made locally aware: it is averaged with its neighbors in a 3 by 3 window, so a patch knows a little about its surroundings without losing resolution.

\phi_{i,j}\big(\mathcal{N}_p^{(h,w)}\big) = f_\text{agg}\Big(\big\{\phi_{i,j}(a,b) \;\big|\; (a,b)\in\mathcal{N}_p^{(h,w)}\big\}\Big), \qquad \htmlClass{t-blue}{\mathcal{M}} = \bigcup_{x_i\in\mathcal{X}_N} \mathcal{P}_{s,p}\big(\phi_j(x_i)\big)

Eq. 2 and Eq. 4 in PatchCore v2

  • \phi_{i,j}(a,b) is the feature vector of image x_i at layer j and grid cell (a,b); \mathcal{N}_p^{(h,w)} is the p \times p block of cells around (h,w).
  • f_\text{agg} is adaptive average pooling: the block's vectors become one vector.
  • \mathcal{P}_{s,p} collects these vectors at every cell (stride s = 1), and the memory bank \htmlClass{t-blue}{\mathcal{M}} is the union over all normal training images \mathcal{X}_N.

How do layers 2 and 3 become one vector? The paper says the layer-3 grid is rescaled to the layer-2 grid and the two are aggregated. The official code makes this concrete (code-only): layer 3 is upsampled bilinearly to layer 2's 28 x 28 grid, each layer's neighborhood is average-pooled to 1024 numbers, and the two vectors, laid end to end, are average-pooled again to a final 1024. That last pooling averages neighboring numbers within each layer's half, so in effect the final vector is 512 numbers from layer 2 followed by 512 from layer 3 (our reading of PyTorch's adaptive pooling windows). There is no learned projection. The interlude after this chapter steps through every shape.

The coreset: cover the cloud with few points

A category with a few hundred training images gives a bank of hundreds of thousands of vectors (our computation for the 15 MVTec AD categories: 2,845,136 in all). Searching it for every test patch is slow. Random subsampling is fast but leaves holes: rare normal patches lose all their neighbors and start to look anomalous. PatchCore instead asks for the subset with the best coverage, the one whose worst-covered bank point is as close as possible to a kept point.

\htmlData{step=1, link=core}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{\mathcal{M}_C^*} = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\mathcal{M}_C\subset\mathcal{M}}} \;\htmlData{step=2, link=bank}{\max_{\htmlClass{t-blue}{m}\in\htmlClass{t-blue}{\mathcal{M}}}}\;\htmlData{step=3, link=radius}{\htmlClass{t-red}{\min_{n\in\mathcal{M}_C} \lVert m - n \rVert_2}}

Eq. 5 in PatchCore v2 (minimax facility location)

  • \htmlClass{t-blue}{\mathcal{M}_C} is a candidate coreset of the target size: the large points in the figure.
  • For each bank point \htmlClass{t-blue}{m}, the inner \min is its distance to the nearest kept point. The \max takes the worst such point: that distance is the red coverage radius.
  • The best coreset \mathcal{M}_C^* makes the coverage radius as small as possible.

Finding the exact best subset is NP-hard, so PatchCore uses the greedy approximation from active learning. Start with an empty coreset and add, one at a time, the bank point that is farthest from the coreset so far:

\htmlData{step=1, link=pick}{m_i \leftarrow \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{m\in\mathcal{M}-\mathcal{M}_C}}\; \htmlData{step=2, link=radius}{\htmlClass{t-red}{\min_{n\in\mathcal{M}_C} \lVert \psi(m) - \psi(n) \rVert_2}}, \qquad \htmlData{step=3, link=core}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{\mathcal{M}_C} \leftarrow \htmlClass{t-blue}{\mathcal{M}_C} \cup \{m_i\}}

Algorithm 1 in PatchCore v2

  • m_i is the next pick: the white point in the figure, sitting at the far end of the red radius.
  • \psi is a random linear projection to fewer dimensions, used only to make the distance computations cheaper. The official code projects to 128 dimensions (code-only). The coreset stores the original vectors, not the projected ones.
  • The loop stops at the target size: 25%, 10% or 1% of the bank in the paper's tables. The paper does not say how the first point is chosen; the code starts from the mean distance to 10 random points (code-only).

Scoring a test image

At test time the image goes through the same frozen path, and every patch finds its nearest neighbor in the coreset. The worst patch decides the image:

\htmlData{step=1, link=test}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{m^{\text{test},*}}, m^* = \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{m^\text{test}}\in\mathcal{P}(x^\text{test})}}\;\htmlData{step=2, link=nn}{\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{m\in\mathcal{M}} \lVert \htmlClass{t-yellow}{m^\text{test}} - m\rVert_2}, \qquad \htmlData{step=3, link=nn}{\htmlClass{t-red}{s^*} = \lVert \htmlClass{t-yellow}{m^{\text{test},*}} - m^* \rVert_2}

Eq. 6 in PatchCore v2 (here \mathcal{M} is the coreset)

  • \mathcal{P}(x^\text{test}) is the set of patch features of the test image; each one's nearest-neighbor distance is its patch score, and these scores form the anomaly map.
  • \htmlClass{t-yellow}{m^{\text{test},*}} is the test patch with the largest score (the yellow point), m^* its nearest kept neighbor, and \htmlClass{t-red}{s^*} their distance (the red line).

The paper then reweights s^*: if the nearest normal neighbor m^* is itself far from its own neighbors in the bank, it was a rare normal pattern, and the score goes up.

s = \left(1-\frac{\exp \lVert m^{\text{test}, *} - m^* \rVert_2}{\sum_{m \in \mathcal{N}_b(m^*)} \exp \lVert m^{\text{test},*} - m \rVert_2}\right) \cdot s^*

Eq. 7 in PatchCore v2

Here \mathcal{N}_b(m^*) is the set of the b bank points nearest to m^*. Two caveats matter if you use the method. The paper never states b. And the official code does not implement this step at all: its image score is the plain maximum of the patch scores, each the squared distance (faiss returns squared L2) to the 1 nearest bank vector (code-only). The anomaly map is the grid of patch scores, upsampled to the input size and smoothed with a Gaussian of width 4 pixels, a value the paper says it did not tune.

Act IIIModel the density, or fake the defect

Turn normal into a perfect Gaussian

PaDiM forces normal features into one Gaussian per position, and PatchCore keeps raw examples. A normalizing flow learns an invertible warp that bends the real, oddly shaped cloud of normal features into a perfect Gaussian, so the likelihood of any feature becomes an exact number.

Start at layer 0. The small dots are normal patch features, and the blue surface under them is their density: a twisted crescent that no single Gaussian fits. Predict before you press 4: once normal is a round bell, where will the feature from the scratch land? Each layer warps the plane a little. The crescent tilts back, untwists, straightens into a thin band, and swells into a standard Gaussian. The scratch's feature rides along and ends far out in the tail, where the bell is almost flat. There, a likelihood is easy to read: a standard Gaussian's density depends only on the distance from its center.

Watch the readout while you step. The log-likelihood of the test feature never changes. What changes is its split: the height of the surface under the point, plus a correction for how much the warp has stretched the plane so far. That correction is the yellow ring.

Why a flow gives an exact likelihood

A normalizing flow \htmlClass{t-green}{f_\theta} is an invertible network: every feature \htmlClass{t-yellow}{x} maps to exactly one latent z = \htmlClass{t-green}{f_\theta}(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{x}) of the same size, and back. Train it so that normal features land on a standard normal distribution. Then the probability of any feature follows from the change-of-variables rule: the density of z under the bell, corrected for how much the flow squeezes or stretches space at \htmlClass{t-yellow}{x}.

\htmlData{step=1, link=test}{\log p_X(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{x})} = \htmlData{step=2, link=base}{\log p_Z\big(\htmlClass{t-green}{f_\theta}(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{x})\big)} + \htmlData{step=3, link=logdet}{\log \left\lvert \det \frac{\partial \htmlClass{t-green}{f_\theta}(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{x})}{\partial \htmlClass{t-yellow}{x}} \right\rvert}, \qquad z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)

Eq. 2 in FastFlow v2 (the paper prints the mean of the normal as o).

  • \htmlClass{t-yellow}{x} is a feature vector at one position of the backbone's feature map.
  • \htmlClass{t-green}{f_\theta} is the flow with trainable parameters \theta; p_Z is the standard normal density, the round bell at layer 4.
  • The determinant term is the log of the local volume change. A flow that stretches the plane around \htmlClass{t-yellow}{x} spreads the probability thinner there, and this term pays it back. In the figure it is the ring's change in area.
  • Normal features get a high \log p_X. A defect's feature lands in the tail of the bell and gets a low one: that is the anomaly score.

One coupling layer

The determinant of a large network's Jacobian is expensive. Flows avoid it with affine coupling layers: split the channels into two halves, pass one half through unchanged, and use it to scale and shift the other half. The first half is untouched, so the layer is trivially invertible, and the Jacobian is triangular: its log-determinant is just the sum of the log-scales.

\htmlData{step=1}{y_a, y_b = \operatorname{split}(y)}, \qquad \htmlData{step=2}{y'_a = y_a}, \qquad \htmlData{step=3, link=logdet}{y'_b = s(y_a) \odot y_b + b(y_a)}, \qquad \htmlData{step=4}{y' = \operatorname{concat}(y'_a, y'_b)}

Eq. 3 in FastFlow v2. The paper's Fig. 2(b) applies \exp(s) before the multiplication and runs two such couplings per step, with an activation normalization and a channel permutation; the toy above uses that exponential form, so each layer's log-determinant is the sum of s(y_a).

Here s and b are small neural networks. They can be as complex as you like, because they are never inverted: to undo the layer, compute s(y_a) and b(y_a) again from the unchanged half and solve for y_b. Stack many such layers, alternating which half is held fixed, and the flow can bend any reasonable distribution into a Gaussian. The toy's four layers do exactly that, one bend each.

What makes FastFlow two-dimensional

Earlier flows for anomaly detection, such as DifferNet and CFLOW-AD, worked on one-dimensional vectors. Flattening a feature map into a vector "destroys the inherent spatial positional relationship", in FastFlow's words, and CFLOW-AD's sliding windows cost many forward passes. FastFlow's subnets s and b are convolutional instead. Each flow step maps a whole C \times H \times W feature map to a latent of the same shape, so one pass gives a likelihood for every position at once. The steps alternate 3 \times 3 convolutions, which see a neighborhood, with cheap 1 \times 1 convolutions, which see one position.

Training maximizes the log-likelihood of normal features. The paper does not print its loss; our reading is the negative of Eq. 2, averaged over every position of every training image. At test time, FastFlow turns the latent into a map: "we sum the two-dimensional probabilities of each channel to get the final probability map and upsample it to the input image resolution using bilinear interpolation." With a ResNet backbone, three flows run on three feature scales and their maps are averaged. The paper does not say how it turns the map into one image score.

Make fake defects (CutPaste, DRAEM)

If you have no defects, manufacture plausible ones. Paste fake damage onto good images and train a network to find it, and anomaly detection becomes ordinary supervised learning.

Both methods start from a good image and damage it on purpose. CutPaste cuts a rectangle out of the image and pastes it somewhere else in the same image: drag the rectangles and see a seam appear in the weave. DRAEM makes a blob-shaped mask from Perlin noise and paints a foreign texture into it: move the threshold to grow or shrink the blob, and lower the opacity to make the fake fainter and harder. Neither fake looks much like a real scratch. Keep that in mind; it is the central question of this chapter.

CutPaste: learn to spot an edit, then forget the classifier

CutPaste trains an ordinary classifier on two classes: the good image x, and its edited copy \mathrm{CP}(x). A seam in a weave or a bolt hole in the wrong place is an irregularity, and to spot it the network has to learn what regular looks like.

\htmlData{step=1}{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CP}} = \mathbb{E}_{x \in \mathcal{X}}\big\{} \htmlData{step=2}{\mathbb{CE}\big(g(\htmlClass{t-blue}{x}), 0\big)} + \htmlData{step=3, link=mask}{\mathbb{CE}\big(g(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{\mathrm{CP}(x)}), 1\big)} \htmlData{step=1}{\big\}}

Eq. 1 in CutPaste v1. \mathcal{X} is the set of good images, g a binary classifier and \mathbb{CE} the cross-entropy. The best variant has three classes: normal, large patch, and scar.

Then comes the twist: the classifier is thrown away. Its job was only to teach the network useful features. To score a test image, CutPaste fits one Gaussian to the features f(x) of the good training images and measures how unlikely a test feature is under it:

\htmlData{step=1}{\log p_{\mathrm{gde}}(x)} \propto \htmlData{step=2}{\Big\{ -\tfrac{1}{2} \big(f(x) - \htmlClass{t-blue}{\mu}\big)^{\top} \htmlClass{t-blue}{\Sigma}^{-1} \big(f(x) - \htmlClass{t-blue}{\mu}\big) \Big\}}

Eq. 2 in CutPaste v1: a Gaussian density estimator, with mean \htmlClass{t-blue}{\mu} and covariance \htmlClass{t-blue}{\Sigma} of good images' features. The bracket is minus half a squared Mahalanobis distance, the same distance as PaDiM's.

With a ResNet-18 trained from scratch, the three-class version reaches 95.2 image AUROC on MVTec AD, and an ensemble of five reaches 96.1. Fine-tuning an ImageNet-pretrained EfficientNet-B4 with CutPaste gives 96.6. For a heat map, CutPaste trains a second model on small crops and scores every patch, with a separate Gaussian per location for aligned objects; that model reaches 96.0 pixel AUROC. The per-location Gaussian is PaDiM's idea again.

The most interesting result is a picture. In a two-dimensional map of the learned features (Fig. 3 of the paper), the pasted fakes sit far from the real defects, yet the features still separate real defects from good parts. The authors read it this way: CutPaste "is still not a perfect simulation of real defects", and "learning on it to find irregularity generalizes well on unseen anomalies."

DRAEM: repair the fake, then compare

DRAEM adds a second idea. A reconstructive subnet learns to repair the fake, mapping \htmlClass{t-yellow}{I_a} back to the clean image \htmlClass{t-blue}{I}. A discriminative subnet then looks at the input and its repair side by side and outputs a pixel map of where they disagree. The segmenter does not learn what a fake looks like. It learns how far an image is from its own repaired version, and that distance also exists for real defects. First, the fake (hover the terms to light the panels):

\htmlData{step=1, link=beta}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{I_a}} = \htmlData{step=2, link=image}{\overline{M}_a \odot \htmlClass{t-blue}{I}} + \htmlData{step=3, link=mask}{(1-\beta)\,(M_a \odot \htmlClass{t-blue}{I})} + \htmlData{step=4, link=texture}{\beta\,(M_a \odot \htmlClass{t-brown}{A})}

Eq. 4 in DRAEM v2. M_a is the thresholded Perlin mask and \overline{M}_a its inverse; \htmlClass{t-brown}{A} is a texture from the Describable Textures Dataset after three random augmentations; the opacity \beta is drawn uniformly from [0.1, 1.0]. The paper draws the noise threshold at random too; the slider makes it a control.

Training minimizes two losses together. The repair must match the clean image, measured by structural similarity plus squared error. The map must match the fake's mask, measured by a focal loss, a cross-entropy that down-weights pixels that are already easy:

\htmlData{step=1}{L = \underbrace{\lambda L_{\mathrm{SSIM}}(I, I_r) + l_2(I, I_r)}_{L_{rec}}} \htmlData{step=2}{\; + \; L_{seg}(M_a, M)}

Eq. 2 and 3 in DRAEM v2. The paper gives no value for \lambda (the official code uses 1) and does not print the focal loss; it cites Lin et al. 2017, and the code uses focusing parameter 2.

At test time there is no fake: the real image goes in, and the map M_o is the anomaly map. The image score is the largest value of the map after a mean filter, so one stray pixel cannot decide the verdict:

\htmlData{step=1}{\eta = \max\big(M_o \ast f_{s_f \times s_f}\big)}

Eq. 5 in DRAEM v2. The filter size s_f is not in the paper; the official code uses 21.

On MVTec AD, one model per product, DRAEM reaches 98.0 image AUROC, 97.3 pixel AUROC and 68.4 pixel average precision. In the same table, DRAEM's own run of PaDiM has the higher pixel AUROC, 97.4, but only 55.0 average precision. DRAEM argues for average precision for exactly the reason in chapter 2: the false-positive rate is swamped by the huge number of normal pixels.

Does realism matter?

Predict before you press the buttons. Which hurts DRAEM more: fakes that look nothing like real defects, or removing the repair-and-compare design?

The design matters far more than realism. Flat color blobs still score 96.2 and rectangles 96.9, both above the PaDiM number DRAEM prints, 95.5. But train the segmenter alone on the same fakes, without the repair subnet, and it falls to 93.9. The authors blame "overfitting of the discriminative sub-network to the simulated anomalies".

That is the risk of the whole family: the model can learn your fakes instead of real defects. A segmenter trained to find blobs of foreign texture finds blobs of foreign texture. Both papers soften the risk with an extra step that is not about the fakes: CutPaste discards its classifier and keeps a Gaussian on the features, and DRAEM compares an image with its own repair. Both also fail on the same kind of defect. CutPaste lists missing and flipped components among its failure cases, and DRAEM writes that "the absence of a part of the object is especially difficult to detect". A missing part leaves no foreign texture to find. Chapter 10 attacks that case directly.

Act IVFast, and one model for all

A student that fails only on defects

Train a small network to copy the features of a bigger one, but show it only good parts. Where the copy goes wrong, the part contains something the student never learned.

Start with the scratch: the student has never seen a scratch, so its copy of the teacher's features breaks there, and the student-versus-teacher map lights up. Now predict before you press the last button: the extra hole looks exactly like the six real holes. Which map will catch it? Not the student-versus-teacher map, which sees one small patch at a time, and an ordinary hole is normal for a patch. The autoencoder map catches it, because the autoencoder squeezes the whole part through a narrow code and has only ever reconstructed six holes in six places.

Distillation, from scratch

Knowledge distillation trains a student network to reproduce the outputs of a fixed teacher network. For anomaly detection, the student sees only good parts. On a good part the student has learned to match the teacher. On a defect it was never trained, so its output drifts away from the teacher's, and the squared difference between them is an anomaly map. Uninformed Students (1911.02357) introduced this student-teacher idea. EfficientAD (WACV 2024, first posted 2023-03-25) makes it fast and adds a second map for a different kind of defect.

Speed comes from the network shape. In EfficientAD-S, both teacher and student are a patch description network (PDN) of only four convolutional layers; EfficientAD-M uses a deeper, wider PDN. Each output vector sees a 33x33 pixel patch, and the teacher outputs 384 channels. The paper times the PDN at under 800 microseconds for a 256x256 image on one GPU. The teacher gets good features by distillation too: once, on ImageNet, it learns to copy the features that PatchCore uses from a frozen WideResNet-101.

Two tricks that make the student fail only off the normal data

A plain student generalizes too well: with enough capacity it learns to imitate the teacher on defects too, and the map goes quiet. EfficientAD adds two pressures. The first is the hard feature loss. Of all the squared differences between teacher and student outputs, it trains only on the largest ones, the elements at or above the p_\text{hard} quantile. With 0.999, that is on average about ten percent of the values along each of the three dimensions. The student stops polishing easy background and spends its capacity on the hard normal structures.

\htmlData{step=1, link=local}{D_{c,w,h} = \big(\htmlClass{t-brown}{T(I)_{c,w,h}} - \htmlClass{t-green}{S(I)_{c,w,h}}\big)^2}, \qquad \htmlData{step=2, link=hard}{L_\text{hard} = \operatorname{mean}\{\, D_{c,w,h} \;:\; D_{c,w,h} \ge d_\text{hard} \,\}}

Sec. 3.2 in EfficientAD v3. The paper writes these inline and unnumbered; L_\text{hard} is defined in words there and transcribed here.

  • \htmlClass{t-brown}{T(I)} and \htmlClass{t-green}{S(I)} are the teacher's and student's feature maps for a good training image I; c, w, h index channel and position.
  • D is the squared difference at every element. Averaged over channels it is the local anomaly map at test time.
  • d_\text{hard} is the p_\text{hard}-quantile of all elements of D; only elements at or above it get a gradient. p_\text{hard} = 0 is the plain student-teacher loss.

The second pressure is a penalty on ImageNet images. In every step the student also sees one random ImageNet picture P, and the loss pushes its output on that picture toward zero. So the student learns to imitate the teacher on good parts and to say nothing elsewhere.

\htmlData{step=1, link=hard}{L_\text{ST} = L_\text{hard}} \htmlData{step=2, link=student}{+ \; (CWH)^{-1} \textstyle\sum_c \lVert \htmlClass{t-green}{S(P)_c} \rVert_F^2}

Sec. 3.2 in EfficientAD v3 (inline). C, W, H are the channel count and map size; \lVert\cdot\rVert_F^2 sums the squares over a whole channel map.

The cumulative ablation (Table 4) adds the tricks one at a time and scores the mean of three benchmarks: added after the map normalization, the hard feature loss gains + 1.0 point of image AU-ROC. The table in the figure shows the whole p_\text{hard} sweep: the default is the best setting, and much higher values train on too little.

Logical defects need a global view

Some defects are not damage at all. MVTec LOCO, a benchmark from the same group, has logical anomalies: missing, misplaced or surplus objects, or a wrong combination of normal objects. Every patch of such a part can look normal. A student that sees 33x33 patches cannot notice that a part has one cable too many.

So EfficientAD adds an autoencoder A. It takes the whole image, squeezes it through a code of 64 numbers, and reconstructs the teacher's feature map. It cannot store where each detail sits, so it reconstructs the typical layout, and where the part breaks the layout, its output disagrees with the image. Autoencoders also blur fine detail on good parts. To cancel that, the student gets a second set of 384 output channels (768 in all) that learns to predict the autoencoder's output. It learns the autoencoder's normal blur, but not its failures on anomalies.

The two maps live on different scales. EfficientAD calibrates each one on held-out good images: the 0.9 quantile of its normal scores maps to 0 and the 0.995 quantile maps to 0.1. Then it averages the two maps and takes the maximum as the image score. The targets change nothing about ranking; they put both maps on one color scale.

Limits

The authors name two. Fine-grained logical defects stay hard: a screw two millimeters too long needs measurement, not a learned map. And unlike nearest-neighbor methods, EfficientAD trains per product, mostly so the autoencoder can learn the layout. Its ancestors and cousins in the student-teacher family are Uninformed Students, STPM (2103.04257), Reverse Distillation (2201.10703) and AST (2210.07829). AST makes student and teacher different networks so that the student fails off the normal data; EfficientAD gets the same failure from the loss instead, at no cost at test time.

One model for every product

Every method so far trains one model per product, and a factory has hundreds of products. One model for all of them sounds easy, but a single reconstruction model fails in a specific way: it learns to copy its input, defects included.

Start with full attention and select a token on the scratch. Predict first: if a token may look at itself, where will it look? At itself, and at the overlapping tokens beside it that look almost the same: nothing is more similar to a token than the token. So every token is rebuilt from itself, the scratch is copied, and the error map stays nearly blank. Now press "Neighbor mask". The token cannot see itself or its neighbors, so it must rebuild itself from look-alikes elsewhere on the mesh. Normal texture has look-alikes everywhere; the scratch has none outside its own window, so it lights up.

One model per product does not scale

The setting so far has been separate: one model per category, trained on that category's good images. UniAD (NeurIPS 2022, first posted 2022-06-08) asks for the unified setting: one model trained on good images of all 15 MVTec AD categories together, with no category label at training or test time. The benchmarks now call this the multi-class setting.

UniAD is a reconstruction method. A network learns to rebuild the features of good images; at test time, whatever it fails to rebuild is anomalous. That works only if the network rebuilds normal content and nothing else. In practice it can learn a shortcut: return the input unchanged. The paper calls this the identical shortcut. The model then rebuilds defects as well as anything else, and the error map goes blank. Learning the joint appearance of fifteen products is hard, so in the unified setting copying becomes the easy way out. The paper shows the effect directly: its baseline networks drive the training loss toward zero while the test AUROC peaks early and then falls.

\htmlData{step=1, link=copy}{\bm{y} = \htmlClass{t-blue}{\bm{x}^+}\,\bm{w} + \bm{b}}, \qquad \htmlData{step=2, link=query}{\bm{y} = \operatorname{softmax}\!\big(\htmlClass{t-green}{\bm{q}}\,(\htmlClass{t-blue}{\bm{x}^+})^{T} / \sqrt{C}\big)\,\htmlClass{t-blue}{\bm{x}^+}}

Eq. 1 and Eq. 2 in UniAD v3.

  • Left: a fully connected layer rebuilds the K normal tokens \htmlClass{t-blue}{\bm{x}^+} (each with C channels). Mean squared error is minimized by \bm{w} \to I, \bm{b} \to 0: the identity, which copies an anomalous input \bm{x}^- just as well. A convolution is the same trap.
  • Right: attention with a learned query \htmlClass{t-green}{\bm{q}}. To rebuild the input, the attention map must point each output at its own input token, so \htmlClass{t-green}{\bm{q}} must be tied to what normal tokens look like. A query trained on normal data cannot point at an anomalous token, so the anomaly is not copied.

Three ways to stop the copy

UniAD rebuilds frozen CNN features with a transformer that is built so that it cannot copy:

  1. A learned query in every decoder layer. Each of the 4 decoder layers starts from its own learned query embedding and reads the encoder output through attention, so no output is a direct function of its own input token.
  2. Neighbor-masked attention. In every attention map, a token may not attend to keys in its own 7 by 7 neighborhood, itself included, on the 14 by 14 token grid. The figure's mask is this rule.
  3. Feature jittering. During training, Gaussian noise is added to the input tokens, while the target stays the clean feature. Training becomes denoising, and a copy of a noisy input is a bad answer.
\htmlData{step=1}{D \sim \mathcal{N}\!\Big(\mu = 0,\ \sigma^2 = \big(\alpha\,\tfrac{\lVert \bm{f}_{tok} \rVert_2}{C}\big)^2\Big)}

Eq. 3 in UniAD v3. \bm{f}_{tok} is one token, C its channel count, \alpha the jitter scale (20), added with probability 1.

Which fix matters most? The ablation (Table 5a, unified MVTec AD, image AUROC) answers it. Without any query, the transformer scores 69.5. A query in the first decoder layer only, as in a vanilla transformer, lifts it to 87.6, and a query in every layer to 95.0. The neighbor mask on top of one query alone reaches 96.1, and all three together 96.5. The mask size matters too: masking only the token itself (1 by 1) gives 94.6, because the neighbors still leak the token's content.

Ask CLIP whether the part is damaged

Every method so far needed good images of the product before it could judge one. CLIP already pairs images with words such as "flawless" and "damaged", so WinCLIP asks it directly, window by window, with no image of the product at all.

Press Slide the windows. Each small window covers four patches and gets one score; the scratch lights up the windows that touch it. Now predict: what does the whole-image window say about this part? Press Whole image. It says "flawless". One scratch is a small part of the whole image, so the whole-image embedding barely moves. For the same reason, scoring whole images works for detection only when defects are large; small windows are what make a map possible.

Then compare Harmonic mean with Arithmetic mean. Every pixel is covered by several windows. The arithmetic mean smears a defect window's score over all of its pixels, so the red area grows well past the scratch. The harmonic mean is pulled down hard by any confidently normal window, so a pixel stays normal unless every window that covers it looks damaged. Last, try the cap: the toy scorer also flags the designed rim, because a generic idea of "damaged" does not know which edges belong to this product. WinCLIP reports the same kind of failure on real data, for example designed holes on a circuit board flagged as defects.

Zero-shot means no images, not no knowledge

CLIP was trained to place an image and its caption close together in one embedding space. You know how CLIP classifies with no training: embed one text prompt per class, embed the image, and take a softmax over the cosine similarities. WinCLIP (CVPR 2023, arXiv v1 of 2023-03-26) uses exactly two classes, normal and anomalous.

\htmlData{step=1, link=prob}{p(\text{anomalous}\mid \htmlClass{t-yellow}{x})} = \frac{\exp\big(\langle \htmlData{step=2, link=win}{f(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{x})}, \htmlData{step=3, link=text}{\htmlClass{t-red}{g_a}} \rangle / \tau\big)}{\exp\big(\langle f(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{x}), \htmlClass{t-blue}{g_n} \rangle / \tau\big) + \exp\big(\langle f(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{x}), \htmlClass{t-red}{g_a} \rangle / \tau\big)}

  • \htmlClass{t-yellow}{x} is the image, or one window of it; f is the frozen CLIP image encoder, and \langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle is cosine similarity.
  • \htmlClass{t-blue}{g_n} and \htmlClass{t-red}{g_a} are text embeddings for "normal" and "anomalous", built from many prompts (below); \tau is CLIP's temperature.
  • The output is a number between 0 and 1: the bars in the figure. With only the two bare prompts "normal [object]" and "anomalous [object]", this scores 74.0 image AUROC on MVTec AD (Table 1).

Many prompts, averaged into two

One prompt is a noisy probe. WinCLIP writes a compositional prompt ensemble: 7 normal states (such as "flawless [o]" and "[o] without defect"), 4 anomalous states (such as "damaged [o]" and "[o] with flaw"), and 22 sentence templates (such as "a photo of a [c] for visual inspection."). Every state goes into every template, which gives 154 normal and 88 anomalous prompts (our computation). The text encoder embeds each one, and the embeddings of each class are averaged into a single prototype.

\htmlData{step=1, link=text}{\htmlClass{t-red}{g_a} = \frac{1}{|S_a|\,|T|} \sum_{s \in S_a} \sum_{t \in T} g\big(t(s(o))\big)}, \qquad \htmlData{step=2, link=text}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{g_n} \text{ likewise over } S_n}

Our notation for "the average of text embeddings per label" (WinCLIP Sec. 4.1); the paper prints no formula.

  • o is the product name from the dataset, such as "bottle". So zero-shot here means no images of the product, but the name is still used. AnomalyCLIP, in the next chapter, removes it.
  • S_a, S_n are the state phrases; T is the set of templates; g is the frozen CLIP text encoder.

The ablation in Table 3 shows where the gain comes from. On MVTec AD, image AUROC goes from 74.0 with two bare prompts to 89.8 with the state words, 90.8 with the templates as well, and 91.8 with multi-crop prediction. Scoring against the normal prompt alone, with no anomalous class, gives only 34.2: the contrast between the two classes is what carries the signal.

Windows instead of patch tokens

Why not score CLIP's patch tokens directly? CLIP aligns only its class token with text. Its last-layer patch tokens score 22.4 pixel AUROC on MVTec AD, worse than chance. So WinCLIP builds many small images. The backbone is OpenCLIP ViT-B/16+, trained on LAION-400M, at an input of 240 pixels; that gives 225 patch tokens, a 15 by 15 grid. A window keeps a square of patches and drops all the others before the forward pass. Small windows are 32 pixels on a side (2 by 2 patches), mid windows 48 pixels (3 by 3), and both move with a stride of 16 pixels. That is 196 small and 169 mid windows, plus the whole image (our computation, assuming no padding). Each window's class token goes through Eq. 1, and its score is painted onto every pixel of the window.

\htmlData{step=1, link=harm}{\bar{\mathbf{M}}^{\tt W}_{0,ij}} = \left(\htmlData{step=2, link=win}{\frac{1}{\sum_{u,v} (\mathbf{w}_{uv})_{ij}} \sum_{u,v} \frac{(\mathbf{w}_{uv})_{ij}}{\mathbf{M}^{\tt W}_{0,uv}}}\right)^{-1}

Eq. 3 in WinCLIP v1: the harmonic mean, at pixel (i, j), of the scores of all windows that cover it.

  • (\mathbf{w}_{uv})_{ij} is 1 when window (u, v) covers pixel (i, j), and 0 otherwise.
  • \mathbf{M}^{\tt W}_{0,uv} is the zero-shot score of window (u, v). Dividing by it makes a near-zero (confidently normal) window dominate, which is the paper's stated intent: weight "more on scores towards normality".
  • The maps of the three scales are combined by a second harmonic mean. Without harmonic averaging, MVTec AD pixel AUROC drops from 85.1 to 81.5 (Table 8). Removing the small, mid or image scale gives 84.7, 84.0 and 82.0.

The price is many forward passes per image: zero-shot WinCLIP takes 389.4 ms per image on an Amazon EC2 G4dn instance (Table 8; the paper names neither the GPU model nor the batch size).

Some defects are defined only against a reference, such as a part that is flipped upside down. Learned prompts

A prompt reaches CLIP's text encoder as a sequence of word embeddings, one vector per token. "Prompt learning" (as in CoOp, an earlier method for CLIP) replaces some of those vectors with free vectors and trains them by gradient descent, while every CLIP weight stays frozen. The words are gone; only the vectors remain, and they can sit anywhere in the embedding space, not only at real words.

AnomalyCLIP (ICLR 2024, arXiv v1 of 2023-10-29; this page cites v12 of 2026-01-01) learns two such prompts. The only fixed words are "object" and "damaged".

\htmlData{step=1, link=prompt-n}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{g_n} = [\htmlClass{t-green}{V_1}][\htmlClass{t-green}{V_2}]\dots[\htmlClass{t-green}{V_E}]}\htmlData{step=3, link=object}{[\text{object}]}, \qquad \htmlData{step=2, link=prompt-a}{\htmlClass{t-red}{g_a} = [\htmlClass{t-green}{W_1}][\htmlClass{t-green}{W_2}]\dots[\htmlClass{t-green}{W_E}][\text{damaged}]}\htmlData{step=4, link=object}{[\text{object}]}

Object-agnostic templates, AnomalyCLIP v12 Sec. 3.2 (unnumbered). The object-aware version puts the product name [cls] where these say [object].

  • \htmlClass{t-green}{V_i} and \htmlClass{t-green}{W_i} are learned word embeddings, E = 12 per prompt. They are the main trained weights.
  • \htmlClass{t-blue}{g_n} and \htmlClass{t-red}{g_a} are what the frozen text encoder makes of each prompt: one "normal" and one "abnormal" embedding, used exactly as in WinCLIP's two-class softmax.
  • "object" replaces the product name, which, in the paper's words, blocks out the class semantics of objects. The prompts must then encode what damage looks like on anything.

The paper also adds learned tokens deeper in the text encoder: 4 fresh vectors overwrite the first positions of each of the first 9 text layers, and the encoder's outputs at those positions are thrown away. This lets the prompt shape the text embedding at several depths, not only at the input.

How much does the word "object" matter by itself? Less than the headline suggests. The paper prints the gain of object-agnostic over object-aware prompt learning as differences only (Fig. 5): 0.8 points of image AUROC on MVTec AD and 0.3 on VisA, but 4.4 on MPDD. Making CLIP's patch tokens local again

To draw a map, AnomalyCLIP compares the text embeddings with every patch token, not only the class token. The last chapter showed why that fails in plain CLIP: its last-layer patch tokens are not local. In CLIP's attention, many patches attend to the same few tokens, so each patch's output is mostly someone else's content. The fix, DPAM (diagonally prominent attention map), changes how attention weights are computed and adds no weights at all:

a_{ij} = e^{\,q_i k_j^{\top}/\sqrt{D}} \;\longrightarrow\; a^{vv}_{ij} = e^{\,v_i v_j^{\top}/\sqrt{D}}

Standard attention weights and the V-V weights DPAM uses, AnomalyCLIP v12 Appendix C (unnumbered).

The paper argues that a value vector, which takes no part in the original attention map, scores highest with itself, so a^{vv}_{ii} tends to dominate its row: each patch mostly keeps its own content, and the attention map becomes diagonally prominent. DPAM runs from the 6th visual layer. In the paper's overview figure it sits in a separate path that feeds only the patch tokens, and the class token keeps the original attention (our reading of that figure). In the module ablation, DPAM alone raises MVTec AD pixel AUROC from 46.8 to 68.4 and leaves image AUROC unchanged, as it should.

\htmlData{step=1, link=total}{L_{total}} = \htmlData{step=2, link=global}{L_{global}} + \htmlData{step=3, link=local}{\lambda \textstyle \sum_{M_l \in \mathcal{M}} L_{local}^{M_l}}, \qquad \htmlData{step=4, link=local}{L_{local} = \mathrm{Focal}\big(\mathrm{Up}([S_{n}, S_{a}]), S\big) + \mathrm{Dice}\big(\mathrm{Up}(S_{n}), I - S\big) + \mathrm{Dice}\big(\mathrm{Up}(S_{a}), S\big)}

Eq. 2 and the unnumbered local loss, AnomalyCLIP v12 Sec. 3.3 (layer superscripts dropped in the second part). Hover a term to open that stage of the diagram below.

  • L_{global} is a cross-entropy between the image-level probability of "abnormal" and the image label. The paper describes it in words only.
  • S_n, S_a are the per-patch probabilities of normal and abnormal, upsampled (Up) to the image size; S is the ground-truth defect mask and I a matrix of ones.
  • Focal loss copes with the imbalance (defects are small); the two Dice terms ask the normal map to cover the normal region and the abnormal map to cover the defect. \lambda = 4.

A CLIP method beating it at equal shots on both datasets. On VisA image AUROC that partly happens: APRIL-GAN scores 91.2 with one image, against 87.4 for AnomalyDINO at 672 pixels.

The method is PatchCore with each part re-chosen for a tiny memory. AnomalyDINO (WACV 2025; arXiv v1 of 2024-05-23, read at v3) runs DINOv2 ViT-S, with 21 million parameters and 14-pixel patches, on images whose shorter edge is 448 pixels. Every patch token of the good images goes into the memory. Each test patch scores by its cosine distance to the nearest memory patch, and the image scores by its worst patches:

Claim
A plain reconstruction model on frozen DINOv2 features, with a few deliberately simple parts, closes most of the gap between one model for all categories and one model per category.
How measured
One model trained on the good images of all categories at once: 3,629 training images for MVTec AD's categories; also VisA and Real-IAD. Compared with Dinomaly trained one model per category, and with earlier multi-class methods (Dinomaly says most of these rows come from the ADer benchmark; see the caption).
Result
Image AUROC with one model for all: 99.6 on MVTec AD, 98.7 on VisA, 89.3 on Real-IAD. One model per category: 99.7, 98.9, 92.0.
What would falsify it
A clear drop from one-per-category to one-for-all. On MVTec AD and VisA there is almost none; on Real-IAD, with its many categories and camera views, a drop remains.

Recall the problem from the UniAD chapter: a reconstruction model trained on many products learns to copy its input, so defects are reconstructed too. UniAD fought copying with a neighbor mask and feature noise. Dinomaly (CVPR 2025; arXiv v1 of 2024-05-23, read at v5) keeps the same goal and swaps each part for something simpler. Its encoder is a frozen ViT-Base/14 trained by DINOv2-R (DINOv2 with 4 register tokens), at a 392-pixel crop; the decoder tries to rebuild the features of its middle 8 layers.

The decoder has 8 Transformer layers with linear attention. Softmax attention can put almost all of its weight on one token, including the token itself, which is exactly an identity copy. Linear attention has no softmax, so it cannot form such a sharp peak:

\mathrm{LA}(Q, K, V) = \big(\phi(Q)\,\phi(K)^{\top}\big)V = \phi(Q)\big(\phi(K)^{\top}V\big), \qquad \phi(x) = \mathrm{elu}(x) + 1

Eq. 3 in Dinomaly v5 (the paper writes \phi(K^{T}), and names \mathrm{elu}(x) + 1 as the usual choice of \phi).

Each output must draw on a summary of all tokens, \phi(K)^{\top}V, rather than on its own position, which is what a reconstruction decoder for anomaly detection wants. The ablation makes the title's point. The framework with none of the four parts, already on DINOv2, scores 98.41 image AUROC on MVTec AD, above UniAD's 96.5; all four parts give 99.60. And UniAD's own tricks nearly match Dinomaly's at their best settings: feature jitter instead of dropout gives 99.59, and softmax attention with a neighbor mask gives 99.56. Our reading: most of the gain over UniAD comes from the backbone. The encoder ablation agrees: the same model on MAE features scores 96.27, on DINOv2-R 99.60.

The cost is a ViT. The base model has 148.0M parameters (including the frozen encoder, by our reading) and needs 104.7G multiply-accumulate operations per image, for 58.1 images per second on an RTX3090 (Table 4). That is throughput with batches of images, not the latency of one image, so Dinomaly is not on the latency chart in chapter 15.

Speed versus accuracy, desktop and Jetson

Accuracy tables hide the other half of the choice: a line that inspects twenty parts per second gives each part only 50 milliseconds. Here are the published timings, device by device, with the accuracy each paper reports next to them.

Why the families differ so much

Latency follows from the model of normal. A memory bank pays for a nearest-neighbor search over its stored patches, so PatchCore's cost grows with the bank; that is why its coreset matters (chapter 6). PaDiM pays for one covariance matrix per patch position. On a desktop CPU, PaDiM with a heavy backbone takes 22553 ms per image; with a MobileNetV2 backbone (PaDiM-Edge) it takes 35 ms, and with diagonal covariances on top (PaDiM-Lite) 24 ms. MVTec AD image AUROC falls along the way, from 0.96 to 0.94 to 0.85. A student-teacher method needs only two small networks; EfficientAD's patch description network is the smallest model on the desktop chart. Vision-language methods carry a large backbone: on a Jetson Orin Nano Super with TensorRT FP16, a CLIP ViT-L/14 image encoder alone takes 130.26 ms, before any anomaly scoring.

Latency and throughput are different numbers. Latency is the time for one image, which is what a part on a moving line waits for. Throughput is images per second with a batch, which suits offline checks. EfficientAD-S reaches 614 images per second with batching on the RTX A6000, against a latency of 2.2 ms. The EfficientAD paper also warns that parameter counts mislead: a convolution's cost depends on how large its input feature map is, not only on how many weights it has.

On a Jetson

Only one source times PatchCore, EfficientAD and a DINO-based method on the same Jetson Orin Nano: a 2026 benchmark of continual learning. EfficientAD* takes 34.7 ms, the DINOv3 coreset method DINOSaur 91.6 ms, and PatchCore* with a ResNet-18 119.0 ms. Their accuracy comes from a continual protocol, where products arrive one after another; there EfficientAD* keeps its speed but its MVTec AD image AUROC collapses to 0.529. That is a statement about continual learning, not about EfficientAD on one product.

Zero-shot detection on a Jetson needs a small backbone. EdgeZSAD runs at 32.83 ms on a Jetson Orin Nano Super with TensorRT FP16 and reaches 91.6 image AUROC on MVTec AD, scored on the device. Zero-shot here means that no MVTec AD image is used in training; its training set, Real-IAD-DR, still shares some category names with MVTec AD (our reading). Compression is not free: on a Jetson Xavier NX, quantizing the student to FP16 with TensorRT (the teacher stays FP32) cuts reverse distillation from 166.60 ms in PyTorch FP32 to 19.68 ms, but its mean AUROC falls from 0.96 to 0.82, while STFPM keeps 0.94 at 24.98 ms. The family tree

Every paper this page read, placed by family and date. Filled nodes are the papers read in full; hollow nodes are branches, read for one idea and one number. Lines join a paper to the one it changed.

Read the tree from left to right and three phases stand out. Until 2021 the field learned to use pretrained features well: nearest neighbors, Gaussians and flows on top of an ImageNet backbone, plus fake defects to train on. From 2022 it chased cost: one model for every product, and models small enough for a line. From 2023 the vision-language row fills up, because a model that already knows the word "damaged" needs no images of your product. The map in chapter 3 counts the same shift across the whole arXiv scan.

The scoreboard and what to use when

One table for every method read in full. Sort by any column, but compare only within a setting: a zero-shot score and a score from a model trained on the product answer different questions.

Three things to read off the table. First, on MVTec AD the strongest one-class methods all land between 98.0 (DRAEM) and 99.4 (FastFlow) image AUROC, so the benchmark no longer separates them; MVTec AD 2, built to be harder, has a best AU-PRO of 58.7% at the usual false positive limit of 0.30; its own, stricter metric uses 0.05 (chapter 2). Second, AU-PRO sits lower and spreads wider than pixel AUROC, as chapter 2 predicts: WinCLIP scores 85.1 pixel AUROC but only 64.6 AU-PRO on MVTec AD. Third, the zero-shot rows trail the trained rows by several points on images and by more on AU-PRO: having no images of the product has a real cost.

If you have this, start here

  • Hundreds of good images of one product, and a desktop GPU. Start with PatchCore: 99.1 image AUROC on MVTec AD with nothing to train. If the line is fast, EfficientAD-S gives 98.8 at 2.2 ms on an RTX A6000 (one image at a time, float16). If defects can be logical (a missing, extra or misplaced part), prefer EfficientAD: in the EfficientAD paper, EfficientAD-M scores 90.7 on MVTec LOCO against 80.3 for the authors' re-run of PatchCore.
  • Many products and one model. Dinomaly reaches 99.6 image AUROC with one model for all MVTec AD classes, against UniAD's 96.5. PatchCore also survives this setting (98.8 in ADer), but its MVTec AD test took 9h22m in total there, and it ran out of memory on VisA.
  • A handful of good images. AnomalyDINO with one image per product gives 96.5 on MVTec AD and 85.6 on VisA, against WinCLIP+ at 93.1 and 83.8.
  • No image of the product. AnomalyCLIP (91.5 image AUROC, 91.1 pixel AUROC on MVTec AD) localizes defects better than WinCLIP (91.8 and 85.1). Treat either as a start: collect good images and move up the list.
  • A Jetson at line speed. On a Jetson Orin Nano, the continual-learning versions timed in one benchmark take 34.7 ms (EfficientAD*) and 119.0 ms (PatchCore* with a ResNet-18) (chapter 15). For zero-shot, EdgeZSAD runs at 32.83 ms on an Orin Nano Super with TensorRT FP16. Measure accuracy again after every export to TensorRT or INT8.
