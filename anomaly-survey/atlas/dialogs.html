One-class setting

The model trains on images of good parts only, with no defect images and no labels. It must then judge new images that may contain defects of any kind. The literature also calls this the unsupervised setting. It matches a real line, where defects are rare, varied and often new.

Variants in this tour: synthetic defects added to the good images (chapter 5), one model for many products (chapter 7), a handful of good images (few-shot) and no image of the product at all (zero-shot, chapter 8).

Feature vector

A list of numbers that describes a patch of the image. Similar-looking patches get similar vectors, so distance between vectors measures how different two patches look.

The real methods take their features from a network pretrained on ImageNet photos (or on CLIP or DINOv2 data). The toy in this tour uses three hand-made features per 16 by 16 pixel patch of the toy image: horizontal-versus-vertical edges, diagonal edges and brightness.

Anomaly score

A number per patch, or per image, that is high when the input looks unlike normal. Each family in this tour computes it differently: a distance to stored features, a Mahalanobis distance, a low likelihood, a predicted defect probability, a student-teacher disagreement, a reconstruction error, or a similarity to the word "damaged".

The image score is usually the worst patch score. The patch scores laid over the image form the anomaly map, shown as a heat map.

Threshold

The score above which a part is rejected. It trades two errors. A low threshold rejects good parts (false alarms). A high threshold lets defective parts pass (missed defects).

Benchmarks avoid choosing a threshold: AUROC and AU-PRO grade the score at every threshold at once (chapter 2). A real line must still choose one, usually from held-out good images.

Nearest neighbor (kNN)

k-nearest-neighbor search: for a query vector, find the k stored vectors closest to it. In anomaly detection the stored vectors are features of good patches, and the distance to the nearest one (k = 1) is the anomaly score. Nothing is trained; fitting means storing features.

The cost is the search: every test patch is compared with every stored patch. That is why PatchCore thins its memory to a coreset (chapter 3).

AUROC

Area under the receiver operating characteristic curve. Sweep the threshold from high to low. At each threshold, the true positive rate is the share of defective samples flagged, and the false positive rate is the share of good samples flagged. The ROC curve plots one against the other; AUROC is the area under it.

A plain reading: pick one defective and one good sample at random. AUROC is the chance that the defective one scores higher. 1 is perfect; 0.5 is a coin flip. Image AUROC grades one score per image. Pixel AUROC grades one score per pixel. Papers print it as 0 to 1 or as 0 to 100.

MVTec AD

The standard benchmark of the field (CVPR 2019): 15 categories, 5 textures and 10 objects. 5,354 images: 3,629 good images for training and 1,725 for testing, of which 1,258 are defective. The defects come with pixel-precise masks, so both detection and localization can be graded.

VisA (ECCV 2022) is the second common benchmark: 12 subsets and 10,821 images, with complex structures and several objects per image.

PRO and AU-PRO

PRO is the per-region overlap. Split the true defect mask into connected regions. At a threshold, measure the share of each region that the detector flags, and average over all regions. A tiny scratch counts as much as a large stain.

AU-PRO is the area under the PRO curve, drawn against the false positive rate on defect-free pixels. Uninformed Students (1911.02357) integrates it up to a false positive rate of 30% and normalizes the area to a maximum of 1, because a detector is useless at high false-positive rates anyway. MVTec AD 2 moves the limit to 0.05.

Pretrained backbone

A convolutional network or vision transformer trained on a large photo collection, usually ImageNet classification, and then frozen. Its intermediate feature maps describe every region of an image with a vector. Middle layers work best for defects: early layers are too local and generic, and the last layers are biased toward ImageNet's object classes.

In this tour the backbone is brown: frozen. Methods such as SPADE, PaDiM and PatchCore train nothing at all on top of it.

Gaussian

The normal distribution, a bell-shaped density. In several dimensions it has a mean (the center of the bell) and a covariance matrix (how wide the bell is in each direction, and which directions vary together). Its contours are ellipses, not circles, when the features vary more in some directions than in others.

Mahalanobis distance

Distance measured in units of the normal spread. For a mean mu and covariance Sigma, the distance of x is sqrt((x - mu)^T Sigma^-1 (x - mu)). Multiplying by the inverse covariance divides each direction by its normal variance, so a small step in a direction where good samples barely vary counts as much as a large step where they vary a lot.

Euclidean distance treats every direction alike, so it misses a defect that moves a feature only along a direction that is normally very still.

Coreset

A small subset of the memory bank chosen so that every bank point stays close to some kept point. The worst such distance is the coverage radius. Finding the best subset is hard (minimax facility location), so PatchCore uses a greedy rule: start from one point and repeatedly add the bank point farthest from the current coreset.

Random subsampling is cheaper but leaves holes: rare normal patches lose their neighbors and start to look anomalous. The greedy coreset keeps the rare ones.

Normalizing flow

An invertible network f that maps every input x to a latent z of the same size, and back. Train it so that normal features land on a standard Gaussian. Then the change-of-variables rule gives the exact density of any feature: the Gaussian density at f(x), times how much f stretches or squeezes space at x (the absolute Jacobian determinant).

Earlier flows for anomaly detection (DifferNet, CFLOW-AD) worked on vectors; FastFlow works on whole two-dimensional feature maps.

Affine coupling layer

Split the input into two halves a and b. Keep a unchanged, and transform b as b' = b * exp(s(a)) + t(a), where s and t are small neural networks. The layer is trivially invertible: compute s(a) and t(a) again from the unchanged half and solve for b. Its Jacobian is triangular, so the log-determinant is just the sum of s(a).

Stacking many couplings, alternating which half is kept, lets the flow bend a complex distribution into a Gaussian.

Perlin noise

Smooth random noise made by interpolating random gradients on a coarse grid. Thresholding it gives irregular, blob-shaped regions with natural-looking edges, unlike rectangles. DRAEM uses these blobs as masks for its fake anomalies and fills them with a texture from the Describable Textures Dataset.

Knowledge distillation

Train a student network to reproduce the outputs of a fixed teacher network. In anomaly detection the student sees only good images, so it learns to imitate the teacher only on normal content; where the two disagree, something is new. Uninformed Students (1911.02357) introduced this student-teacher idea for MVTec AD.

EfficientAD distills twice: first a small teacher from a large frozen ImageNet network, once; then, per product, a student from that teacher.

Autoencoder

A network that squeezes its input through a narrow code and rebuilds it. Trained on good images only, it learns to rebuild normal content; parts it cannot rebuild are candidates for anomalies. Because the code is small, it captures the typical global layout but blurs fine detail.

EfficientAD's autoencoder rebuilds the teacher's feature map from a code of 64 numbers, which is why it notices a part that is in the wrong place.

Multi-class setting

One model is trained on the good images of many products (all categories of a benchmark) at once, with no category label at training or test time. It must then judge any of those products. UniAD called this the unified setting; later benchmarks such as ADer call it multi-class.

The opposite is the separate setting: one model per product, which is what most methods in this tour assume.

Attention

In a transformer, each output token is a weighted average of value vectors from all tokens. The weights come from a softmax over the similarity between the token's query and every token's key. A token is usually most similar to itself, so with full attention it can simply copy itself.

A mask sets some weights to zero before the softmax. UniAD's neighbor mask hides each token's own neighborhood, so the token must be rebuilt from the rest of the image.

CLIP

Contrastive language-image pre-training: an image encoder and a text encoder trained together on image-caption pairs, so that an image and its caption land close together in one embedding space. To classify with no training, embed one text prompt per class, embed the image, and take a softmax over the cosine similarities.

CLIP aligns only its class token with text, not its patch tokens; that is why WinCLIP scores whole windows and AnomalyCLIP changes the attention to make patch tokens local again.

Prompt

The text given to CLIP's text encoder, such as "a photo of a flawless bottle". Its embedding acts as the classifier weight for that class. A prompt ensemble averages the embeddings of many phrasings. Prompt learning replaces some word embeddings with free vectors and trains them by gradient descent, while CLIP itself stays frozen.

Zero-shot

The model judges a product without any image of that product: no good images and no defect images. It relies on what it already knows, here CLIP's link between images and words such as "damaged". Learned-prompt methods such as AnomalyCLIP still train on labelled images of other products.

Few-shot

The model gets only a handful of good images of the product, typically 1 to 16 (the "shots"), and no defect images. Memory-based methods store the patch features of those images; vision-language methods add them as a reference next to the text prompts (WinCLIP+).

DINOv2

A vision transformer (ViT) trained by self-supervised learning, with no labels, on a large curated image collection; the scaled-up successor of DINO. Its patch tokens already separate parts, textures and backgrounds, which makes them strong features for nearest-neighbor search. AnomalyDINO uses the small ViT-S model with 14-pixel patches.

Jetson

A family of small embedded computers with an integrated GPU, used for vision on the factory floor next to the camera. The papers in this tour time models on the Jetson Orin Nano, the Orin Nano Super and the Xavier NX. The same method is usually much slower there: EfficientAD takes 2.2 ms on the EfficientAD paper's desktop GPU, and 34.7 ms on a Jetson Orin Nano in a separate benchmark. A method that keeps up with a line on a desktop may not keep up on a Jetson.

Quantization

Storing and computing a network's weights and activations with fewer bits: 16-bit floats or 8-bit integers instead of 32-bit floats. It cuts latency and memory on small devices, but it can change the outputs. For anomaly detection, where the score is a small difference between two networks, that change can hide defects, so accuracy must be measured again after every export.

Harmonic mean

For scores p1 ... pn, the harmonic mean is n / (1/p1 + ... + 1/pn). One small value pulls it down hard: if any window that covers a pixel looks confidently normal, the pixel stays normal. The arithmetic mean instead spreads one damaged window's score over every pixel it covers, so the red area grows past the defect.

Continual-learning protocol

Products arrive one after another, and the model must learn each new product without forgetting the earlier ones, usually with a small replay memory. Accuracy measured this way mixes detection quality with forgetting, so it is not comparable with the one-model-per-product numbers elsewhere in this tour.

Quantile

The q quantile of a set of numbers is the value below which a share q of them falls. The 0.999 quantile of the squared teacher-student differences is the level that only the largest 0.1% exceed. EfficientAD also uses quantiles of the scores on held-out good images to put its two maps on one scale.
