Act IThe problem and the yardstick

Learn normal, measure surprise

You cannot train a defect classifier on defects you do not have. So flip the problem: learn only what good parts look like, and flag whatever is far from that.

Start with the scratched cap: its worst patch sits far outside the blue cloud, and the scratch lights up on the part. Now inspect a good cap and predict: with only one good cap in memory, will a second good cap pass? Press 1 and see. It fails. Nothing is wrong with the cap; memory simply has gaps, and normal patches that fall into a gap look surprising. Five caps fill most of the gaps, and twenty leave a wide margin. This is the first lesson of the field: a detector is only as good as its picture of normal.

The setting

On a real line almost every part is good, and the bad ones carry defects of kinds you cannot list in advance: a scratch today, a crack or a missing screw next month. A supervised classifier needs many examples of each kind. Anomaly detection instead trains on good parts only. This is called the one-class or unsupervised setting, and it is the main setting of this page.

The model then answers two questions for each new image. Is this part defective? That is image-level detection, one score per image. Where is the defect? That is pixel-level localization, one score per pixel, shown as a heat map. Most methods compute scores for small patches and get both answers from them: the image score is the worst patch, and the heat map is the patch scores laid back over the image.

\htmlData{step=1, link=test}{s(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{p})} = \htmlData{step=2, link=dist}{\min_{\htmlClass{t-blue}{m} \in \htmlClass{t-blue}{\mathcal{M}}} \big\lVert \phi(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{p}) - \htmlClass{t-blue}{m} \big\rVert}, \qquad \htmlData{step=3, link=max}{S(\text{image}) = \max_{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{p}\,\in\,\text{image}} s(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{p})}
  • \htmlClass{t-yellow}{p} is one patch of the image under inspection, and \phi(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{p}) is its feature vector (here a toy one; from chapter 4 on, a pretrained network's).
  • \htmlClass{t-blue}{\mathcal{M}} is the memory of normal: features of patches from good parts. \htmlClass{t-blue}{m} is one of them.
  • s(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{p}) is the patch's anomaly score: the distance to its nearest normal neighbor. The heat map is these scores, laid over the image.
  • S is the image score: the worst patch. A threshold on S gives the verdict, pass or reject.

The threshold trades two errors. Set it low and good parts get rejected (false alarms); set it high and scratched parts pass (missed defects). Drag the slider in the figure and watch both happen. Chapter 2 shows how benchmarks score a detector without picking a threshold at all.
