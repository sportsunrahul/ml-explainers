Act IVFast, and one model for all

A student that fails only on defects

Train a small network to copy the features of a bigger one, but show it only good parts. Where the copy goes wrong, the part contains something the student never learned.

Start with the scratch: the student has never seen a scratch, so its copy of the teacher's features breaks there, and the student-versus-teacher map lights up. Now predict before you press the last button: the extra hole looks exactly like the six real holes. Which map will catch it? Not the student-versus-teacher map, which sees one small patch at a time, and an ordinary hole is normal for a patch. The autoencoder map catches it, because the autoencoder squeezes the whole part through a narrow code and has only ever reconstructed six holes in six places.

Distillation, from scratch

Knowledge distillation trains a student network to reproduce the outputs of a fixed teacher network. For anomaly detection, the student sees only good parts. On a good part the student has learned to match the teacher. On a defect it was never trained, so its output drifts away from the teacher's, and the squared difference between them is an anomaly map. Uninformed Students (1911.02357) introduced this student-teacher idea. EfficientAD (WACV 2024, first posted 2023-03-25) makes it fast and adds a second map for a different kind of defect.

Speed comes from the network shape. In EfficientAD-S, both teacher and student are a patch description network (PDN) of only four convolutional layers; EfficientAD-M uses a deeper, wider PDN. Each output vector sees a 33x33 pixel patch, and the teacher outputs 384 channels. The paper times the PDN at under 800 microseconds for a 256x256 image on one GPU. The teacher gets good features by distillation too: once, on ImageNet, it learns to copy the features that PatchCore uses from a frozen WideResNet-101.

Two tricks that make the student fail only off the normal data

A plain student generalizes too well: with enough capacity it learns to imitate the teacher on defects too, and the map goes quiet. EfficientAD adds two pressures. The first is the hard feature loss. Of all the squared differences between teacher and student outputs, it trains only on the largest ones, the elements at or above the p_\text{hard} quantile. With 0.999, that is on average about ten percent of the values along each of the three dimensions. The student stops polishing easy background and spends its capacity on the hard normal structures.

\htmlData{step=1, link=local}{D_{c,w,h} = \big(\htmlClass{t-brown}{T(I)_{c,w,h}} - \htmlClass{t-green}{S(I)_{c,w,h}}\big)^2}, \qquad \htmlData{step=2, link=hard}{L_\text{hard} = \operatorname{mean}\{\, D_{c,w,h} \;:\; D_{c,w,h} \ge d_\text{hard} \,\}}

Sec. 3.2 in EfficientAD v3. The paper writes these inline and unnumbered; L_\text{hard} is defined in words there and transcribed here.

  • \htmlClass{t-brown}{T(I)} and \htmlClass{t-green}{S(I)} are the teacher's and student's feature maps for a good training image I; c, w, h index channel and position.
  • D is the squared difference at every element. Averaged over channels it is the local anomaly map at test time.
  • d_\text{hard} is the p_\text{hard}-quantile of all elements of D; only elements at or above it get a gradient. p_\text{hard} = 0 is the plain student-teacher loss.

The second pressure is a penalty on ImageNet images. In every step the student also sees one random ImageNet picture P, and the loss pushes its output on that picture toward zero. So the student learns to imitate the teacher on good parts and to say nothing elsewhere.

\htmlData{step=1, link=hard}{L_\text{ST} = L_\text{hard}} \htmlData{step=2, link=student}{+ \; (CWH)^{-1} \textstyle\sum_c \lVert \htmlClass{t-green}{S(P)_c} \rVert_F^2}

Sec. 3.2 in EfficientAD v3 (inline). C, W, H are the channel count and map size; \lVert\cdot\rVert_F^2 sums the squares over a whole channel map.

The cumulative ablation (Table 4) adds the tricks one at a time and scores the mean of three benchmarks: added after the map normalization, the hard feature loss gains + 1.0 point of image AU-ROC. The table in the figure shows the whole p_\text{hard} sweep: the default is the best setting, and much higher values train on too little.

Logical defects need a global view

Some defects are not damage at all. MVTec LOCO, a benchmark from the same group, has logical anomalies: missing, misplaced or surplus objects, or a wrong combination of normal objects. Every patch of such a part can look normal. A student that sees 33x33 patches cannot notice that a part has one cable too many.

So EfficientAD adds an autoencoder A. It takes the whole image, squeezes it through a code of 64 numbers, and reconstructs the teacher's feature map. It cannot store where each detail sits, so it reconstructs the typical layout, and where the part breaks the layout, its output disagrees with the image. Autoencoders also blur fine detail on good parts. To cancel that, the student gets a second set of 384 output channels (768 in all) that learns to predict the autoencoder's output. It learns the autoencoder's normal blur, but not its failures on anomalies.

\htmlData{step=1, link=ae}{L_\text{AE} = (CWH)^{-1} \textstyle\sum_c \lVert \htmlClass{t-brown}{T(I)_c} - \htmlClass{t-green}{A(I)_c} \rVert_F^2}, \qquad \htmlData{step=2, link=global}{L_\text{STAE} = (CWH)^{-1} \textstyle\sum_c \lVert \htmlClass{t-green}{A(I)_c} - \htmlClass{t-green}{S'(I)_c} \rVert_F^2}

Sec. 3.3 in EfficientAD v3 (inline). The training loss is L_\text{ST} + L_\text{AE} + L_\text{STAE}.

  • \htmlClass{t-green}{A(I)} is the autoencoder's reconstruction of the teacher's features (not of pixels).
  • \htmlClass{t-green}{S'(I)} is the student's second half of channels. At test time (A - S')^2, averaged over channels, is the global map; (T - S)^2 is the local map.

Two maps, one score

The two maps live on different scales. EfficientAD calibrates each one on held-out good images: the 0.9 quantile of its normal scores maps to 0 and the 0.995 quantile maps to 0.1. Then it averages the two maps and takes the maximum as the image score. The targets change nothing about ranking; they put both maps on one color scale.

\htmlData{step=1, link=local}{\hat M = 0.1\,\dfrac{M - q_a}{q_b - q_a}}, \qquad \htmlData{step=2, link=combine}{M = 0.5\,\hat M_\text{ST} + 0.5\,\hat M_\text{AE}}, \qquad \htmlData{step=3, link=combine}{m_\text{image} = \max_{i,j} M_{i,j}}

On MVTec AD the gain is small, because the benchmark is nearly saturated. The gap opens on MVTec LOCO, where the global map earns its keep. The speed gap is larger still. On an RTX A6000 at batch size 1, with every network in float16, EfficientAD-S takes 2.2 ms per image and processes 614 images per second at batch 16; EfficientAD-M takes 4.5 ms. The re-run PatchCore takes 32 ms and AST 53 ms. Table 1 pairs these latencies with a mean AU-ROC over MVTec AD, VisA and MVTec LOCO (95.4 for S, 96.0 for M), not with MVTec AD alone; chapter 15 puts all the methods on one chart.

Inside EfficientAD: forward, then backward

Training has three stages. Stage 1 happens once, on ImageNet, and makes the teacher. Stage 2 happens per product and trains the student and the autoencoder. Stage 3 only measures quantiles. The first figure is stage 1.

Limits

The authors name two. Fine-grained logical defects stay hard: a screw two millimeters too long needs measurement, not a learned map. And unlike nearest-neighbor methods, EfficientAD trains per product, mostly so the autoencoder can learn the layout. Its ancestors and cousins in the student-teacher family are Uninformed Students, STPM (2103.04257), Reverse Distillation (2201.10703) and AST (2210.07829). AST makes student and teacher different networks so that the student fails off the normal data; EfficientAD gets the same failure from the loss instead, at no cost at test time.
