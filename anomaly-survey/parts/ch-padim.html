One Gaussian per patch position

When parts sit in the same place in every image, the patch at a given position always shows the same thing: a bolt hole here, plain metal there. PaDiM fits one Gaussian per patch position, so each patch is judged against what normally appears at its own position.

Start at the position where the hole is. Normal patches at this position are plain metal and look alike, so they form a tight cluster, and the dark hole lands far outside it, by either distance. Now predict: what did chapter 4's nearest-neighbor search say about this same patch? The readout gives it: a near-perfect match, because the patch looks like a bolt hole, and bolt holes are normal elsewhere on the cap. On the toy cap, judging each patch at its own position lifts the image AUROC for holes from 0.573 to 0.950 (toy numbers, 20 holed and 20 good test caps).

Distance in units of normal spread

Euclidean distance treats every direction alike. But normal variation is rarely round: at a rim position, the edge direction of a patch varies a lot from cap to cap while its brightness hardly varies. A patch that differs a little in brightness is then more surprising than one that differs a lot in edge direction. Press "A patch that fools Euclidean distance": the probe sits on the first Euclidean ring, no farther from the mean than many normal patches, yet it lies far outside the narrow side of the bell. The Mahalanobis distance measures distance in units of the normal spread along each direction:

\htmlData{step=1, link=maha}{M(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{x_{ij}})} = \sqrt{\htmlData{step=2, link=test}{(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{x_{ij}} - \htmlClass{t-blue}{\mu_{ij}})^T}\, \htmlData{step=3, link=cov}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{\Sigma_{ij}}^{-1}}\, (\htmlClass{t-yellow}{x_{ij}} - \htmlClass{t-blue}{\mu_{ij}})}

Eq. 2 in PaDiM v1.

  • \htmlClass{t-yellow}{x_{ij}} is the test patch's feature vector at position (i, j).
  • \htmlClass{t-blue}{\mu_{ij}} and \htmlClass{t-blue}{\Sigma_{ij}} are the mean and covariance of the normal features at that position.
  • Multiplying by \htmlClass{t-blue}{\Sigma_{ij}}^{-1} divides each direction by its normal variance, so directions where normal patches barely vary count the most.
  • The anomaly map is M at every position, resized and smoothed with a Gaussian of width 4. The image score is the maximum of the map.

Fitting: a mean and a covariance per position

PaDiM (Defard et al., first posted 2020-11-17, accepted at an ICPR 2020 workshop) runs the N normal images through a pretrained CNN once. At every position (i, j) it computes the sample mean of the N feature vectors, and their covariance:

\htmlData{step=1, link=cov}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{\Sigma_{ij}}} = \htmlData{step=2, link=normals}{\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{k=1}^{N} (\htmlClass{t-blue}{x^k_{ij}} - \htmlClass{t-blue}{\mu_{ij}})(\htmlClass{t-blue}{x^k_{ij}} - \htmlClass{t-blue}{\mu_{ij}})^T} + \htmlData{step=3, link=eps}{\epsilon I}

Eq. 1 in PaDiM v1.

  • \htmlClass{t-blue}{x^k_{ij}} is the feature vector of normal image k at position (i, j); \htmlClass{t-blue}{\mu_{ij}} is their mean.
  • The sum is the ordinary sample covariance: how the feature dimensions vary, and vary together, across normal images.
  • \epsilon I adds a small constant, \epsilon = 0.01, to every variance. The paper says it makes the covariance "full rank and invertible".

That last term is not cosmetic. An MVTec AD category has about 240 training images, but PaDiM's features have hundreds of dimensions. A sample covariance from fewer samples than dimensions is singular and cannot be inverted (our reading of why the paper needs the term). Nothing here is learned by gradient descent: fitting is averaging.

Which features

Each position's vector joins the outputs of the first three layers of the CNN. Fine and coarse layers see different things, and a full covariance can also capture how they vary together. Modeling that correlation adds 1.1 points of pixel AUROC and 1.8 points of PRO over adding up three separate layer scores (Table I, ResNet18). With ResNet18 the joined vector has 448 dimensions. PaDiM then keeps a random subset of them, chosen once before fitting: 100 for ResNet18 and 550 for Wide-ResNet-50-2 (out of 1792, our reading of the layer widths). Surprisingly, random dimensions beat the principal components:

The paper's explanation: PCA keeps the directions with the most variance in normal data, and those are not necessarily the directions that separate defects. Our gloss on that: a defect often shows up in a direction where normal data barely varies, which is exactly what PCA throws away and what the Mahalanobis distance weights most.

How well it works

With Wide-ResNet-50-2 and 550 random dimensions, PaDiM reaches a pixel AUROC of 97.5, a PRO of 92.1 and an image AUROC of 95.3 on MVTec AD (Tables III and V). With an EfficientNet-B5 backbone (layers 7, 20, 26) the image AUROC is 97.9; the paper gives no localization numbers for that backbone. On the texture categories, PaDiM beats SPADE by 4.0 points of pixel AUROC and 4.8 points of PRO.

Does it need perfectly aligned parts?

The model assumes that position (i, j) shows the same part of the object in every image. The paper tests this directly. It builds a shifted copy of MVTec AD, Rd-MVTec AD, with a random rotation of up to 10 degrees and a random crop applied to training and test images, and compares pixel AUROC:

Misalignment hurts every method, and PaDiM less than SPADE. The position prior helps on aligned parts but is not a hard requirement: the features themselves are fairly tolerant of small shifts. The toy cannot show this effect: rotating the toy caps by up to 10 degrees leaves the per-position model's hole detection intact, because the toy's two features barely change under small rotations.

What it costs

The test cost no longer depends on the training set: one matrix product per position, whatever N is. PaDiM's measured inference time is 0.95 seconds per image against SPADE's 7.10, both on a CPU (Table VII; the paper reports no GPU timing). Fitting takes about 150 seconds per MVTec AD category, on a different CPU (a 72-thread server processor, serial implementation). The price is memory: Table VIII gives 3.8 GB for the Wide-ResNet model. Our computation explains the number: each position stores a 550-dimensional mean and a 550 by 550 covariance, 303,050 numbers or about 1.2 MB in 32-bit floats, and 3,136 positions (a 56 by 56 grid) make 3.80 GB. The memory grows with image resolution and feature size, not with the number of training images.

An earlier paper in this branch, Gaussian AD (arXiv 2005.14140), fitted a Gaussian to the pooled features of whole images at each level of the network and summed the Mahalanobis distances. It scores images well, an image AUROC of 95.8 on MVTec AD with EfficientNet-B4 (its best configuration, Table V of that paper), but has no map. PaDiM's step is to fit the Gaussians per position.
