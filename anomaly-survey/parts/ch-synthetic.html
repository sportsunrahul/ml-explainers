Make fake defects (CutPaste, DRAEM)

If you have no defects, manufacture plausible ones. Paste fake damage onto good images and train a network to find it, and anomaly detection becomes ordinary supervised learning.

Both methods start from a good image and damage it on purpose. CutPaste cuts a rectangle out of the image and pastes it somewhere else in the same image: drag the rectangles and see a seam appear in the weave. DRAEM makes a blob-shaped mask from Perlin noise and paints a foreign texture into it: move the threshold to grow or shrink the blob, and lower the opacity to make the fake fainter and harder. Neither fake looks much like a real scratch. Keep that in mind; it is the central question of this chapter.

CutPaste: learn to spot an edit, then forget the classifier

CutPaste trains an ordinary classifier on two classes: the good image x, and its edited copy \mathrm{CP}(x). A seam in a weave or a bolt hole in the wrong place is an irregularity, and to spot it the network has to learn what regular looks like.

\htmlData{step=1}{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CP}} = \mathbb{E}_{x \in \mathcal{X}}\big\{} \htmlData{step=2}{\mathbb{CE}\big(g(\htmlClass{t-blue}{x}), 0\big)} + \htmlData{step=3, link=mask}{\mathbb{CE}\big(g(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{\mathrm{CP}(x)}), 1\big)} \htmlData{step=1}{\big\}}

Eq. 1 in CutPaste v1. \mathcal{X} is the set of good images, g a binary classifier and \mathbb{CE} the cross-entropy. The best variant has three classes: normal, large patch, and scar.

Then comes the twist: the classifier is thrown away. Its job was only to teach the network useful features. To score a test image, CutPaste fits one Gaussian to the features f(x) of the good training images and measures how unlikely a test feature is under it:

\htmlData{step=1}{\log p_{\mathrm{gde}}(x)} \propto \htmlData{step=2}{\Big\{ -\tfrac{1}{2} \big(f(x) - \htmlClass{t-blue}{\mu}\big)^{\top} \htmlClass{t-blue}{\Sigma}^{-1} \big(f(x) - \htmlClass{t-blue}{\mu}\big) \Big\}}

Eq. 2 in CutPaste v1: a Gaussian density estimator, with mean \htmlClass{t-blue}{\mu} and covariance \htmlClass{t-blue}{\Sigma} of good images' features. The bracket is minus half a squared Mahalanobis distance, the same distance as PaDiM's.

With a ResNet-18 trained from scratch, the three-class version reaches 95.2 image AUROC on MVTec AD, and an ensemble of five reaches 96.1. Fine-tuning an ImageNet-pretrained EfficientNet-B4 with CutPaste gives 96.6. For a heat map, CutPaste trains a second model on small crops and scores every patch, with a separate Gaussian per location for aligned objects; that model reaches 96.0 pixel AUROC. The per-location Gaussian is PaDiM's idea again.

The most interesting result is a picture. In a two-dimensional map of the learned features (Fig. 3 of the paper), the pasted fakes sit far from the real defects, yet the features still separate real defects from good parts. The authors read it this way: CutPaste "is still not a perfect simulation of real defects", and "learning on it to find irregularity generalizes well on unseen anomalies."

DRAEM: repair the fake, then compare

DRAEM adds a second idea. A reconstructive subnet learns to repair the fake, mapping \htmlClass{t-yellow}{I_a} back to the clean image \htmlClass{t-blue}{I}. A discriminative subnet then looks at the input and its repair side by side and outputs a pixel map of where they disagree. The segmenter does not learn what a fake looks like. It learns how far an image is from its own repaired version, and that distance also exists for real defects. First, the fake (hover the terms to light the panels):

\htmlData{step=1, link=beta}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{I_a}} = \htmlData{step=2, link=image}{\overline{M}_a \odot \htmlClass{t-blue}{I}} + \htmlData{step=3, link=mask}{(1-\beta)\,(M_a \odot \htmlClass{t-blue}{I})} + \htmlData{step=4, link=texture}{\beta\,(M_a \odot \htmlClass{t-brown}{A})}

Eq. 4 in DRAEM v2. M_a is the thresholded Perlin mask and \overline{M}_a its inverse; \htmlClass{t-brown}{A} is a texture from the Describable Textures Dataset after three random augmentations; the opacity \beta is drawn uniformly from [0.1, 1.0]. The paper draws the noise threshold at random too; the slider makes it a control.

Training minimizes two losses together. The repair must match the clean image, measured by structural similarity plus squared error. The map must match the fake's mask, measured by a focal loss, a cross-entropy that down-weights pixels that are already easy:

\htmlData{step=1}{L = \underbrace{\lambda L_{\mathrm{SSIM}}(I, I_r) + l_2(I, I_r)}_{L_{rec}}} \htmlData{step=2}{\; + \; L_{seg}(M_a, M)}

Eq. 2 and 3 in DRAEM v2. The paper gives no value for \lambda (the official code uses 1) and does not print the focal loss; it cites Lin et al. 2017, and the code uses focusing parameter 2.

At test time there is no fake: the real image goes in, and the map M_o is the anomaly map. The image score is the largest value of the map after a mean filter, so one stray pixel cannot decide the verdict:

\htmlData{step=1}{\eta = \max\big(M_o \ast f_{s_f \times s_f}\big)}

Eq. 5 in DRAEM v2. The filter size s_f is not in the paper; the official code uses 21.

On MVTec AD, one model per product, DRAEM reaches 98.0 image AUROC, 97.3 pixel AUROC and 68.4 pixel average precision. In the same table, DRAEM's own run of PaDiM has the higher pixel AUROC, 97.4, but only 55.0 average precision. DRAEM argues for average precision for exactly the reason in chapter 2: the false-positive rate is swamped by the huge number of normal pixels.

Does realism matter?

Predict before you press the buttons. Which hurts DRAEM more: fakes that look nothing like real defects, or removing the repair-and-compare design?

The design matters far more than realism. Flat color blobs still score 96.2 and rectangles 96.9, both above the PaDiM number DRAEM prints, 95.5. But train the segmenter alone on the same fakes, without the repair subnet, and it falls to 93.9. The authors blame "overfitting of the discriminative sub-network to the simulated anomalies".

That is the risk of the whole family: the model can learn your fakes instead of real defects. A segmenter trained to find blobs of foreign texture finds blobs of foreign texture. Both papers soften the risk with an extra step that is not about the fakes: CutPaste discards its classifier and keeps a Gaussian on the features, and DRAEM compares an image with its own repair. Both also fail on the same kind of defect. CutPaste lists missing and flipped components among its failure cases, and DRAEM writes that "the absence of a part of the object is especially difficult to detect". A missing part leaves no foreign texture to find. Chapter 10 attacks that case directly.
