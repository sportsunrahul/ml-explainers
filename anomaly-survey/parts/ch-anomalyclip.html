Prompts that know "damaged", not "which object"

WinCLIP's hand-written prompts name the product, and words alone are a weak signal for small local defects. AnomalyCLIP learns two prompts once, on a labelled dataset of other products, and reuses them unchanged on products it has never seen.

Before you look at the numbers, predict: prompts trained on VisA, with its circuit boards, candles and capsules, then tested on MVTec AD's screws, carpets and bottles: will they still work? The top row answers. On MVTec AD they reach 91.5 image AUROC, next to WinCLIP's 91.8, which used the product names. On pixels they reach 91.1 against WinCLIP's 85.1. Switch to Pixel AUROC: the gap is largest on localization, which is what the learned prompts and the training on masks were for. WinCLIP still leads on MVTec AD image AUROC, by 0.3 points.

Learned prompts

A prompt reaches CLIP's text encoder as a sequence of word embeddings, one vector per token. "Prompt learning" (as in CoOp, an earlier method for CLIP) replaces some of those vectors with free vectors and trains them by gradient descent, while every CLIP weight stays frozen. The words are gone; only the vectors remain, and they can sit anywhere in the embedding space, not only at real words.

AnomalyCLIP (ICLR 2024, arXiv v1 of 2023-10-29; this page cites v12 of 2026-01-01) learns two such prompts. The only fixed words are "object" and "damaged".

\htmlData{step=1, link=prompt-n}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{g_n} = [\htmlClass{t-green}{V_1}][\htmlClass{t-green}{V_2}]\dots[\htmlClass{t-green}{V_E}]}\htmlData{step=3, link=object}{[\text{object}]}, \qquad \htmlData{step=2, link=prompt-a}{\htmlClass{t-red}{g_a} = [\htmlClass{t-green}{W_1}][\htmlClass{t-green}{W_2}]\dots[\htmlClass{t-green}{W_E}][\text{damaged}]}\htmlData{step=4, link=object}{[\text{object}]}

Object-agnostic templates, AnomalyCLIP v12 Sec. 3.2 (unnumbered). The object-aware version puts the product name [cls] where these say [object].

  • \htmlClass{t-green}{V_i} and \htmlClass{t-green}{W_i} are learned word embeddings, E = 12 per prompt. They are the main trained weights.
  • \htmlClass{t-blue}{g_n} and \htmlClass{t-red}{g_a} are what the frozen text encoder makes of each prompt: one "normal" and one "abnormal" embedding, used exactly as in WinCLIP's two-class softmax.
  • "object" replaces the product name, which, in the paper's words, blocks out the class semantics of objects. The prompts must then encode what damage looks like on anything.

The paper also adds learned tokens deeper in the text encoder: 4 fresh vectors overwrite the first positions of each of the first 9 text layers, and the encoder's outputs at those positions are thrown away. This lets the prompt shape the text embedding at several depths, not only at the input.

How much does the word "object" matter by itself? Less than the headline suggests. The paper prints the gain of object-agnostic over object-aware prompt learning as differences only (Fig. 5): 0.8 points of image AUROC on MVTec AD and 0.3 on VisA, but 4.4 on MPDD. A second test (Fig. 8) trains with "object" and puts the product name back at test time: MVTec AD image AUROC falls from 91.5 to 89.1. Switch the figure to its second view to see both. The large jump in the paper's module ablation, from 66.3 to 90.8 (Table 4), adds learned prompts and the agnostic wording in one step, so it does not measure the wording alone.

Zero-shot still needs labelled anomalies from somewhere

The prompts are trained on an auxiliary dataset with defect masks, and tested on a different one. To test on MVTec AD, AnomalyCLIP trains on the VisA test split (962 normal and 1200 anomalous images). To test on everything else, it trains on the MVTec AD test split (467 normal and 1258 anomalous images). So "zero-shot" means zero images of the target products, but it uses thousands of labelled images of other products. One pair of prompts then serves every category of the target dataset; the paper evaluates 17 datasets, including medical ones.

Making CLIP's patch tokens local again

To draw a map, AnomalyCLIP compares the text embeddings with every patch token, not only the class token. The last chapter showed why that fails in plain CLIP: its last-layer patch tokens are not local. In CLIP's attention, many patches attend to the same few tokens, so each patch's output is mostly someone else's content. The fix, DPAM (diagonally prominent attention map), changes how attention weights are computed and adds no weights at all:

a_{ij} = e^{\,q_i k_j^{\top}/\sqrt{D}} \;\longrightarrow\; a^{vv}_{ij} = e^{\,v_i v_j^{\top}/\sqrt{D}}

Standard attention weights and the V-V weights DPAM uses, AnomalyCLIP v12 Appendix C (unnumbered).

The paper argues that a value vector, which takes no part in the original attention map, scores highest with itself, so a^{vv}_{ii} tends to dominate its row: each patch mostly keeps its own content, and the attention map becomes diagonally prominent. DPAM runs from the 6th visual layer. In the paper's overview figure it sits in a separate path that feeds only the patch tokens, and the class token keeps the original attention (our reading of that figure). In the module ablation, DPAM alone raises MVTec AD pixel AUROC from 46.8 to 68.4 and leaves image AUROC unchanged, as it should.

The loss grades the image and every pixel

\htmlData{step=1, link=total}{L_{total}} = \htmlData{step=2, link=global}{L_{global}} + \htmlData{step=3, link=local}{\lambda \textstyle \sum_{M_l \in \mathcal{M}} L_{local}^{M_l}}, \qquad \htmlData{step=4, link=local}{L_{local} = \mathrm{Focal}\big(\mathrm{Up}([S_{n}, S_{a}]), S\big) + \mathrm{Dice}\big(\mathrm{Up}(S_{n}), I - S\big) + \mathrm{Dice}\big(\mathrm{Up}(S_{a}), S\big)}

Eq. 2 and the unnumbered local loss, AnomalyCLIP v12 Sec. 3.3 (layer superscripts dropped in the second part). Hover a term to open that stage of the diagram below.

  • L_{global} is a cross-entropy between the image-level probability of "abnormal" and the image label. The paper describes it in words only.
  • S_n, S_a are the per-patch probabilities of normal and abnormal, upsampled (Up) to the image size; S is the ground-truth defect mask and I a matrix of ones.
  • Focal loss copes with the imbalance (defects are small); the two Dice terms ask the normal map to cover the normal region and the abnormal map to cover the defect. \lambda = 4.

Both terms matter. Trained with the global loss only, MVTec AD pixel AUROC is 80.3; with the local loss only, 91.0 (Table 5). Plain prompt learning that optimizes the global image match, as CoOp does, reaches 88.8 image AUROC but only 33.3 pixel AUROC on MVTec AD.
