Ask CLIP whether the part is damaged

Every method so far needed good images of the product before it could judge one. CLIP already pairs images with words such as "flawless" and "damaged", so WinCLIP asks it directly, window by window, with no image of the product at all.

Press Slide the windows. Each small window covers four patches and gets one score; the scratch lights up the windows that touch it. Now predict: what does the whole-image window say about this part? Press Whole image. It says "flawless". One scratch is a small part of the whole image, so the whole-image embedding barely moves. For the same reason, scoring whole images works for detection only when defects are large; small windows are what make a map possible.

Then compare Harmonic mean with Arithmetic mean. Every pixel is covered by several windows. The arithmetic mean smears a defect window's score over all of its pixels, so the red area grows well past the scratch. The harmonic mean is pulled down hard by any confidently normal window, so a pixel stays normal unless every window that covers it looks damaged. Last, try the cap: the toy scorer also flags the designed rim, because a generic idea of "damaged" does not know which edges belong to this product. WinCLIP reports the same kind of failure on real data, for example designed holes on a circuit board flagged as defects.

Zero-shot means no images, not no knowledge

CLIP was trained to place an image and its caption close together in one embedding space. You know how CLIP classifies with no training: embed one text prompt per class, embed the image, and take a softmax over the cosine similarities. WinCLIP (CVPR 2023, arXiv v1 of 2023-03-26) uses exactly two classes, normal and anomalous.

\htmlData{step=1, link=prob}{p(\text{anomalous}\mid \htmlClass{t-yellow}{x})} = \frac{\exp\big(\langle \htmlData{step=2, link=win}{f(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{x})}, \htmlData{step=3, link=text}{\htmlClass{t-red}{g_a}} \rangle / \tau\big)}{\exp\big(\langle f(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{x}), \htmlClass{t-blue}{g_n} \rangle / \tau\big) + \exp\big(\langle f(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{x}), \htmlClass{t-red}{g_a} \rangle / \tau\big)}

Eq. 1 in WinCLIP v1 with k = 2 classes. The paper defines its zero-shot score in words; reading it as the anomalous-class probability of Eq. 1 is our reading of Sec. 4.1.

  • \htmlClass{t-yellow}{x} is the image, or one window of it; f is the frozen CLIP image encoder, and \langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle is cosine similarity.
  • \htmlClass{t-blue}{g_n} and \htmlClass{t-red}{g_a} are text embeddings for "normal" and "anomalous", built from many prompts (below); \tau is CLIP's temperature.
  • The output is a number between 0 and 1: the bars in the figure. With only the two bare prompts "normal [object]" and "anomalous [object]", this scores 74.0 image AUROC on MVTec AD (Table 1).

Many prompts, averaged into two

One prompt is a noisy probe. WinCLIP writes a compositional prompt ensemble: 7 normal states (such as "flawless [o]" and "[o] without defect"), 4 anomalous states (such as "damaged [o]" and "[o] with flaw"), and 22 sentence templates (such as "a photo of a [c] for visual inspection."). Every state goes into every template, which gives 154 normal and 88 anomalous prompts (our computation). The text encoder embeds each one, and the embeddings of each class are averaged into a single prototype.

\htmlData{step=1, link=text}{\htmlClass{t-red}{g_a} = \frac{1}{|S_a|\,|T|} \sum_{s \in S_a} \sum_{t \in T} g\big(t(s(o))\big)}, \qquad \htmlData{step=2, link=text}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{g_n} \text{ likewise over } S_n}

Our notation for "the average of text embeddings per label" (WinCLIP Sec. 4.1); the paper prints no formula.

  • o is the product name from the dataset, such as "bottle". So zero-shot here means no images of the product, but the name is still used. AnomalyCLIP, in the next chapter, removes it.
  • S_a, S_n are the state phrases; T is the set of templates; g is the frozen CLIP text encoder.

The ablation in Table 3 shows where the gain comes from. On MVTec AD, image AUROC goes from 74.0 with two bare prompts to 89.8 with the state words, 90.8 with the templates as well, and 91.8 with multi-crop prediction. Scoring against the normal prompt alone, with no anomalous class, gives only 34.2: the contrast between the two classes is what carries the signal.

Windows instead of patch tokens

Why not score CLIP's patch tokens directly? CLIP aligns only its class token with text. Its last-layer patch tokens score 22.4 pixel AUROC on MVTec AD, worse than chance. So WinCLIP builds many small images. The backbone is OpenCLIP ViT-B/16+, trained on LAION-400M, at an input of 240 pixels; that gives 225 patch tokens, a 15 by 15 grid. A window keeps a square of patches and drops all the others before the forward pass. Small windows are 32 pixels on a side (2 by 2 patches), mid windows 48 pixels (3 by 3), and both move with a stride of 16 pixels. That is 196 small and 169 mid windows, plus the whole image (our computation, assuming no padding). Each window's class token goes through Eq. 1, and its score is painted onto every pixel of the window.

\htmlData{step=1, link=harm}{\bar{\mathbf{M}}^{\tt W}_{0,ij}} = \left(\htmlData{step=2, link=win}{\frac{1}{\sum_{u,v} (\mathbf{w}_{uv})_{ij}} \sum_{u,v} \frac{(\mathbf{w}_{uv})_{ij}}{\mathbf{M}^{\tt W}_{0,uv}}}\right)^{-1}

Eq. 3 in WinCLIP v1: the harmonic mean, at pixel (i, j), of the scores of all windows that cover it.

  • (\mathbf{w}_{uv})_{ij} is 1 when window (u, v) covers pixel (i, j), and 0 otherwise.
  • \mathbf{M}^{\tt W}_{0,uv} is the zero-shot score of window (u, v). Dividing by it makes a near-zero (confidently normal) window dominate, which is the paper's stated intent: weight "more on scores towards normality".
  • The maps of the three scales are combined by a second harmonic mean. Without harmonic averaging, MVTec AD pixel AUROC drops from 85.1 to 81.5 (Table 8). Removing the small, mid or image scale gives 84.7, 84.0 and 82.0.

The price is many forward passes per image: zero-shot WinCLIP takes 389.4 ms per image on an Amazon EC2 G4dn instance (Table 8; the paper names neither the GPU model nor the batch size).

WinCLIP+: add a few good images

Some defects are defined only against a reference, such as a part that is flipped upside down. With K good images (1, 2 or 4), WinCLIP+ stores three memories: the small-window embeddings, the mid-window embeddings and the last-layer patch tokens of those images. A query feature then scores by its distance to the nearest stored feature, exactly as in SPADE and PatchCore, but with CLIP features.

\htmlData{step=1, link=win}{\mathbf{M}_{ij} = \min_{r \in \htmlClass{t-brown}{\mathbf{R}}} \tfrac{1}{2}\big(1 - \langle \mathbf{F}_{ij}, r \rangle\big)}, \qquad \htmlData{step=2, link=prob}{\mathrm{ascore}_{\tt W}(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{x}) = \tfrac{1}{2}\Big(\mathrm{ascore}_0\big(f(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{x})\big) + \max_{ij} \mathbf{M}^{\tt W}_{ij}\Big)}

Eq. 4 and Eq. 6 in WinCLIP v1. The three memory maps are averaged (Eq. 5) and then fused with the language map; the paper does not give the fusion formula.

  • \htmlClass{t-brown}{\mathbf{R}} is the stored memory from the K good images; \mathbf{F}_{ij} is the query feature at location (i, j). The score is half the cosine distance to the nearest stored feature.
  • The image score is half the language score of the whole image and half the worst vision-memory score anywhere.

Nothing is trained in either version: "fitting" WinCLIP+ means storing the features of K images. Each few-shot result is the mean of 5 random choices of the K images.

Two readings of these tables matter. First, zero-shot detection on MVTec AD is already close to the one-good-image results, but zero-shot localization is far behind: a window scored against words cannot match a map scored against real normal patches. Second, VisA is much harder for words than MVTec AD, with its multiple instances and complex circuit boards. The paper also notes that low-shot segmentation is not solved: the best pixel F1-max stays below 60% on both datasets, although pixel AUROC in the same setting looks excellent (the 1-shot rows above). That gap is chapter 2's warning about pixel AUROC, seen in practice.
