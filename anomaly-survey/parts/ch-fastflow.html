Act IIIModel the density, or fake the defect

Turn normal into a perfect Gaussian

PaDiM forces normal features into one Gaussian per position, and PatchCore keeps raw examples. A normalizing flow learns an invertible warp that bends the real, oddly shaped cloud of normal features into a perfect Gaussian, so the likelihood of any feature becomes an exact number.

Start at layer 0. The small dots are normal patch features, and the blue surface under them is their density: a twisted crescent that no single Gaussian fits. Predict before you press 4: once normal is a round bell, where will the feature from the scratch land? Each layer warps the plane a little. The crescent tilts back, untwists, straightens into a thin band, and swells into a standard Gaussian. The scratch's feature rides along and ends far out in the tail, where the bell is almost flat. There, a likelihood is easy to read: a standard Gaussian's density depends only on the distance from its center.

Watch the readout while you step. The log-likelihood of the test feature never changes. What changes is its split: the height of the surface under the point, plus a correction for how much the warp has stretched the plane so far. That correction is the yellow ring.

Why a flow gives an exact likelihood

A normalizing flow \htmlClass{t-green}{f_\theta} is an invertible network: every feature \htmlClass{t-yellow}{x} maps to exactly one latent z = \htmlClass{t-green}{f_\theta}(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{x}) of the same size, and back. Train it so that normal features land on a standard normal distribution. Then the probability of any feature follows from the change-of-variables rule: the density of z under the bell, corrected for how much the flow squeezes or stretches space at \htmlClass{t-yellow}{x}.

\htmlData{step=1, link=test}{\log p_X(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{x})} = \htmlData{step=2, link=base}{\log p_Z\big(\htmlClass{t-green}{f_\theta}(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{x})\big)} + \htmlData{step=3, link=logdet}{\log \left\lvert \det \frac{\partial \htmlClass{t-green}{f_\theta}(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{x})}{\partial \htmlClass{t-yellow}{x}} \right\rvert}, \qquad z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)

Eq. 2 in FastFlow v2 (the paper prints the mean of the normal as o).

  • \htmlClass{t-yellow}{x} is a feature vector at one position of the backbone's feature map.
  • \htmlClass{t-green}{f_\theta} is the flow with trainable parameters \theta; p_Z is the standard normal density, the round bell at layer 4.
  • The determinant term is the log of the local volume change. A flow that stretches the plane around \htmlClass{t-yellow}{x} spreads the probability thinner there, and this term pays it back. In the figure it is the ring's change in area.
  • Normal features get a high \log p_X. A defect's feature lands in the tail of the bell and gets a low one: that is the anomaly score.

One coupling layer

The determinant of a large network's Jacobian is expensive. Flows avoid it with affine coupling layers: split the channels into two halves, pass one half through unchanged, and use it to scale and shift the other half. The first half is untouched, so the layer is trivially invertible, and the Jacobian is triangular: its log-determinant is just the sum of the log-scales.

\htmlData{step=1}{y_a, y_b = \operatorname{split}(y)}, \qquad \htmlData{step=2}{y'_a = y_a}, \qquad \htmlData{step=3, link=logdet}{y'_b = s(y_a) \odot y_b + b(y_a)}, \qquad \htmlData{step=4}{y' = \operatorname{concat}(y'_a, y'_b)}

Eq. 3 in FastFlow v2. The paper's Fig. 2(b) applies \exp(s) before the multiplication and runs two such couplings per step, with an activation normalization and a channel permutation; the toy above uses that exponential form, so each layer's log-determinant is the sum of s(y_a).

Here s and b are small neural networks. They can be as complex as you like, because they are never inverted: to undo the layer, compute s(y_a) and b(y_a) again from the unchanged half and solve for y_b. Stack many such layers, alternating which half is held fixed, and the flow can bend any reasonable distribution into a Gaussian. The toy's four layers do exactly that, one bend each.

What makes FastFlow two-dimensional

Earlier flows for anomaly detection, such as DifferNet and CFLOW-AD, worked on one-dimensional vectors. Flattening a feature map into a vector "destroys the inherent spatial positional relationship", in FastFlow's words, and CFLOW-AD's sliding windows cost many forward passes. FastFlow's subnets s and b are convolutional instead. Each flow step maps a whole C \times H \times W feature map to a latent of the same shape, so one pass gives a likelihood for every position at once. The steps alternate 3 \times 3 convolutions, which see a neighborhood, with cheap 1 \times 1 convolutions, which see one position.

Training maximizes the log-likelihood of normal features. The paper does not print its loss; our reading is the negative of Eq. 2, averaged over every position of every training image. At test time, FastFlow turns the latent into a map: "we sum the two-dimensional probabilities of each channel to get the final probability map and upsample it to the input image resolution using bilinear interpolation." With a ResNet backbone, three flows run on three feature scales and their maps are averaged. The paper does not say how it turns the map into one image score.

Results

On MVTec AD, one model per product, FastFlow's best numbers use the CaiT backbone at 448 pixels: 99.4 image AUROC and 98.5 pixel AUROC. With WRN50-2 at 256 pixels it gets 99.3 and 98.1. The same table credits CFLOW-AD with 98.6 pixel AUROC, so FastFlow leads on detection, not on localization.

Three more results matter. First, the cheap 1 \times 1 steps pay off on large backbones: on WRN50-2, all-3 \times 3 subnets need 74.4 M added parameters and score 98.2, worse than the alternating design. Second, FastFlow with CaiT keeps 99.2 image AUROC when the test images are randomly zoomed, rotated and shifted, so it does not depend on aligned parts the way a per-position Gaussian does. Third, it is not a general novelty detector: on CIFAR-10 classes it scores 66.7. Pretrained features plus a density are good at "this patch looks wrong", not at "this is a different object".
