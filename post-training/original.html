From autocomplete to assistant

A pretrained language model knows a lot but only continues text. Post-training teaches it to follow instructions, prefer helpful and honest answers, and, most recently, to reason through hard problems. This guide covers supervised fine-tuning, RLHF, DPO and reasoning-focused reinforcement learning.

1

Why pretraining isn't enough

Ask a raw base model a question and it may answer, or it may continue with more questions, because on the web a question is often followed by another question. It has no notion of being an assistant, no preference for truthful over plausible text, and no reason to refuse harmful requests. Post-training reshapes which of its many learned behaviours it expresses. It uses a tiny fraction of pretraining compute but changes how useful the model is enormously.

After post-trainingThe capital of France is Paris.

The standard recipe, popularised by InstructGPT (Ouyang et al., 2022), has three stages: supervised fine-tuning on demonstrations, training a reward model on human preferences, then reinforcement learning against that reward. Labellers preferred the 1.3B InstructGPT model's outputs to those of the 175B GPT-3 it started from. Later work simplified the pipeline (DPO) and extended it to reasoning with automatically checkable rewards.

2

Supervised fine-tuning

SFT (or instruction tuning) continues training with the same next-token loss, but on curated conversations: prompts paired with ideal responses written by people or by stronger models. Conversations are formatted with a chat template, special tokens marking system, user and assistant turns. Crucially, the loss is masked so the model only learns to produce the assistant's words, not the user's.

Quality matters more than quantity. FLAN (2021) showed that fine-tuning on many tasks phrased as instructions improves zero-shot generalisation; Self-Instruct (2022) had a model generate its own instruction data; LIMA (2023) got strong results from only 1,000 carefully chosen examples, suggesting SFT mostly teaches format and style for knowledge the model already has. SFT's limit is that it only imitates. It can't learn that one good answer is better than another good answer, or improve beyond its demonstrations. For that we need preferences.

3

Preferences and reward models

Writing a perfect answer is hard; judging which of two answers is better is easy. So people are shown a prompt and two model responses and pick the better one. A reward model, usually the SFT model with its output layer replaced by a single number, is trained to give the chosen response a higher score, using the Bradley–Terry model of pairwise preferences:

P( yw ≻ yl | x ) = σ( r(x, yw) − r(x, yl) ),    LRM = − log σ( r(x, yw) − r(x, yl) ) yw is the preferred (“winning”) response, yl the rejected one, σ the logistic function. Only differences in reward matter, so the reward's absolute level is arbitrary.
4

RLHF with PPO

With a reward model in hand, reinforcement learning from human feedback optimises the policy to earn high reward, with a penalty for moving too far from the reference model:

maxπ Ex, y ~ π [ r(x, y) ] − β · KL( π(·|x) ‖ πref(·|x) ) The KL term keeps outputs fluent and stops the policy from exploiting weaknesses of the reward model. β sets the trade-off.

The standard optimiser is PPO (Schulman et al., 2017). The policy generates responses, the reward model scores them, a separate value model estimates expected reward to reduce variance, and PPO updates the policy with a clipped objective that prevents too-large steps. This works, and powered the original ChatGPT, but it is complex: four large models in memory, many hyperparameters, and sensitivity to implementation details. Its roots are earlier work on learning from human preferences in games (Christiano et al., 2017) and summarisation (Stiennon et al., 2020).

5

Reward hacking and overoptimisation

A reward model is only a proxy for what people want. Push hard enough and the policy finds responses the proxy loves but people don't: excessive length, flattery, confident-sounding filler, or telling users what they want to hear (sycophancy). Gao et al. (2022) measured this: as the policy moves further from the reference (higher KL), the proxy reward keeps rising while the true reward rises, peaks and then falls, a textbook case of Goodhart's law.

The KL-regularised objective has a closed-form optimum, π*(y) ∝ πref(y) · exp(r(y)/β). Below, it is computed exactly over a toy two-dimensional “space of responses”. The proxy reward equals the true reward plus one exploitable bump in a region the reference model rarely visits.

Mitigations include keeping β high enough, ensembles of reward models, regularly collecting fresh preferences on the current policy's outputs, penalising length, and early stopping on held-out human evaluations.

6

Direct Preference Optimization

Rafailov et al. (DPO, 2023) noticed that the closed-form optimum above can be inverted: any policy defines an implicit reward r(x, y) = β log π(y|x)/πref(y|x) + const. Substituting that into the Bradley–Terry loss removes the reward model and the RL loop entirely. What remains is a simple classification-style loss on preference pairs:

LDPO = − log σ( β [ log π(yw|x)/πref(yw|x) − log π(yl|x)/πref(yl|x) ] ) Increase the chosen response's likelihood relative to the reference, decrease the rejected one's. The gradient is largest for pairs the model currently gets wrong.

DPO needs only the policy and a frozen reference, is stable, and became the default for open models. Variants tune its weaknesses: IPO adds regularisation against overfitting to deterministic preferences, KTO learns from single thumbs-up or thumbs-down labels instead of pairs, ORPO folds preference learning into SFT without a reference model, and SimPO uses length-normalised likelihood as the implicit reward. The main trade-off versus PPO is that DPO is usually offline: it learns from fixed pairs rather than the policy's own fresh samples. Online and iterative DPO narrow that gap.

7

AI feedback and constitutions

Human labels are slow, expensive and inconsistent. RLAIF uses a model to produce the preference labels instead. Anthropic's Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022) gives the model a short list of written principles; it critiques and revises its own responses against them (a supervised stage), then judges pairs of responses by the principles to create preference data for RL. This made the model's values explicit and inspectable and reduced the need for people to read harmful content. Today most large-scale preference data is at least partly AI-generated, with humans focusing on hard cases and auditing.

8

Reasoning-focused reinforcement learning

Chain-of-thought prompting (Wei et al., 2022) showed that models answer harder questions when they write out intermediate steps. STaR (2022) bootstrapped this: generate reasoning, keep the attempts that reach correct answers, fine-tune on them, repeat. The big shift came when labs applied large-scale RL to reasoning itself. OpenAI's o1 (2024) was trained with RL to think at length before answering, and its accuracy kept improving with more training compute and with more thinking time at inference, a new scaling axis called test-time compute.

The key ingredient is verifiable rewards (RLVR): for maths, code and logic puzzles you can check the final answer or run tests, so no learned reward model is needed and there is little to hack. Rewards can be given for the final outcome (outcome reward models) or for each step (process reward models; Lightman et al., 2023, found step-level supervision more reliable for maths).

DeepSeek's GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization, 2024) made this cheap. For each prompt, sample a group of G answers, score them, and use each answer's reward relative to the group as its advantage. No value model is needed.

Ai = ( ri − mean(r1..G) ) / std(r1..G) The policy is then updated with a PPO-style clipped objective plus a KL penalty to the reference. If every answer in a group is right, or every answer wrong, all advantages are zero and the prompt teaches nothing, so curricula aim for problems the model solves sometimes.

DeepSeek-R1 (2025) showed how far this goes. R1-Zero applied GRPO directly to a base model with only correctness and format rewards; it learned on its own to write long chains of thought, check its work and backtrack. The released R1 added a small cold-start SFT set for readability, then RL, then SFT on filtered samples, then more RL. Its reasoning traces were also distilled into much smaller open models.

10

Strengths, limits and open problems

What post-training achieves

  • Turns a text predictor into a helpful, conversational assistant with a small fraction of the pretraining compute.
  • Encodes preferences that are easy to judge but hard to write down.
  • Unlocks reasoning: RL on verifiable tasks produced large gains in maths, coding and science.
  • Makes safety behaviours, such as declining harmful requests, trainable.

What remains hard

  • Reward hacking and sycophancy: optimising a proxy for approval is not optimising for truth.
  • Much of what matters (good writing, honest uncertainty, real-world helpfulness) has no verifier.
  • Alignment can be shallow: models can be fine-tuned or prompted around it.
  • Long reasoning costs inference compute, and reasoning traces may not faithfully reflect how the model reached its answer.
  • Evaluating whether a model is aligned, rather than looking aligned, is an open research problem.
