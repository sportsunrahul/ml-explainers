Act IThe problem

Learning from a score, not an answer

  • Exploration
  • Credit assignment

You know how to train a classifier: show it an image, tell it the right label, and nudge the weights toward that label. This chapter asks what changes when nobody can tell the model the right answer, only how well it did. The answer has three parts, and they give this whole course its shape.

The loop

Reinforcement learning has two players. The agent is the learner, the thing you train. The environment is everything else: a game, a robot's body and its room, a person using an app. They take turns. At each step t the agent sees the state S_t, a description of the situation, and picks an action A_t. The environment answers with a reward R_{t+1}, a single number, and the next state S_{t+1}. Then the loop repeats. The figure at the top of the page ends on this loop.

The rule the agent uses to pick actions is its policy, written \pi. Training means changing the policy so that the rewards add up to more. That is the whole job. The difficulty is in what the reward does not tell you.

Same task, two kinds of feedback

The figure below is a tiny world. A robot stands in the middle of a track of seven cells. A charger is at one end, and the robot has five moves to reach it. You are the agent: pick a kind of feedback, then press Left and Right.

Make a guess before you start. Play one episode with each kind of feedback. After which one could you say exactly which move was wrong?

With Supervised feedback, a teacher shows the right move before each press and grades it after. This is how you train a classifier: every input comes with its correct answer. With Reinforcement feedback, there is no teacher. You press, nothing is said, and when the episode ends you get one number: +1 if you reached the charger, 0 if not. Play a few reinforcement episodes. Three things go wrong that never go wrong for a classifier.

Problem 1: the score does not say what would have been better

A score of 0 says only "that did not work". It does not say which way the charger was. The only way to find out is to try the other way. Trying actions you are unsure about, to learn what they are worth, is called exploration. Exploration has a price: while you explore, you are not doing what you currently think is best. Doing the best-looking thing is called exploitation. Sutton and Barto put the tension bluntly: "neither exploration nor exploitation can be pursued exclusively without failing at the task". It runs through the whole course.

Think of choosing a restaurant. Your favorite is reliable. A new place might be better or worse. If you never try new places, you never find a better one. If you always try new places, you rarely eat well.

Problem 2: the score comes late

When an episode scores 0 after five moves, which move was the mistake? Maybe the first move went the wrong way. Maybe every move did. The score is one number for the whole episode, and it does not say which actions earned it. Deciding how much credit or blame each earlier action deserves is the credit assignment problem. In chess the only score is the result at the end, after dozens of your moves.

Problem 3: your data comes from your own choices

Look at the strip "cells you have seen". If you always press Right, you never see the left end of the track, so you never learn what is there. A classifier's training set is fixed before training starts. An agent's data is produced by its own policy: change the policy and the data changes with it. So the data is correlated (each state looks like the one before), and it shifts while you train. Much of chapters 7 to 12 is about living with this.

The goal as one equation

The sum of the rewards in an episode is its return, G. A policy is good if its return is high on average over many episodes. Read the equation as "the score of a policy is its average return".

\htmlData{step=1, link=policy}{\htmlClass{t-green}{J(\pi)}} = \htmlData{step=2, link=avg}{\mathbb{E}_{\pi}}\big[\,\htmlData{step=3, link=return}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{G}}\,\big], \qquad \htmlClass{t-yellow}{G} = \htmlData{step=4, link=return}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{R_1 + R_2 + \cdots + R_T}}

Our notation for the objective. The return here is Sutton and Barto's Eq. 3.7; chapter 3 adds a discount factor (their Eq. 3.8).

  • \htmlClass{t-green}{J(\pi)}: the objective, one number that says how good the policy \pi is.
  • \mathbb{E}_\pi: the average over episodes in which the agent follows \pi. Episodes differ because the policy can be random and because the environment can be.
  • \htmlClass{t-yellow}{G}: the return of one episode, all its rewards added up.
  • R_1, \dots, R_T: the rewards of one episode, which ends at step T.

Try it on the track, with the charger on the right.

  • Right, Right, Right reaches the charger. The rewards are 0, 0, +1, so G = 1.
  • An episode that runs out of moves has five rewards of 0, so G = 0.
  • A policy that presses at random needs three more Rights than Lefts within five moves. That happens with Right, Right, Right (1 chance in 8) or with one Left among the first three presses and four Rights (3 sequences, each 1 chance in 32). So J = 4/32 + 3/32 = 7/32 \approx 0.22, derived here. The exercise below measures 0.216 by simulation.
  • "Always press Right" has J = 1 here, and J = 0 if the charger is on the left.

You might think a reward is a kind of label. It is not. A label says what the right output was. A reward says how good the output you chose was, and it says nothing about the outputs you did not choose. In Sutton and Barto's words, the training signal "evaluates the actions taken rather than instructs by giving correct actions". So a classifier gets a gradient toward the right answer from every example, while an agent must find out, by trying, which direction is better.

Check yourself: an agent plays a game that gives +1 at step 50 and 0 at every other step. Which of the three problems does this create?

Credit assignment, above all. Fifty actions came before the score, and the score does not say which of them mattered. The agent must spread the credit back over time; chapters 3 to 6 build the tools for that. Exploration is involved too: an agent that never reaches step 50 by chance never sees a reward at all.

Check yourself: a recommender learns only from clicks on the items it chose to show. Which problem is that?

Data from your own choices. The system never sees how people would react to items it never showed, so its data is shaped by its own policy. It is also an exploration problem: to learn about other items, it must sometimes show them.

Where this came from

Learning from consequences is older than computers. Thorndike's experiments with cats in puzzle boxes (1898) led to his Law of Effect (1911): of the responses an animal makes in a situation, those closely followed by satisfaction become more firmly connected with that situation. Minsky named the credit-assignment problem in 1961 with one question: "How do you distribute credit for success among the many decisions that may have been involved in producing it?" In engineering, Bellman built dynamic programming and the Markov decision process in 1957; they are the math of chapters 3 to 5. Sutton and Barto, whose textbook this course follows, describe how these threads came together in the late 1980s, with Watkins's Q-learning (1989) as the join. They call "trial-and-error search and delayed reward" the two most important features of reinforcement learning: our exploration and credit-assignment problems. The third problem is this page's framing of what happens when the data comes from your own policy, the theme of the deep RL chapters.

The same loop now trains game players and language models. Starting from random play and given only the rules, AlphaZero reached superhuman play in chess, shogi and Go within 24 hours. And people preferred the answers of a 1.3B-parameter model fine-tuned with reinforcement learning from human feedback over those of the 175B-parameter GPT-3 (chapter 15).

In practice

If you can get labels, use them: supervised learning is far cheaper and more stable. Reach for reinforcement learning when the only feedback is a score that comes from acting, such as a game result, a robot reaching a goal, a person's rating, or a test that passes. Many real systems use both: a model is first trained with labels, then fine-tuned with rewards (chapter 15).
