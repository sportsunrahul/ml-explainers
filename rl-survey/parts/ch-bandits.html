Explore or exploit

  • Exploration
  • Credit assignment
  • Data from your own choices

Start with the smallest version of the problem: one situation, a few actions, and a score right after every action. There is no state that changes and no delay, so credit assignment disappears. Exploration does not, and this chapter shows how to handle it well.

Play it yourself

Picture a row of five slot machines, called arms. Each arm has a hidden average payout. A pull pays that average plus random noise, so one pull tells you little. You have 20 pulls, and your goal is the largest total. This setting is called a multi-armed bandit, after the slang "one-armed bandit" for a slot machine.

Make a guess before you start: is it better to try every arm a few times, or to stay with the first arm that pays well? Play 20 pulls, then press Reveal.

Most people try a few arms, find one that paid well once or twice, and stay there. Sometimes that arm is the best. Often it is a decent arm that got lucky early, and the best arm never got enough pulls to show what it can do. That is the trap this chapter is about.

Estimate an arm by averaging

The natural estimate of an arm's value is the average of the rewards it has paid so far. Write \htmlClass{t-blue}{Q_t(a)} for the estimate of arm a before step t, and N_t(a) for how many times it has been pulled. If arm 3 paid 1.2, 0.4 and 2.0, its estimate is (1.2 + 0.4 + 2.0)/3 = 1.2. In the figure the blue bar of each column is exactly this average.

You do not need to store every reward to keep an average. When the n-th reward R_n arrives, move the old estimate a fraction 1/n of the way toward it:

\htmlData{step=1, link=estimate}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{Q_{n+1}}} = \htmlData{step=1, link=estimate}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{Q_n}} + \htmlData{step=2, link=count}{\frac{1}{n}}\Big[\,\htmlData{step=3, link=error}{\htmlClass{t-red}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{R_n} - \htmlClass{t-blue}{Q_n}}}\,\Big]

Eq. 2.3 in Sutton and Barto (2018).

  • \htmlClass{t-blue}{Q_n}: the estimate after n - 1 rewards; \htmlClass{t-blue}{Q_{n+1}} is the estimate after one more.
  • \htmlClass{t-yellow}{R_n}: the reward just received.
  • \htmlClass{t-red}{R_n - Q_n}: the error, how far the new reward is from what you expected.
  • 1/n: the step size. It shrinks as evidence piles up, so each new reward moves the estimate less.

Try it with numbers. Arm 3's estimate is 1.0 after two pulls. The third pull pays 2.5. The error is 2.5 - 1.0 = 1.5, the step size is 1/3, so the new estimate is 1.0 + 1.5/3 = 1.5, the same as averaging all three rewards. Hover over the terms of the equation after a pull to see each one in the figure.

Look at the shape: new estimate = old estimate + step size × (target − old estimate). Sutton and Barto write it as a general rule (their Eq. 2.4), and you will meet it in almost every chapter from here on. Only the target changes: a reward here, a one-step guess in TD learning (chapter 6), a guess about the best next action in Q-learning (chapter 7).

Greedy gets stuck

The simplest policy always pulls the arm with the highest estimate. This is the greedy policy, and it is pure exploitation. Its flaw is the one you may have felt in the game: once an arm looks best, the others stop getting pulled, so their estimates never improve, and a bad early estimate is never corrected.

The fix is a little deliberate randomness. The epsilon-greedy policy picks the greedy arm most of the time, and with a small probability \varepsilon picks an arm at random:

\htmlClass{t-green}{A_t} = \begin{cases} \htmlData{step=1, link=greedy}{\arg\max_a \htmlClass{t-blue}{Q_t(a)}} & \text{with probability } 1 - \varepsilon \\[2pt] \htmlData{step=2, link=e1}{\text{a random arm}} & \text{with probability } \htmlData{step=2, link=e1}{\varepsilon} \end{cases}

Our notation for the epsilon-greedy rule of Sutton and Barto, section 2.2; the greedy choice alone is their Eq. 2.2. Ties in the arg max are broken at random.

The figure below plays the standard test for bandit methods. Each task has 10 arms whose means are drawn from a normal distribution, and each method plays 1000 pulls. One task proves little, so the curves average 2000 tasks, as in Sutton and Barto. Make a guess before you press Run: after 1000 pulls, which earns more, pure greedy or epsilon-greedy with \varepsilon = 0.1?

Greedy rises fastest at first and then flattens near a reward of 1: it locks onto an arm that is not the best and never leaves. Sutton and Barto report that greedy found the best arm in only about one-third of their tasks. Epsilon-greedy keeps sampling every arm a little, so it keeps fixing its estimates and keeps finding the best arm. The readout gives the numbers from your run.

Why does \varepsilon = 0.1 level off below 100% best-arm choices? Even when it knows the best arm, it explores on 10% of steps, and a random pick hits the best arm only 1 time in 10. So at most 0.9 + 0.1 \times 0.1 = 0.91 of its choices can be the best arm, the 91% ceiling Sutton and Barto give. A smaller \varepsilon = 0.01 learns more slowly, but its ceiling is higher, so in the long run it does better. Sutton and Barto make the same point about their version of this figure.

A simple trick gives early exploration for free: start every estimate high. If every Q_1(a) is +5 while real rewards are near 0, each pulled arm disappoints, so the greedy policy moves on to an untried arm, and every arm gets tried. Sutton and Barto call these optimistic initial values; their test uses a constant step size of 0.1 instead of 1/n, so the optimism fades over several pulls instead of vanishing after the first. The trick only helps at the start, because the optimism is used up.

UCB: explore where you are unsure

Epsilon-greedy explores blindly: it spends exploration on arms that are clearly bad as often as on arms that might be good. A better idea is to prefer arms whose estimates are uncertain. An arm pulled only a few times could be better than it looks. So give each arm a bonus that is large when its count N_t(a) is small, and pick the arm with the highest estimate plus bonus. This is the upper confidence bound (UCB) rule:

\htmlClass{t-green}{A_t} = \htmlData{step=1, link=choice}{\arg\max_a} \Big[\, \htmlData{step=2, link=estimate}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{Q_t(a)}} + \htmlData{step=3, link=bonus}{c \sqrt{\frac{\ln t}{\htmlData{step=4, link=count}{N_t(a)}}}} \,\Big]

Eq. 2.10 in Sutton and Barto (2018). An arm with N_t(a) = 0 counts as the best choice, so every arm is tried once first.

  • \htmlClass{t-blue}{Q_t(a)}: the estimate, the exploit part.
  • c \sqrt{\ln t / N_t(a)}: the bonus, the explore part. It shrinks as the arm gets pulled. It grows slowly, as the logarithm of the step t, for arms that are left alone, so no arm is ignored forever.
  • c: how much to value uncertainty. The testbed uses c = 2.

Try it with numbers at step t = 100, where \ln 100 \approx 4.61. An arm pulled 4 times gets a bonus of 2\sqrt{4.61/4} \approx 2.15. An arm pulled 50 times gets only 2\sqrt{4.61/50} \approx 0.61. So a rarely pulled arm with estimate 0.5 (total 2.65) beats a well-known arm with estimate 1.8 (total 2.41): UCB checks the uncertain arm once more. Press "Show UCB bounds" in the game to see each arm's bound and the arm UCB would pull next. In the testbed, UCB ends ahead of both epsilon-greedy curves.

Learn preferences, not values

There is a different way to choose. Instead of estimating each arm's value, keep a preference H_t(a) for each arm and turn the preferences into probabilities with a softmax, the same function a classifier uses for its class scores:

\htmlClass{t-green}{\pi_t(a)} = \frac{e^{H_t(a)}}{\sum_{b} e^{H_t(b)}}, \qquad H_{t+1}(A_t) = H_t(A_t) + \alpha\,\big(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{R_t} - \bar R_t\big)\big(1 - \htmlClass{t-green}{\pi_t(A_t)}\big), \qquad H_{t+1}(a) = H_t(a) - \alpha\,\big(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{R_t} - \bar R_t\big)\,\htmlClass{t-green}{\pi_t(a)} \ \text{ for } a \ne A_t

Eq. 2.11 and Eq. 2.12 in Sutton and Barto (2018). A bandit has no next state, so their chapter 2 writes the reward of step t as R_t; from chapter 3 on it is R_{t+1}.

After each pull, compare the reward with \bar R_t, the average of the rewards so far. If the reward beat the average, raise the preference of the arm you pulled and lower the others; if it fell short, do the opposite. The comparison point \bar R_t is called a baseline.

Try it with numbers. Three arms start with equal preferences, so each has probability 1/3. You pull arm 1 and it pays 2.0, while the baseline is 0.5, and \alpha = 0.1. Arm 1's preference rises by 0.1 \times 1.5 \times (1 - 1/3) = 0.1. Each other preference falls by 0.1 \times 1.5 \times 1/3 = 0.05. Arm 1 is now a little more likely to be pulled.

This rule is more important than it looks. Sutton and Barto show that it is stochastic gradient ascent on the expected reward. It is the first policy gradient on this page: the policy is changed directly, in the direction that makes good actions more likely. Chapter 10 builds the general version, and the PyTorch exercise below shows that autograd produces exactly this update. The baseline matters too. Sutton and Barto test it on arms whose means are shifted by +4, so every reward is positive; without the baseline every pull raises the pulled arm, and they find performance "significantly degraded". In the exercise below, removing it cuts how often the best arm is chosen from 85% to 48%.

Check yourself: an arm's estimate is 2.0 after 4 pulls, and the fifth pull pays 0. What is the new estimate?

2.0 + \tfrac15 (0 - 2.0) = 1.6. As a check, the four earlier rewards summed to 8.0, and 8.0/5 = 1.6.

Check yourself: why does UCB try every arm at least once before it repeats any?

An arm with N_t(a) = 0 has an infinite bonus, so it beats every arm that has been pulled. Once every arm has one pull, the bonuses are finite and the estimates start to matter.

Where this came from

Bandit problems began in statistics, where they fall under the "sequential design of experiments" of Thompson (1933) and Robbins (1952). The UCB rule above is called UCB1 and is due to Auer, Cesa-Bianchi and Fischer (2002). The term "action value", and possibly the first use of epsilon-greedy, are due to Watkins (1989). The name "soft-max" for Eq. 2.11 comes from Bridle (1990), and the gradient bandit is a special case of the gradient methods of Williams (1992), which chapter 10 builds on. This chapter follows chapter 2 of Sutton and Barto, whose parameter study of all these methods concludes: "UCB seems to perform best" on this testbed.

  • How the gradient bandit changes action probabilities directly, and what its baseline does.

In practice

A/B tests, ad selection and recommendation are bandit problems, and UCB-style bonuses and epsilon-greedy are used there directly. In full reinforcement learning the same trade-off spans whole sequences of actions. Epsilon-greedy is still the default exploration of DQN (chapter 9), and policy-gradient methods explore by keeping their action probabilities from collapsing too early (chapters 11 and 13).
