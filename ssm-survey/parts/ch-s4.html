S4: structure and the convolution view

Drop the 1/t from HiPPO and the memory becomes time-invariant: the same A, B and C at every step. Then one system can be computed in three ways, and one of them trains like a convolutional network, every position at once.

The three views come from LSSL, the paper between HiPPO and S4 (2021). S4 writes the input as u and the state as x; here they stay \htmlClass{t-blue}{x} and \htmlClass{t-yellow}{h}. The continuous view is the differential equation h'(t) = A\,h(t) + B\,x(t) from two chapters ago. The recurrent view samples it every \Delta and steps the state forward one token at a time, like an RNN. The convolutional view unrolls that recurrence. Because A, B and C are the same at every step, the input from j steps ago always reaches the output with the same weight, C\bar A^{\,j}\bar B. Line those weights up and you have one long filter, the kernel \bar K, and the whole output is the input convolved with it.

\htmlData{step=1, link=state}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{h_k} = \bar A\, \htmlClass{t-yellow}{h_{k-1}}} \htmlData{step=2, link=input}{+ \bar B\, \htmlClass{t-blue}{x_k}}, \qquad \htmlData{step=3, link=output}{\htmlClass{t-green}{y_k} = C\, \htmlClass{t-yellow}{h_k}}, \qquad \htmlData{step=4, link=delta}{\bar A = \big(I - \tfrac{\Delta}{2} A\big)^{-1}\big(I + \tfrac{\Delta}{2} A\big),\;\; \bar B = \big(I - \tfrac{\Delta}{2} A\big)^{-1} \Delta B}

The recurrent view with the bilinear rule: S4 Eq. (3); LSSL Eqs. (4) and (5).

\htmlData{step=1, link=kernel}{\bar K = \big(C\bar B,\; C\bar A\bar B,\; C\bar A^{2}\bar B,\; \dots,\; C\bar A^{L-1}\bar B\big)} \qquad \htmlData{step=2, link=output input}{\htmlClass{t-green}{y} = \bar K \ast \htmlClass{t-blue}{x}}

The convolutional view: S4 Eqs. (4) and (5); LSSL Eqs. (6) and (7), where Eq. (6) drops a C by a typo.

  • \bar A, \bar B: the continuous A, B turned into a per-token step of size \Delta. S4 uses the bilinear rule, as HiPPO did; Mamba will use a different one (zero-order hold).
  • \htmlClass{t-yellow}{h_k}: the state after token k, the four yellow cells that ride along in the recurrent view.
  • \bar K: entry j says how much the input j steps back adds to the output now. It is the yellow row at the bottom: the state's memory, unrolled.
  • \ast: convolution. Output k is the sum of \bar K_j\, x_{k-j} over j: slide the flipped kernel along the input.

The dots and the rings in the figure sit on each other exactly: the recurrent and convolutional views are the same arithmetic in a different order, and they agree to the last digit of a floating-point number. The continuous curve passes through them only up to the error of the bilinear step, 0.0054 at the default \Delta, and the gap shrinks as \Delta does. Each view has a job. The convolution trains: all outputs at once, with no loop over time. The recurrence generates: one constant-cost step per new token. The continuous view sets the clock.

Before you drag the step size: if each token covers less continuous time (a smaller \Delta), does the kernel get longer or shorter? Press Small Δ. It gets longer: at \Delta = 0.03 the kernel stays above 5% of its peak for 42 steps, against 13 at 0.1 and 4 at 0.3. The same continuous memory, read with finer steps, spans more tokens. LSSL puts it this way: the length of dependencies a layer captures is roughly proportional to 1/\Delta, so \Delta sets the width of the convolution.

Against attention, this is the payoff. Training a layer is one fast Fourier transform (FFT) convolution per channel, about L \log L work instead of attention's L^2 scores, and generating is one O(N) step per token (Theorem 2) with no cache. In S4's own benchmark at length 4,096, S4 trained 5.19 times as fast as a Transformer of the same size, in 0.091 of its memory (Table 3); as a generator it was 60x faster (abstract).

The hard part: computing the kernel

The convolution is only cheap if \bar K is. Building it the obvious way, multiplying by \bar A once per lag, costs O(N^2 L) operations and O(NL) memory, and LSSL paid exactly that. The usual fix for powers of a matrix is to diagonalize it, but HiPPO's A cannot be diagonalized safely: its eigenvector matrix has entries as large as 2^{4N/3}, far beyond what floating point survives (S4 Lemma 3.2).

S4's way around it, in plain words. HiPPO's A is normal plus low rank: a well-behaved matrix that a stable change of basis makes diagonal, plus one outer product P Q^{\top} (rank r=1 for LegS, Theorem 1). Next, S4 does not compute the kernel lag by lag. It computes the kernel's Fourier transform at L frequencies, where all the powers of \bar A collapse into a single matrix inverse. The Woodbury identity, a standard formula for inverting "easy matrix plus low rank", splits that inverse into the diagonal part and a small correction. For a diagonal matrix, each frequency is a sum over the N eigenvalues \lambda_j of terms shaped like 1/(\omega - \lambda_j), a Cauchy kernel, a well-studied computation. One inverse FFT turns the L values back into \bar K (Algorithm 1). The theory cost is \tilde O(N + L) operations (Theorem 3, the tilde hiding log factors); the released code runs the simple Cauchy loop instead, O(NL) time and O(N + L) memory (S4 Appendix A). Each channel trains 5N complex numbers for this: the eigenvalues, P, Q, B and C, plus its step \Delta. You do not need to follow each step; the point is that HiPPO's memory became a layer whose kernel costs about as much to build as to apply.

Branch: is the low-rank part needed? (S4D)

S4D drops the low-rank term and keeps only a diagonal A; the kernel becomes "2 lines of code". It works, but only when the diagonal starts from HiPPO. On Path-X, S4D-LegS (HiPPO's diagonal part) reaches 91.95% and S4D-Inv (a closed-form fit to it) 92.80%, while S4D-Lin, a plain Fourier-like spread, fails (S4D Table 5). S4D's ablations also find random diagonal initializations clearly worse than HiPPO-based ones. S4's own ablation says the same of the full model: with A trained, random and HiPPO starts all fit the training set, but validation accuracy differs by over 15% (S4 Sec. 4.4). The structure makes S4 fast; the HiPPO start makes it remember.

The test S4 was built for is the Long Range Arena (LRA): six classification tasks with inputs of 1K-16K tokens. Its hardest task, Path-X, shows a 128 by 128 image as one sequence of 16384 pixels and asks whether two marked dots are connected by a path. Every model before S4 scored at chance, 50%.

Inside an S4 layer: forward, then backward

One S4 layer is H copies of the toy above, one per feature channel, each with its own N-dimensional state and its own \Delta, followed by a step that mixes the channels. The stepper uses the Path-X configuration, so every shape is the paper's.

S4 made the HiPPO memory fast and trainable. But look again at the kernel: once training ends it is fixed. Every input, a key word or filler, is weighed by its distance alone. The next act starts there.
