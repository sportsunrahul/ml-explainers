Act IVBeyond text, and honesty

Vision Mamba, VMamba, and a counterpoint

A vision Transformer (ViT) reads an image as a sequence: it cuts the picture into square patches, turns each patch into a token, and lets attention compare every patch with every other. An SSM can read the same sequence, but its scan runs in one direction, and a picture has no natural first patch.

Start with the causal raster scan: patches are read row by row, left to right, the way you read text. The green patch's output can only use patches the scan has already passed, so everything to its right and below is empty. Those patches come later, and they cannot reach it at all.

Even the patches it can see are reached unevenly. The patch to its left is one step back in the sequence. The patch directly above is a whole row back, 14 steps, so with a decay of 0.85 per step it arrives with weight 0.8514 = 0.10. Meanwhile the last patch of the row above, at the far edge of the image, is only seven steps back and counts three times as much. Flattening keeps some neighbours close and pushes others far away.

Vim (Vision Mamba) scans the same row-order sequence twice, once forward and once backward, with separate SSM weights, and adds the two gated outputs. Press Vim: forward + backward: now every patch can reach every other, and the right side lights up. But the patch below is still a row away in either direction, so vertical neighbours stay faint. VMamba (Visual State Space Model) adds two column-order routes, down the columns and back up, each with its own SSM; its authors call this SS2D, a 2D selective scan. In a column route the patch above is one step away. Press VMamba: four routes: the heat becomes a cross, strong along the row and along the column.

\htmlData{step=1, link=target}{\htmlClass{t-green}{y^{(r)}_p}} = \htmlData{step=2, link=before}{\sum_{q \,\preceq_r\, p}} \htmlData{step=3, link=decay}{C_p \Big(\prod_{k \,\in\, (q,\, p]_r} \htmlClass{t-yellow}{\bar A_k}\Big)} \htmlData{step=4, link=patches}{\bar B_q\, \htmlClass{t-blue}{x_q}} \qquad \htmlData{step=5, link=routes}{\htmlClass{t-green}{y_p} = \mathrm{Linear}\big((\htmlClass{t-green}{y^{\rightarrow}_p} + \htmlClass{t-green}{y^{\leftarrow}_p}) \odot \mathrm{SiLU}(z_p)\big)}

Left: Vim Eq. 2 (the recurrence) unrolled along one route r; Mamba-2's Eq. 3 is the same sum for one sequence. Right: Vim Algorithm 1, the two gated directions added and projected.

  • \htmlClass{t-green}{y^{(r)}_p}: the output of route r's scan at patch p, the green patch.
  • q \preceq_r p: the sum runs only over patches that come no later than p on this route. That is the causal mask; every other patch contributes nothing.
  • \prod \htmlClass{t-yellow}{\bar A_k}: the state's decay, one factor for every step between q and p along the route. Mamba computes each factor from the token (selection, chapter 7). The toy sets every factor to the slider's value a, so the product is a^{d} for a patch d steps back. That is the blue heat.
  • \bar B_q \htmlClass{t-blue}{x_q}: what patch q writes into the state when the scan passes it; C_p reads the state out at p.
  • Right: Vim gates both directions with the same \mathrm{SiLU}(z), adds them, and projects back to width D. VMamba runs four such routes, each with its own \bar A, \bar B, C; its text says the four outputs are reshaped and merged, without saying how, so this page does not guess.

Before you press VMamba: with Vim's two scans, the patch diagonally below and to the right of the green one is fifteen steps away and arrives at 0.09. Will VMamba's column routes make it stronger? They do not. On a column route that patch is also fifteen steps away, so in one layer its weight stays the same. Diagonal context arrives only through a second layer, by way of a patch that shares a row with one and a column with the other.

How much of this survives in a trained network is an empirical question, and the papers answer it in parts. Vim's ablation is the evidence that the backward scan matters. With the forward scan only, ImageNet top-1 is 73.2 and the ADE20K segmentation score is 32.3 mIoU (mean intersection over union); with a backward SSM and its own convolution, 73.9 and 35.9 (Table 4, no class token). The gain is much larger for segmentation, where every pixel needs context from all sides, than for classification.

Where to put the class token, the extra token whose final output the classifier reads? In a scan, position matters. Vim tried five designs, and the middle won: 76.1 against 75.2 with the token at the head (Table 5). The paper credits the recurrence and the fact that ImageNet objects tend to sit near the centre. Our reading adds the decay: from the middle, every patch is at most about half the sequence away in one of the two scans, while a token at the head is up to 196 steps from the far corner.

The results: Vim-Ti reaches 76.1 top-1 with 7M parameters, against 72.2 for DeiT-Ti, a ViT of the same size. Vim-S reaches 80.3 against 79.8. (That is the value in v3 of the paper, the version read for this page; the README and later papers cite 80.5.) The headline claim is about cost, and it is this chapter's cost against attention. On square images 1248 pixels wide, 6084 tokens, Vim-Ti runs 2.8x faster than DeiT-Ti and uses 86.8% less GPU memory, measured on the backbone alone. Attention's score matrix grows with the square of those tokens; the scan's state does not grow at all. In the paper's memory chart DeiT-Ti's point at that size is marked OOM, out of memory. At 512 pixels the two are similar, and at the 224 pixels of ImageNet training the paper makes no speed claim. Press 1248 px to see that point on the gauge.

VMamba is built differently. It is hierarchical, like a convolutional network: for a 224-pixel input its four stages work on grids of 56, 28, 14, 7 patches per side (3136 tokens in the first stage), with no class token and no position embeddings. Does scanning four ways beat one? VMamba's Table 10 says yes, by a little: 82.26 top-1 with one route, 82.60 with four. Remove the small depthwise convolution in front of the scan and the gap grows, 80.88 against 82.25. Our reading: that convolution already mixes each patch with its two-dimensional neighbours, which is much of what the extra routes add.

One detail points ahead to chapter 11. VMamba's final models keep a state of size N = 1 per channel, against 16 in its first version and in Mamba. Cutting it (Table 5, step e.2) raised throughput to 1942 images per second at 81.87% top-1, and later steps won the accuracy back. The whole redesign in that table runs from 82.17% at 426 images per second to 82.60% at 1686. For ImageNet-sized inputs, the state hardly needs to hold anything. What a state of fixed size can and cannot hold is the question of the next chapter.

Inside Vision Mamba: forward, then backward

The counterpoint: is an image a long sequence?

MambaOut (Yu and Wang, 2024) asks the blunt question: do we need the SSM for vision at all? Its argument starts from cost. An SSM's advantage over attention is that its cost stays linear in the sequence length L, so the question is when attention's quadratic part actually dominates. Count the operations of one Transformer block with an MLP four times as wide as the model:

\text{FLOPs} = \htmlData{step=1, link=linear}{24D^2L} + \htmlData{step=2, link=quad}{\htmlClass{t-brown}{4DL^2}}, \qquad \htmlData{step=3, link=threshold}{r_L = \frac{\htmlClass{t-brown}{4DL^2}}{24D^2L} = \frac{L}{6D}}

MambaOut Eq. 6 and Eq. 7.

  • 24D^2L: the projections and the MLP, applied to each of the L tokens separately. Linear in L.
  • \htmlClass{t-brown}{4DL^2}: attention's scores and weighted sum, every token against every token. Quadratic in L.
  • r_L = L / 6D: the quadratic part wins only when the sequence is longer than six times the width. For ViT-S (D = 384) the threshold is 2304 tokens; for ViT-B (D = 768), 4608; for Vim-Ti's width of 192 it would be 1,152.

ImageNet at 224 pixels with 16-pixel patches is 196 tokens, so at ViT-S width the ratio is 0.09: short. Detection on COCO at 800 by 1280 pixels and segmentation on ADE20K at 512 by 2048 give about 4K tokens: long. Move the figure's resolution and width to watch the verdict flip. The second half of the argument is about direction. An SSM mixes tokens causally, and MambaOut shows that causal mixing hurts image understanding: making ViT-S's attention causal and retraining drops it from 79.8 to 78.9. Two scans soften this, but each branch on its own is still causal.

Claim card: ImageNet classification does not need the SSM, detection and segmentation may (MambaOut)

Claim. An SSM is worth having for long sequences; ImageNet classification is short, so it does not need one, while detection and segmentation are long enough to benefit.

How it was measured. MambaOut stacks Gated CNN blocks: Mamba's block with the SSM removed, so the only token mixer is a 7x7 depthwise conv. It is trained with its own depths, widths and recipe (batch 4096) and compared with the published rows of visual Mamba models: ImageNet top-1, COCO with Mask R-CNN, ADE20K with UperNet. Not held fixed: everything except the token mixer differs between rows. It is a comparison across models, not a same-model ablation (our reading).

Result. ImageNet: MambaOut-Tiny 82.7 against 82.5 for VMamba's final tiny model (MambaOut calls it VMambaV9-T; VMamba's own table gives 82.6), tied with LocalVMamba-T at 82.7. COCO box AP: 45.1 against 47.4. ADE20K mIoU: 47.4 against 48.3. Without the SSM it ties on the short task and trails on the long ones, as the hypothesis predicts. (These detection rows use Mask R-CNN; Vim's use Cascade Mask R-CNN, so the two papers' detection numbers are not comparable.)

What would have falsified it. MambaOut matching VMamba on COCO and ADE20K too, or falling clearly behind on ImageNet. The cleaner test, which the paper does not run, removes the SSM from one visual Mamba at matched compute and recipe; a drop on ImageNet there would refute the first half.

A second paper explains why Mamba does well on images in the first place, using the lens of chapter 9. MLLA (Mamba-Like Linear Attention; the final manuscript renames its model MILA) writes the selective scan beside causal linear attention and lines up the terms: C plays the query, B the key, the input the value, and the state is linear attention's running sum of keys times values. It finds six differences.

Claim card: the forget gate and the block design carry Mamba's gain in vision (MLLA)

Claim. In vision, Mamba behaves like single-head linear attention with an input gate (\Delta), a forget gate (\bar A), a skip connection (D) and no normalization, inside Mamba's block design. Of these, the forget gate and the block design do most of the work.

How it was measured. Start from Swin-T with its softmax attention replaced by linear attention. Add or remove one difference at a time and report ImageNet top-1 and throughput (Table 1). Then remove the forget gate and try position encodings in its place (Table 2).

Result. The baseline scores 77.6 at 1152 images per second. Adding the forget gate gives 78.4, but throughput falls to 743, because the gate turns the computation back into a recurrence. Adopting Mamba's block design gives 80.9. Position encodings can take the forget gate's place at no recurrent cost: 81.6 with LePE, 81.7 with CPE. The paper's reading is that images need local bias and positional information more than a causal decay.

What would have falsified it. The forget gate adding nothing on its own, or position encodings failing to replace it. Both are tested directly, one change at a time, on one architecture.

Put together, the vision papers draw a line through this survey's argument. Where sequences are short and every patch can be seen at once, the scan's direction is a cost and attention or a convolution does as well; where sequences grow to thousands of tokens, the fixed-size state pays for itself. But a fixed state has a price of its own. The next chapter measures what an SSM forgets that attention does not.
