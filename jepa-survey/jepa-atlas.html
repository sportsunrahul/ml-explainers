What a JEPA does

One motion: see, hide, predict, measure

A JEPA learns from unlabeled data by hiding part of an input and predicting the hidden part. The twist is where it predicts. It does not paint the missing pixels. It predicts the embedding of the hidden part from the embedding of the visible part, and the loss is the distance between the guess and the target embedding.

The stage shows one image cut into patches. Each patch rises to its embedding (blue). A block of patches is hidden (yellow). The predictor's guess (green) lands near the hidden embeddings, and the red segment is the miss that training shrinks. Drag to turn the scene.

Main findings of the survey

  • Predicting in embedding space lets the model skip unpredictable detail. On ImageNet-1k, I-JEPA ViT-H/14 reaches 79.3% linear-probe top-1 in under 72 hours on 16 A100s, with about 5 times fewer iterations than pixel reconstruction (MAE).
  • Nothing in the loss forbids the trivial answer "every embedding is the same". JEPAs avoid by an asymmetry: an , a and a predictor. LeJEPA replaces the asymmetry with one regulariser and one hyperparameter.
  • The same recipe scales to video (V-JEPA, V-JEPA 2) and, with an action-conditioned predictor, becomes a world model that plans a real robot arm from 62 hours of robot video.
  • The field has grown from 7 arXiv papers in 2023 to 447 in 2026 up to the scan date of 8 October; one third of them are about world models, planning and robotics.

A JEPA predicts what it cannot see, in the space where things mean something, not in the space of pixels.

Where the loss lives

Three ways to fill in a blank

Reconstruct the pixels

Autoencoders and masked autoencoders (MAE) hide patches and paint them back. The loss is measured on every pixel. The network must reproduce leaves, texture and sensor noise, so most of its capacity goes to detail that carries no meaning.

Move the slider: as the hidden block gets more unpredictable detail, the pixel loss grows, even though nothing about the scene changed. Balestriero and LeCun (2402.11337) show that the pixel directions that explain 90% of the variance give worse classifiers than the bottom 20%.

Make two views agree

Contrastive and self-distillation methods (SimCLR, BYOL, DINO) take two augmented views of one image and pull their embeddings together. No pixels are predicted. The cost is a hand-made list of augmentations, and the embedding must be invariant to all of them, which throws away position and layout.

The two blue points are the two views. Training pulls them together. The trivial solution, every point at the same place, would also pull them together; that is the collapse problem of chapter 4.

Predict the embedding

A JEPA encodes the visible part (blue) and the hidden part (yellow) separately, then a predictor maps the first embedding to the second. The loss lives in embedding space. The target encoder may drop the leaves and keep "a tree", so the predictor never has to guess noise.

Move the slider again: the embedding loss stays flat as the hidden block gets noisier, because the target encoder is free to ignore what cannot be predicted. This is the whole argument for the family. data2vec (2202.03555) is the closest predecessor: it predicts a teacher's averaged layer outputs.

Where you measure the error decides what the network must learn. Measure it in embedding space, and the network learns meaning.

I-JEPA

Predict the hidden block, in embedding space

Masks

Mask

A cuts a 224 px image into a 14 x 14 grid of patches. I-JEPA samples four target blocks (each 15% to 20% of the image, aspect ratio 0.75 to 1.5) and one context block (85% to 100% of the image) and removes every target patch from the context.

Encode

The context encoder (a ViT) sees only the context patches and outputs one vector per patch. The target encoder is a copy of it updated by an ; it sees the whole image, and its outputs at the target positions are the things to predict. No gradient ever flows into it.

On the stage, context patches turn into blue embeddings and target patches into yellow ones. The target encoder sees everything, so a target embedding knows its surroundings; masking its input instead costs 11 points (Table 11: 67.3 vs 56.1).

Predict

The predictor is a narrow ViT. It reads the context embeddings plus one mask token per target patch (a learned vector plus that patch's position), and outputs a guess for each target embedding. Because it is told where to predict, it must model layout, not just content.

Green arrows run from the context embeddings to each target position. The predictor's width is 384, narrower than the encoder (1024 for ViT-L); a narrower predictor is better (Table 14: 70.7 vs 68.4 at width 1024).

Loss

The loss is the mean squared distance between each guess and the target encoder's output at that patch. Gradients update the context encoder and the predictor. The target encoder follows by EMA with momentum rising from 0.996 to 1.0.

L = (1/M) sum over target blocks i, patches j in block i of || pred_j - s_y,j ||^2

The red segments are the per-patch misses. Switch the targets to pixels and the same architecture drops from 66.9 to 40.7 on 1% ImageNet, even with 800 epochs instead of 500 (Table 7).

ImageNet-1k linear probe, ViT-H/14, 300 epochs (Table 1)79.3

Large target blocks force semantic prediction; embedding targets let the model skip the pixels. Both are needed.

Why it does not collapse

An asymmetry, not a loss term

Stop-grad
Predictor
EMA target
Depth

"Output the same vector for everything" has zero prediction error. Nothing in the I-JEPA loss forbids it. What prevents it is an asymmetry between the two branches: the target branch gets no gradient (), the online branch has an extra predictor, and the target weights lag behind ().

The stage trains a tiny linear JEPA live, following the deep linear self-distillation analysis of Littwin et al. (2407.03475). The data has a slow axis (predictable), a noisy axis (unpredictable) and a mixed one. Watch the blue cloud: with stop-gradient and a predictor, the noisy axis flattens and the slow axis survives. Turn stop-gradient off and the whole cloud shrinks to a point.

What the theory says

  • Slow features win (2211.10831). A predictive loss between two moments in time keeps the features that change slowly and drops the fast, noisy ones.
  • Order of learning (2407.03475). In a deep linear model, each data axis is learned at a rate set by its predictability over its noise, raised to the depth. Noisy axes are never learned, which is the implicit regulariser.
  • A hidden prior (2210.07277). Methods that pull two views together assume balanced classes. On class-imbalanced batches MSN and VICReg lose 17.7 points on ImageNet; MAE and data2vec lose under 1 point.
SimSiam, ImageNet linear eval with vs without stop-gradient (2011.10566, Fig. 2)67.7 vs 0.1

JEPAs do not forbid collapse. They make the trivial answer unreachable by gradient descent, and the same asymmetry prefers slow, predictable features.

V-JEPA: masks through time

A video is a block of patches; hide tubes, not frames

Mask

V-JEPA (2404.08471) feeds 16 frames, cut into tubelets of 2 frames x 16 x 16 pixels, so a clip is an 8 x 14 x 14 block of tokens. The masks span every time slot. If they did not, the predictor could copy a hidden patch from the next frame and never learn motion. The loss is an L1 distance between predicted and target embeddings, with no pixels and no contrastive pairs.

The stage is the token block with the paper's two mask types, sampled with the paper's block sizes. The "one frame only" option is not the paper's setting; it shows why the tubes are needed. The paper's masks hide about 90% of the clip on average.

V-JEPA 2: plan in embedding space

Freeze the encoder, condition the predictor on actions, search

V-JEPA 2 (2506.09985) first scales the video recipe: over 1 million hours of video, a 1B-parameter ViT-g encoder, progressive resolution and 64-frame clips. Then it freezes that encoder and trains a 300M-parameter action-conditioned predictor on about 62 hours of robot video (Droid). The predictor is a world model: given the current embedding and an action, it predicts the next embedding.

The stage is a toy latent space with a hand-written dynamics function, but the planner runs the paper's algorithm: the cross-entropy method () samples action sequences, rolls them through the predictor, scores each by the distance of its final embedding to the goal embedding (red), and refits the sampling distribution to the best ones. "Act one step" executes the first action and replans.

LeJEPA: the shape of a good embedding

Replace the heuristics with one test for one distribution

Cloud
Direction

LeJEPA (2511.08544) asks which distribution of embeddings is best for every downstream task and proves it is the isotropic Gaussian. It then enforces that shape directly with : project the embeddings onto random directions and test each 1D projection against a standard normal with the Epps-Pulley statistic. The EMA target, stop-gradient and the rest of the heuristics go away; one hyperparameter remains.

The stage runs Algorithm 1 of the paper on a toy cloud. Pick a cloud shape and a test direction; the histogram compares the projection with N(0, 1). An isotropic Gaussian passes in every direction. The stretched cloud passes only along its unit-variance axis, and the clumpy one fails almost everywhere.

ImageNet-1k frozen linear probe, ViT-H/14 (abstract; the tables report ViT-L at 77.1)79%

The training loss tracks downstream accuracy with a Spearman correlation of about 85% (nearly 99% after a rescaling), so model selection needs no labels. The same recipe trains ResNets, ConvNeXts and ViTs up to 1.8B parameters.

Say which shape the embeddings must have, and test for it. The collapse problem becomes a statistics problem.

The family, 2022 to 2026

Where the 569 JEPA papers went

An arXiv scan on 2026-10-08 (title and abstract queries for JEPA and "joint-embedding predictive") finds 569 unique papers: 1 in 2022, 7 in 2023, 38 in 2024, 76 in 2025 and 447 in 2026 so far. The October 2026 bar is a partial month.

The three views: monthly counts, counts per domain (hand-assigned from titles and abstracts), and the lineage of the papers this tour is built on. In the lineage, blue is the image line, yellow the video line, green the world-model line and red the theory line.

Branches worth knowing

  • Image world models (IWM, 2403.00504): tell the predictor which augmentation separates context and target, and it learns to simulate it; the predictor can then be fine-tuned cheaply.
  • DINO-WM, PLDM, LeWorldModel, H-JEPA: planning with frozen or jointly trained latent world models; LeWorldModel plans Push-T at 96% success with about 15M parameters and two loss terms; H-JEPA stacks levels for long horizons (AntMaze 18% to 73%).
  • V-JEPA 2.1: a loss on every token and intermediate layers makes the features spatially dense without losing the global picture.
  • Beyond vision: audio, graphs, molecules, time series, medical signals and language each have JEPA variants in the scan; the mechanism does not depend on pixels.

The idea is one sentence: predict the embedding of what you cannot see. The field is still finding out how far that sentence goes.

EMA target encoder

The target encoder has the same architecture as the context encoder, but its weights are not trained by gradient descent. After every step they move a little toward the context encoder's weights: w_target = m * w_target + (1 - m) * w_context, with the momentum m close to 1 (I-JEPA raises it from 0.996 to 1.0 over training). The target therefore changes slowly and always lags behind.

BYOL introduced this for two-view learning. In a JEPA it gives the predictor a stable thing to aim at and is one of the asymmetries that keep the embeddings from collapsing.

Stop-gradient

The loss compares the prediction with the target embedding, but the gradient is only sent back through the prediction branch. The target branch is treated as a constant. Without this, the fastest way to reduce the loss is to move both branches to the same constant output.

SimSiam measured it directly: the same model reaches 67.7% ImageNet linear accuracy with the stop-gradient and 0.1% without.

Vision transformer and patches

A vision transformer (ViT) cuts the image into a grid of square patches (16 x 16 pixels for ViT-B/16, so 14 x 14 = 196 patches at 224 px), turns each patch into a vector, adds a position code, and runs attention layers over the set. The output is one vector per patch, which is what a JEPA predicts.

For video, the patch becomes a tubelet: 2 frames x 16 x 16 pixels.

Linear probe and attentive probe

To test a frozen encoder, train a small head on top of its features with labels and report that head's accuracy. A linear probe averages the patch vectors and fits a linear classifier. An attentive probe learns a small attention layer that chooses which patches to look at before classifying.

V-JEPA reports that attentive probing adds about 17 points on Kinetics-400 over average pooling, for V-JEPA and pixel-reconstruction models alike.

Cross-entropy method (CEM)

A gradient-free planner. Keep a Gaussian over action sequences. Sample many sequences, roll each through the world model, score each by how close its predicted final embedding lands to the goal embedding, keep the best few (the elites), and refit the Gaussian to them. Repeat. V-JEPA 2-AC uses 800 samples, 10 iterations and the top 10 elites, with a horizon of 1 for the robot tasks.

Collapse

Complete collapse: every input maps to the same embedding, so every prediction is perfect and useless. Dimensional collapse: the embeddings use only a few directions of the space. In the energy view of LeCun (2022), a collapsed model has a flat energy landscape: every pair is equally compatible.

Contrastive methods fight it by pushing wrong pairs apart. JEPAs rely on an asymmetry (EMA, stop-gradient, predictor). LeJEPA regularises the embedding distribution directly.

SIGReg

Sketched isotropic Gaussian regularisation. Draw random unit directions, project the batch of embeddings onto each, and compute the Epps-Pulley statistic: a weighted integral of the squared difference between the empirical characteristic function of the projection and that of a standard normal. Minimising the sum over directions pushes the embedding cloud toward an isotropic Gaussian, which the LeJEPA paper proves is the distribution that minimises worst-case downstream risk. The minibatch bias of the statistic is of order 1/N.
