Act ILearning without labels

Three ways to fill in a blank

Cover part of a photo with your hand. You can still say what is under it: more sky, more leaves, the rest of the road. You learned that without anyone labelling photos for you. Self-supervised learning is the attempt to give a network the same teacher: the data itself. Hide part of an input, ask the network to predict it, and grade the guess. The whole question of this page is where to grade the guess.

The first route is the obvious one. Guess the hidden pixels and grade every one of them. This is what an autoencoder does, and what MAE (masked autoencoders, 2021) does at scale: it hides 75% of an image's patches, lets a transformer see only the rest, and asks a small decoder to paint the missing pixels, with a mean squared error on pixels, counted only over the hidden patches. It works surprisingly well for fine-tuning: a huge model reaches 87.8% ImageNet accuracy after being tuned on the labels. But look at the toy. The best pixel guess for a leafy region is a blur, and the loss punishes every leaf the blur got wrong, even though no one could have known where that leaf was. The network must spend capacity on unpredictable detail.

The second route refuses to predict anything. Take two views of the same image (two crops, two color jitters) and push their embeddings together. SimCLR, BYOL, SimSiam, DINO and VICReg all live here; they differ in how they stop the embeddings from becoming one constant vector, which is the trivial way to make two things agree. That failure mode has a name, collapse, and it gets its own chapter. The cost of this route is subtler: the model is told that the two views are the same, so whatever differs between them (position, crop, color) is explicitly thrown away. Someone has to hand-design the views so that what is thrown away is unimportant.

The third route is the one this page is about. Hide part of the input, but predict its embedding, not its pixels: a short vector that says what is there, produced by an encoder, graded against the encoder's own description of the hidden part. In the toy, four numbers are graded instead of 192 pixels, and when noise is added to the target the embedding loss barely moves while the pixel loss jumps. A real encoder is free to learn a description that keeps "leaves, this green, this texture" and drops "this exact leaf here". That freedom is the whole idea; the rest of the page is how to make it work without collapsing.

Why not just reconstruct?

Claim card: reconstruction fits the wrong pixels first (Balestriero and LeCun, 2024, 2402.11337v1)

Claim. A reconstruction loss is dominated by the directions of highest pixel variance, and those are the least useful for perception; a network trained to reconstruct learns the useful directions "exponentially slower". The paper does not train MAE; it is a theory paper with controlled experiments.

How it was measured. In closed form for a linear encoder and decoder (Theorem 1, an alignment score, Eq. (7)), then by projecting images onto the top or the bottom principal directions of pixel variance and training a ResNet9 classifier on the projected images.

Result. On TinyImagenet, keeping the top directions that explain 90% of pixel variance gives 45% test accuracy; keeping the bottom directions, only 20% of the variance, gives 55%. On Imagenet-10, autoencoders with the same reconstruction loss ranged from 50% to almost 90% probe accuracy depending on whether a supervised signal was added (Fig. 5): the pixel loss cannot tell good features from bad ones.

What would falsify it. The top-variance projection beating the bottom one at matched variance on natural images, or a deep autoencoder fitting the low-variance directions first during training.

The ancestors, in one table

The third route did not appear from nowhere. Each of these papers supplied a part that I-JEPA (next act) assembles: the slow-moving teacher, the predictor, the idea of regressing a teacher's hidden features instead of pixels, and masking inside a two-branch network.

Names used from here on: an encoder turns an input into an embedding; a target encoder or teacher is a copy of it that produces the thing to predict and receives no gradient; a predictor maps one embedding to another; the EMA (exponential moving average) is the slow-averaging rule that updates the teacher from the student.

The energy view (LeCun, 2022)

Before I-JEPA existed, the recipe was written down as a diagram in a A Path Towards Autonomous Machine Intelligence (Version 0.9.2, 2022-06-27), and taught in a set of lectures that became an arXiv tutorial (Dawid and LeCun, 2306.02572v1), which is the source this chapter quotes. The idea rests on one change of viewpoint: stop thinking of a model as a function that outputs an answer, and think of it as a function that scores how well an answer fits.

Energy: a score, not an answer

An energy-based model assigns a number F(x, y) to every pair of an input x and a candidate y. Low means "these go together"; high means "they do not". To answer a question, you do not run the network forward once: you search for the y with the lowest energy. The tutorial puts it in one line (Sec. 3):

\htmlData{step=1, link=surface}{F(x, y)} \;\text{low for compatible pairs},\qquad \htmlData{step=2, link=marker}{\check{y} = \arg\min_y F(x, y)}

Sec. 3 of the tutorial (Dawid and LeCun, 2023), the highlighted definition; the energy is used for inference only, not as the training loss.

  • x: what you are given (the context, the visible patches, the past frames).
  • y: a candidate for what you are asked about (the hidden block, the next frame).
  • F: the scalar score; the surface in the figure. Inference walks downhill on it.

Why bother? Because a function can only return one answer, but many questions have several good answers: the hidden block could contain any of a hundred arrangements of leaves. An energy surface can have several valleys. The tutorial's second move handles the leftover ambiguity with a latent variable z: a small extra input that "captures the information in y that is not readily available in x" (Sec. 3.2). The energy of a pair is then the best energy over all choices of z:

\htmlData{step=1, link=surface}{F(x, y)} = \htmlData{step=2, link=valley}{\min_{z} E(x, y, z)}

Sec. 3.2 of the tutorial (written there with argmin; the minimised form, as in its Fig. 4). The tutorial warns that the capacity of z must be limited, or the model can hide the whole answer inside it.

Collapse, and the two ways out

Here is the trap. If the energy is computed by comparing two embeddings, F(x, y) = D(s_x, s_y), nothing stops both encoders from outputting the same constant: every pair then has zero energy, the surface is flat (press the button above), and the model has learned nothing. The tutorial's Fig. 5 contrasts this "joint-embedding architecture (can collapse!)" with plain regression, which cannot collapse because it must output actual pixels. The price of predicting in embedding space is that you must pay for the shape of the landscape.

Two families pay for it (Sec. 4 of the tutorial). Contrastive methods pick specific wrong answers and push their energy up, as in the hinge loss of Eq. (3); negative log-likelihood turns out to be a contrastive method too, pushing up every other answer at once (Eq. (4)). The cost: in a high-dimensional space there are too many wrong answers to push. Regularised methods instead limit the total volume of low-energy space, by architecture (a bottleneck, a small code) or by a term in the loss that measures that volume. VICReg's variance term is the tutorial's worked example. Hold on to this split: every anti-collapse trick on this page, from the EMA teacher to LeJEPA's single regulariser, is one of these two.

JEPA, as first drawn

Now put the pieces together. Two encoders turn x and y into embeddings; a predictor, given the embedding of x and a latent z, guesses the embedding of y; the energy is the distance between the guess and the real thing. That is the Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture of the tutorial's Fig. 9:

\htmlData{step=1, link=surface}{F(x, y, z)} = D\big(\htmlData{step=3, link=marker}{\htmlClass{t-green}{\tilde{s}_x}},\ \htmlData{step=2, link=valley}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{s_y}}\big), \qquad \htmlClass{t-green}{\tilde{s}_x} = \mathrm{Pred}(\htmlClass{t-blue}{s_x}, z),\quad \htmlClass{t-blue}{s_x} = \mathrm{Enc}_x(x),\quad \htmlClass{t-yellow}{s_y} = \mathrm{Enc}_y(y)

Labels of Fig. 9 in the tutorial (the energy is not a numbered equation there). The two encoders may differ and share no weights; x and y may even be different modalities.

  • s_x: the context embedding; blue throughout this page.
  • s_y: the target embedding, produced by the other encoder; yellow.
  • \tilde{s}_x: the predictor's guess of s_y; green.
  • D: a distance; the loss, red, lives here.

Three things follow from the picture, and each becomes a paper. First, the target encoder may discard: if some detail of y is unpredictable from x, the cheapest way to lower the energy is for \mathrm{Enc}_y to stop representing it. That is a feature when the detail is noise (the leaves) and a bug when the encoder discards everything (collapse). The training loss drawn in the tutorial's Fig. 10 therefore has four terms: the prediction error, a cost on the information in z, and two terms that keep s_x and s_y informative about their inputs. Second, with x = the past and y = the future, the predictor is a world model (Act IV). Third, JEPAs can be stacked: a hierarchical JEPA (Fig. 11) runs a second JEPA on top of the first's embeddings, predicting further ahead with fewer details, "since which information is important may be scale-dependent". That diagram waited four years for a working version (chapter 12).

Act IIJEPA made real

I-JEPA: predicting the missing block

In January 2023 the diagram became a model. I-JEPA (Assran et al., 2301.08243v3, CVPR 2023) takes one image, hides a few large blocks, encodes the rest, and predicts the hidden blocks' embeddings as produced by a second, slowly-updated copy of the encoder. No pixels are painted. No second augmented view is made. The two choices that make it work are both about the mask: the targets must be big enough to mean something, and the context must be spread out enough to say something.

Four parts and one loss

Walk the picture. The image is cut into patches (196 for a 224-pixel image with 16-pixel patches). The target encoder sees all of them and produces one vector per patch; the yellow blocks select which of those vectors are the targets. This order matters: masking the target encoder's output rather than its input is worth 67.3 vs 56.1 in the paper's Table 11 (a ViT-H/16, low-shot probe), because the targets are then contextual: the vector for a leaf patch already knows it sits in a tree. The context encoder sees only the blue patches, on average a quarter of the image (0.25, Table 6). The predictor, a narrow transformer of width 384, takes the context vectors plus one mask token per target patch: a shared learned vector with that patch's position added, which is how the predictor knows where it is being asked to guess. It is run once per target block and outputs one vector per target patch. The loss is the squared distance between guess and target, summed over the patches of a block and averaged over the four blocks:

\htmlData{step=1, link=loss}{\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}} \htmlData{step=2, link=target}{\sum_{j \in B_i}} \big\lVert \htmlData{step=3, link=context}{\htmlClass{t-green}{\hat{s}_{y_j}}} - \htmlData{step=2, link=target}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{s_{y_j}}} \big\rVert_2^2, \qquad \htmlClass{t-green}{\hat{s}_y(i)} = g_\phi\big(\htmlClass{t-blue}{s_x}, \{m_j\}_{j \in B_i}\big)

Sec. 3 of I-JEPA, paragraph "Loss" (the display equation there is unnumbered; the paper calls it "the average L2 distance" although the inner sum is not averaged).

  • M: the number of target blocks, 4; B_i: the patch indices inside block i.
  • s_{y_j}: the target encoder's vector for patch j; nothing is normalised, and no gradient flows into it.
  • \hat{s}_{y_j}: the predictor's guess for that patch, from the context vectors s_x and the mask tokens m_j.
  • g_\phi: the predictor, with parameters \phi; the context encoder has \theta; the target encoder's weights are an average of past \theta and have no parameters of their own.

What stops collapse here? Nothing in the loss. The target encoder's weights are an exponential moving average of the context encoder's, with momentum 0.996 rising to 1.0 over training (BYOL's rule, Eq. (1) in chapter 1), and the predictor sits between the two. Why those two asymmetries are enough is the subject of chapter 5.

Why the mask is the design

Every ablation in the paper points at the same lesson: the task has to be hard in the right way. With four targets, 54.2% low-shot accuracy (ViT-B/16, 1% of ImageNet labels, linear probe); with one target block, 9.0%; two, 22.0%; three, 48.5% (Table 10). Shrink the targets to scale (0.075, 0.2) and the score falls to 19.2% (Table 8): a tiny target can be filled by copying its neighbours, so nothing semantic needs to be learned. Make them too large, (0.2, 0.3), and it falls to 33.6%: there is not enough left to predict from. The alternatives in the figure fare worse still: predicting three quadrants from one (rasterised) gives 15.5%, a single big block 20.2%, random patches 17.6% (Table 6). And the question this whole page asks, pixels or embeddings, has its cleanest answer in Table 7: the same ViT-L/16 trained to predict the target encoder's output reaches 66.9%, trained to predict pixels 40.7%, even though the pixel run trained longer (800 vs 500 epochs).

What it bought

With a frozen encoder and a linear classifier on ImageNet-1k: 72.9% for ViT-B/16, 77.5% for ViT-L/16, 79.3% for ViT-H/14 and 81.1% for ViT-H/16 at 448 pixels (Table 1). MAE, the pixel route with the same backbones, gets 68.0, 76.0 and 77.2 after 1600 epochs. The view-invariance methods that hand-design augmentations are still ahead on this one number (iBOT ViT-L/16: 81.0; DINO ViT-B/8: 80.1), but they lose on the low-level tasks those augmentations throw away: counting objects in Clevr, I-JEPA's ViT-H/14 86.7 vs DINO 86.6 and iBOT 85.7; judging depth, 72.4 vs 53.4 and 62.8 (Table 4). The compute story is the one the authors lead with: ViT-H/14 trains on 16 A100 GPUs in under 72 hours, under 1200 GPU hours, which the paper puts at 2.5x less than a small iBOT ViT-S/16 and 10x less than MAE's ViT-H/14. Each step is about 7% slower than MAE's; it needs roughly 5x fewer of them.

One training step, end to end

Not narrated. The stepper above shows the forward and backward pass of one step; this section is the bookkeeping around it, from the paper's Appendix A and the released ViT-H/14 configuration. Things the sources do not state are marked.

  1. Batch. 2048 images per step across 16 A100 80G GPUs (README), so 128 per GPU (code-only), in bfloat16 (code-only). Each image: random resized crop to 224; no other augmentation.
  2. Masks, in the data loader. Per image, four target blocks and one context block from the ranges in the chapter; at least 10 patches kept (code-only). Within a GPU every image gets the same block sizes, so the context and target tensors are rectangular.
  3. Forward. Target encoder (no gradient, EMA weights) on all patches; context encoder on the context patches; predictor once per target block with mask tokens.
  4. Loss. Summed squared L2 per block, averaged over the four blocks (the chapter's equation).
  5. Backward and update. AdamW on context encoder and predictor. Learning rate: linear warm-up from 1e-4 to 1e-3 over 15 epochs, then cosine to 1e-6 (the released config instead starts at 2e-4 and warms up for 40 epochs; code-only, and the paper and config disagree). Weight decay: linear from 0.04 to 0.4. EMA momentum: linear from 0.996 to 1.0.
  6. Length. 300 epochs for ViT-H/14 (600 for ViT-B/16); about 626 steps per epoch at batch 2048 (our reading; the ImageNet size is not in the sources). Total: 16 A100 GPUs for under 72 hours.
  7. Evaluation. The target encoder is the model that ships: its last layer, average-pooled over patches (or the last 4 layers concatenated, whichever is better), under a linear probe.

Why doesn't it collapse?

Here is a solution to every prediction loss on this page: make the encoder output the same vector for every input. The target is then constant, the prediction is constant, the distance is zero, and nothing has been learned. The energy chapter called this collapse. I-JEPA's loss has no term against it. So why does I-JEPA learn anything at all?

The trivial solution, and three ways to dodge it

Press "Train" with the defaults and watch. The cloud keeps its width along the slow axis, flattens along the noisy one, and the three readouts settle near the values the theory predicts. Now switch the stop-gradient off: both branches are trained to agree, the cheapest agreement is a constant, and the cloud shrinks to a point. Switch the predictor off instead (the predictor becomes the identity): the point again. These are the two asymmetries that BYOL and SimSiam discovered empirically in 2020 and that I-JEPA inherits, with one addition: I-JEPA's target branch is not the current encoder but a slow average of it (EMA), which in the toy behaves like the stop-gradient.

Why is the asymmetry enough? The paper behind the toy works it out for deep linear networks. Write the encoder as a product of L matrices, the predictor as one more matrix, and block the gradient on the target branch. Then each feature of the data evolves on its own, and the equation that governs it (their Theorem Theorem 4.2, Eq. (7)) differs from the pixel-reconstruction version (Eq. (8)) by a single exponent. Two numbers describe a feature: its covariance with the target, \lambda_i, which sets how fast it is learned, and its regression coefficient \rho_i = \lambda_i / \sigma_i^2, how predictive it is per unit of variance, which sets where its weight ends up:

\htmlData{step=1, link=slow}{\bar{w}_i^{\mathrm{JEPA}}(\infty) = \rho_i^{\,L}}, \qquad \htmlData{step=2, link=noisy}{\bar{w}_i^{\mathrm{MAE}}(\infty) = \rho_i^{\,L/(L+1)}}, \qquad \rho_i = \frac{\lambda_i}{\sigma_i^2}

Corollary 4.3 in Littwin et al., "How JEPA Avoids Noisy Features" (2407.03475v1): the weight the encoder gives feature i after training, for a product of L layers.

  • \bar{w}_i: how much the encoder keeps of feature i (the norm of its column in the product of the layers). The cloud's width along an axis.
  • \rho_i: between 0 and 1. A clean, predictable feature has \rho near 1 and is kept at nearly full weight. A noisy feature (large variance, little covariance) has small \rho and, raised to the power L, nearly vanishes.
  • The MAE exponent L/(L+1) is always below 1: pixel reconstruction keeps noisy features too, however deep the encoder. A one-layer JEPA equals an infinitely deep MAE.

So the asymmetry does two jobs at once. It removes the constant solution (the toy shows what happens without it), and it biases the encoder toward features that are predictive per unit of variance: "JEPA prioritizes influential features, whereas MAE only prioritizes highly covarying features, even if they are noisy". Switch the toy between depth 1 and depth 2 and watch the noisy axis shrink further. The paper's own experiments (deep linear networks of width 100 and depth 5, their Fig. 1) show the learning order inverting between the two objectives when covariance and predictiveness pull in opposite directions.

The trap in the gift: slow features

Claim card: a JEPA keeps whatever is slowest (Sobal et al., 2022, 2211.10831v1)

Claim. A JEPA trained to predict the next embedding is satisfied by features that do not change over time. That is a gift when the nuisance changes every frame (the JEPA ignores it without any tuning) and a trap when the nuisance is fixed: a static background becomes the perfect slow feature, the encoder keeps it, and drops the thing that moves.

How it was measured. Sequences of 17 frames of a dot moving under known actions on a 28-pixel image, with added noise that is either resampled every frame or fixed for the whole sequence; a conv encoder, a recurrent predictor, and VICReg or InfoNCE terms against collapse (no EMA, no stop-gradient: this paper uses the loss-term route). A frozen linear probe then has to recover the dot's position; the metric is its error.

Result. With changing noise the JEPA tracks the dot as well as a pixel-reconstruction baseline; with fixed noise it fails while reconstruction still works (their Fig. 3). On a three-dot version, the VICReg JEPA locates the stationary dot with error 0.066 and the action-controlled dot with 0.277, near the random baseline's 0.235; reconstruction gets both (0.021 and 0.026) (their Table 1).

What would falsify it. The same JEPA, after the same hyper-parameter search, tracking the dot under fixed noise as well as reconstruction does.

The toy has a preset for this: "fixed background" adds an axis that is perfectly constant across the pair. Its \rho is 1, so the encoder weights it fully, while the moving feature, whose \rho is a little below 1, is weighted less, and more so with depth. The two papers describe the same bias from two sides. V-JEPA's answer in the next chapter is to make the masks span time, so that copying the static background does not solve the task.

The cost of the other route

Claim card: the loss-term route carries a hidden prior (Assran et al., 2022, 2210.07277v1)

Claim. Methods that prevent collapse with an explicit "spread out" term (SimCLR, VICReg, SwAV, MSN) are implicitly doing k-means with equal-sized clusters, and so they assume the data is balanced. Methods without such a term (data2vec's self-distillation, MAE) make no such assumption.

How it was measured. Pretrain on ImageNet with mini-batches that are either class-balanced (960 classes per batch) or deliberately imbalanced (2 classes per batch for VICReg, MSN, MAE and data2vec), same batch size and the same overall sampling of every image; then compare linear probes.

Result. ImageNet accuracy drops by -11.2 for SimCLR, -17.7 for MSN and -17.7 for VICReg, but only -0.8 for data2vec and -0.1 for MAE (their Table 1). Replacing the uniform prior with the true class frequencies gains +3.0 on long-tailed iNaturalist (Table 2).

What would falsify it. VICReg or MSN unchanged under two-class batches, or data2vec and MAE dropping by the same amount.

This is the fork the rest of the page keeps returning to. The asymmetry route (EMA, stop-gradient, predictor) is cheap and prior-free but hard to reason about; the loss-term route is explicit but smuggles in assumptions. LeJEPA, in Act III, is an attempt to have a loss term whose assumption is the right one.

V-JEPA: adding time

A video is an image with a third axis. V-JEPA (Bardes et al., 2404.08471v1, first posted 2024-02-15) keeps the I-JEPA recipe and changes only what gets masked: the hidden blocks become tubes that run through every frame. The reason is the trap from the last chapter. If a block were hidden in one frame only, the cheapest prediction copies it from the next frame; a static background would be the perfect slow feature. A tube removes that shortcut, so the predictor has to model what moves.

What changes, and what does not

The clip is 16 frames sampled with a stride of 4 (so about 3 seconds of raw footage, by the paper's Sec. 3.4), resized to 224 pixels. A 3D convolution turns every 2 x 16 x 16 block of pixels into one token, which gives 1568 tokens with 3D sin-cos positions. From here the picture is I-JEPA's: the context encoder sees the visible tokens, the target encoder (EMA, momentum 0.998 to 1.0) sees the whole clip, and a predictor of depth 12 and width 384 fills the holes with mask tokens that carry the hidden positions. Two things differ. Each clip gets 2 masks, one short-range and one long-range, and the context encoder and predictor run once per mask while the target encoder runs once. And the distance is an L1, "which we found to be more stable":

\htmlData{step=1, link=mask}{\min_{\theta,\phi}\ \big\lVert} \htmlData{step=2, link=visible}{P_\phi\big(E_\theta(x),\ \Delta_y\big)} - \htmlData{step=3, link=mask}{\mathrm{sg}\big(\overline{E}_\theta(y)\big)} \big\rVert_1

Eq. (1) in V-JEPA, Sec. 3.1; \mathrm{sg} is the stop-gradient, \overline{E}_\theta the EMA target encoder, \Delta_y the positions of the masked tokens (the only thing the predictor is told; the next chapter adds more).

The masked fraction is high: the paper reports "an average masking ratio of ~90%". Its Table 4 is the argument for tubes. On a ViT-L/16, the multi-block tube mask gives 72.9 on Kinetics-400 and 67.4 on Something-Something v2 (frozen probe, single view); random tubes at the same ratio give 51.5 and 46.4; a causal mask that lets the context see only the first 6 of the 16 frames (the other ten are hidden) gives 61.3 and 49.8. And the page's recurring question, Table 1: the same model trained to predict pixels instead of features drops from 73.7 to 68.6 on Kinetics-400 (frozen), while the gap vanishes once the whole network is fine-tuned (85.6 vs 85.4). Feature prediction makes frozen features better; fine-tuning can repair either.

Reading frozen features: the attentive probe

One evaluation detail is worth knowing because every later paper uses it. Averaging 1568 token vectors into one and training a linear classifier throws away where things are; V-JEPA instead trains a one-layer attentive probe, a learned query that attends over the tokens, then a classifier. On Kinetics-400 that is +17.3 points over average pooling (56.7 to 73.7, Table 3). The frozen encoder knew; the readout had been losing it.

What it bought

Trained on about 2 million videos (VideoMix2M: Kinetics, Something-Something v2 and HowTo100M) for 90K steps (batch 3072 at 224 pixels, 2400 at 384), the frozen ViT-H/16 at 384 pixels reaches 81.9 on Kinetics-400, 72.2 on Something-Something v2 and 77.4 on ImageNet (Table 6; the abstract's 77.9 uses a two-layer probe). The telling number is Something-Something, a dataset of hand motions where the direction of movement is the label: image models such as DINOv2 ViT-g score 50.6 there against 83.4 on Kinetics, where appearance is enough. V-JEPA's lead on motion is about 21 points; its deficit on still images against DINOv2 (86.2) is the price of not having been trained on them, which V-JEPA 2.1 later pays off.

The predictor is a world model

So far the predictor has been told one thing about the hole it fills: where it is. Image World Models (Garrido et al., 2403.00504v1) ask what happens if you also tell it what changed. Build the target from one view of an image and the context from another with a different color jitter, maybe blurred or turned grey, then hand the predictor the parameters of that difference. Now it is not filling a hole; it is simulating a transformation in embedding space. That is what a world model does: given a state and an action, predict the next state.

Invariant or equivariant

The paper's vocabulary: a representation is invariant to a transformation if the embedding does not change under it, and equivariant if the change can be tracked, that is, if a predictor can apply it in embedding space. A JEPA whose predictor is not told the transformation (I-JEPA with color jitter between the views) can only lower its loss by becoming invariant: it discards color. Tell the predictor, and give it enough capacity, and it becomes a "capable" world model; the encoder may then keep color, and the predictor learns to shift it. The action is a small vector: the difference in brightness, contrast, saturation and hue between the two views, plus flags for whether blur, grayscale or solarisation were applied; crop and flip are shared by both views and are not part of it. The loss is I-JEPA's with one more input:

\mathcal{L} = \big\lVert \htmlData{step=1, link=arrow}{\htmlClass{t-green}{p_\phi\big(z_x,\ a_{x\to y},\ m_a\big)}} - \htmlData{step=2, link=target}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{z_y}} \big\rVert_2^2,\qquad z_x = f_\theta(x),\quad z_y = f^{\mathrm{EMA}}_\theta(y)

IWM, Sec. 3 (squared L2, as in I-JEPA; the paper sums this over the target positions, omitted here); a_{x \to y} is the action vector and m_a the mask tokens, each built from a position embedding and the action through a three layer network.

Whether the world model actually becomes equivariant depends on three knobs, and the paper measures it directly: encode a bank of 256 transformed images, ask the predictor to produce the embedding of the transformed version from the original plus the action, and check whether the right image is ranked first (Mean Reciprocal Rank, 1 = always). Table 2: with the default jitter and destructive augmentations, a 12-layer predictor scores 0.09 (invariant) and an 18-layer predictor 0.25 on jitter alone and 0.81 or more once the jitter is made strong; the unconditioned I-JEPA predictor scores 0.00 everywhere. Conditioning, capacity and the difficulty of the transformation all have to be there.

Claim card: a pretrained predictor is a cheap task head (IWM Table 4)

Claim. Once the predictor is a capable world model, fine-tuning the predictor on a downstream task, with the encoder frozen, competes with fine-tuning the whole encoder, at a fraction of the trainable parameters.

How it was measured. ImageNet-1k top-1 with a ViT-B/16 encoder pretrained for 300 epochs; four protocols: fine-tune the encoder alone, freeze the encoder and train a freshly initialised predictor, freeze the encoder and fine-tune the pretrained predictor, or fine-tune everything.

Result. For the equivariant model the pretrained predictor reaches 83.3, a gain of +1.8 over a random-init predictor, matching encoder fine-tuning of the invariant model (83.3); end to end it reaches 84.4. For MAE the same trick gains only +0.3; for I-JEPA +0.9. The trade: the equivariant model's linear probe is worse (67.5 vs 74.5), because keeping color and blur in the embedding makes it less linearly separable.

What would falsify it. A random-init predictor matching the pretrained one on the equivariant model, or the invariant model beating the equivariant one under predictor fine-tuning.

Hold on to the shape of this result. An action-conditioned predictor over frozen features, fine-tuned or planned with, is exactly what V-JEPA 2 does with robot actions two chapters from now, and what Act IV builds planners from.

V-JEPA 2: scale, then act

V-JEPA 2 (Assran et al., 2506.09985v1, June 2025) is two papers in one. The first scales the V-JEPA recipe until a frozen encoder understands video well enough to anticipate what a person will do next. The second freezes that encoder, bolts on a small predictor that reads robot actions, trains it on under 62 hours of robot video, and plans with it on a real arm in a lab the model had never seen. The second part is where the predictor becomes a world model you can use.

Part A: four ways to scale

From 2 million to 22 million samples (over 1 million hours of video, including 1M images tiled into still clips): 84.2 to 85.2. A ViT-g encoder of 1B parameters instead of ViT-L: 86.7. Training for 252K steps instead of 90K, with a learning rate held constant until the probes stop improving: 87.5. Then the trick that makes the last step affordable: only the final 12K "cooldown" steps are run at 64 frames (16 seconds at 4 fps) and 384 pixels: 88.2. Training at that size throughout would have cost roughly 60 GPU-years; progressive resolution is 8.4x cheaper. The recipe underneath is V-JEPA's: tubelets of 2x16x16, 3D rotary positions, masks spanning all frames, an L1 loss on masked tokens, a fixed EMA momentum of 0.99925, and a predictor of 22M parameters that stays the same size for every encoder.

The frozen ViT-g at 384 pixels reads motion better than anything before it: 77.3 on Something-Something v2 and 90.2 on Diving-48 (Table 4). On Epic-Kitchens action anticipation (what will this person do 1 second from now, judged by recall at 5), the encoder plus its predictor scores 39.7 against 27.6 for the best prior model, a 44% relative gain (Table 5). Attached to a language model (Llama 3.1 8B, 88.5 million alignment samples) it averages 59.5 across seven video question-answering benchmarks (Table 8), though an image-text-trained encoder still wins two of them.

Claim card: intuitive physics emerges from video prediction (Garrido et al., 2502.11831v1; tests V-JEPA, 2024, not V-JEPA 2)

Claim. A video JEPA, with no training on physics, can be read as a "surprise" meter: run it over a clip, measure the error between its predicted and actual future embeddings, and physically impossible clips surprise it more. This is the violation-of-expectation protocol used on infants.

How it was measured. Pairs of videos that differ only in one physical violation (an object vanishing behind an occluder, passing through a wall, changing shape), from three benchmarks: IntPhys (3 properties, synthetic), GRASP (10 properties, synthetic) and InfLevel (real videos). The metric is how often the impossible video of a pair gets the higher surprise; chance is 50%. Baselines: a pixel-prediction model (VideoMAEv2), two multimodal LLMs, and 20 untrained networks.

Result. V-JEPA ViT-H: 98% on IntPhys, 66% on GRASP, 62% on InfLevel; the only method significantly above untrained networks on all three (their Fig. 1). On the IntPhys private test set its pairwise error on object permanence is 0.28% against 12.5% for human raters (Table S4). A model trained on only 128h of video keeps over 70%.

What would falsify it. The pixel-prediction baseline or the untrained networks matching V-JEPA on IntPhys, or surprise failing to separate the pairs once the context length is fixed rather than tuned per property (the paper checks this: a fixed context of 2 frames still works).

Part B: V-JEPA 2-AC, a predictor that reads actions

Now freeze the ViT-g and throw away its mask-filling predictor. In its place train a new predictor of 300M parameters (24 layers, width 1024) on the Droid dataset: 23k robot trajectories, successes and failures alike, no labels, no rewards, cut into 4 second clips of 16 frames. Each frame goes through the frozen encoder on its own and comes out as a 16x16x1408 feature map. Each time step also contributes the arm's end-effector state (a 7D vector: position, orientation, gripper) and the action, which is the change in that state to the next frame. The predictor's job: given everything up to step k, predict the encoder's feature map of frame k+1:

Eqs. 2 and 3 in V-JEPA 2, Sec. 3.1; summed as the objective, minimised over the predictor's weights \phi only. Teacher forcing feeds the true feature maps as inputs with T = 15; the rollout term feeds the predictor its own output, with T = 2 so the gradient crosses one recurrent step.   • a_t, s_t: action and end-effector state at step t; each becomes one token through its own small linear map.
  • P_\phi: the action-conditioned predictor; attention is block-causal, so step k's tokens see steps up to k only.

Planning: search the actions, not the pixels

To act, the robot is given a goal image. Encode the current frame and the goal with the frozen encoder; then search for the action whose predicted next feature map is closest, in L1, to the goal's feature map. The search is the Cross-Entropy Method: sample 800 candidate actions from a Gaussian, keep the top 10, refit the Gaussian, repeat 10 times, execute the mean, then look again. The horizon in the robot experiments is 1 step (longer "worked reasonably well" but costs time); each decision takes 16 sec on one RTX 4090.

In two labs the model had never seen, with 10 trials per task (Table 2): reaching, 100%; grasping a cup, 65%; grasping a box, 25%; pick-and-place of the cup, 80%, of the box, 65%. Pick-and-place is given two sub-goal images on the way. Octo, a behaviour-cloning policy pretrained on 1M trajectories, grasps the cup 15% of the time in the same setting; a planner built on Cosmos, a pixel-generating video model, takes 4 min per decision and grasps the cup 0% of the time (Table 3). Zero-shot here means no data from these labs; the arm, a Franka, is the one in Droid. The limits are honest too: the inferred actions rotate with the camera angle (about 1.6 cm of error on 5 cm moves), and the goal must be given as an image.

V-JEPA 2.1: dense features

V-JEPA 2 understood scenes but could not place things precisely: on a semantic segmentation benchmark its frozen features scored 24.4 mIoU where DINOv2 scored 49.5. The reason is in the loss. Only the masked tokens were graded, so the visible tokens were free to become global summaries with no obligation to stay about their own patch. V-JEPA 2.1 (Mur-Labadia et al., 2603.14482v3, first posted 2026-03-15) grades every token, weights the visible ones by how close they are to a hole, and does it at four depths of the encoder at once.

The loss is V-JEPA 2's, plus a second term over the context set C:

\htmlData{step=1, link=masked}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{predict}} = \frac{1}{|M|}\sum_{i\in M} \big\lVert P_\phi(E_\theta(x), \Delta_y)_i - \mathrm{sg}(E_{\bar\theta}(y)_i)\big\rVert_1}, \qquad \htmlData{step=2, link=context}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{ctx}} = \frac{1}{|C|}\sum_{i\in C} \lambda_i \big\lVert P_\phi(E_\theta(x), \Delta_y)_i - \mathrm{sg}(E_{\bar\theta}(y)_i)\big\rVert_1}, \qquad \htmlData{step=3, link=weight}{\lambda_i = \frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{d_{\min}(i, M)}}}

Eqs. 1, 2 and 3 of V-JEPA 2.1 (Sec. 2.3.1); the model minimises their sum, at each of 4 encoder depths.

  • M, C: masked and visible (context) token positions. The predictor now outputs a vector at every position.
  • \lambda_i: the weight of visible token i, largest next to a hole, fading as 1/\sqrt{d}. It is warmed up from zero during training.
  • The target for a visible token is still the EMA encoder's embedding of the clean clip at that position, so predicting it is not free: the context encoder never saw the masked content, the target encoder did.

Why not simply weight every visible token at 1? Because then the easiest solution is to copy: the predictor echoes the context features and the encoder stops building a global picture. The ablation in Table 2 shows it: a constant weight of 1.0 drops Something-Something v2 from 72.8 to 51.1, while the distance-weighted, warmed-up version lands at 62.5 with segmentation up from 22.2 to 33.8 mIoU. Even so, ten points of motion understanding are lost at this step. The second ingredient wins them back: deep self-supervision. The encoder's outputs at four depths (blocks 12, 24, 36, 48 of the ViT-G) are concatenated and fused by a small MLP, the predictor (now 24 blocks instead of 12) produces four outputs per token, and each is graded against the EMA encoder's output at the same depth. Something-Something recovers to 72.1, segmentation climbs to 38.6 (Table 1, rows 2-3). The remaining steps are data (a 142M-image set, and more weight on the 19M-video YT-1B share of the mix), a separate 2D tokenizer for images so that both modalities share one encoder, a 2B ViT-G, and the cooldown at 64 frames: 47.9 mIoU and 77.7 on Something-Something.

What it bought

Against V-JEPA 2's ViT-g, the 2.1 ViT-G (2B) gains +23.4 mIoU on ADE20K, +27.6 on Cityscapes and +20.7 on VOC with a frozen encoder (Table 8); on monocular depth (NYUv2, lower is better) the 2B model's 0.307 edges DINOv3's 7B model at 0.309, though DINOv3 keeps every segmentation column (ADE20K 55.9 vs 47.9). Nothing global was lost at 2B, except a point on Diving-48: Something-Something 77.7 against 77.3, Kinetics 87.7 against 87.3; at the matched 1B size the 2.1 model sits slightly below on these. On the robot, with the same planner settings as V-JEPA 2-AC, grasping goes from 60% to 70%; with a different planner budget (horizon 8, 300 samples, 14 sec per step) it reaches 80%, the 20% the paper leads with. Dense features also make the action-conditioned predictor's planning cheaper: a navigation planner built on it runs in 10.6 seconds where the diffusion-based Navigation World Model (Act IV) needs 103.2.

Act IIICleaning up the recipe

LeJEPA: one regulariser instead of the heuristics

Everything so far avoided collapse with an asymmetry: a slow copy of the encoder, a stop-gradient, a predictor on one side only. It works, but nobody can say what embedding distribution it is aiming at, and every new setup needs its schedules re-tuned. LeJEPA (Balestriero and LeCun, 2025-11-11) starts from the other end. First ask: what should the embeddings look like for any downstream task? Then write a single term that pushes them there, and drop the rest.

What the embeddings should look like

The argument runs through the probe. After pretraining, someone will fit a linear classifier, or a nearest-neighbour rule, or a kernel, on the frozen embeddings, for a task nobody knows yet. Section 3 of the paper asks which embedding distribution makes that probe's error smallest in the worst case over tasks, holding the total variance fixed. Two lemmas show anisotropy (some directions stretched, some squashed) can only increase a linear probe's bias and does increase its variance; Theorem 1 (with Theorems 7 and 8 in the appendix) shows that for nearest-neighbour and kernel probes, among all distributions with that variance, the isotropic Gaussian is the unique minimiser of the integrated squared bias. The reason, in one phrase from our reading: at fixed covariance, the Gaussian has the least Fisher information, the least "structure" for a probe to be misled by.

SIGReg: a statistical test as a loss

So the target is known: embeddings should be distributed as \mathcal{N}(0, I). Testing that in a thousand dimensions is hopeless, but a classical trick makes it cheap. A distribution is Gaussian if and only if its projection onto every direction is Gaussian (Cramer-Wold), so sample a few random unit directions, project the batch onto each, and run a one-dimensional test. The test is Epps-Pulley: compare the empirical characteristic function of the projections with the Gaussian's, under a Gaussian weight:

\htmlData{step=1, link=dir}{\mathrm{SIGReg}_T(\mathcal{A}, \{f_\theta(x_n)\}) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}} T\big(\{a^\top f_\theta(x_n)\}_{n=1}^N\big)}, \qquad \htmlData{step=2, link=hist}{T = \mathrm{EP} = N\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\big|\hat\varphi_X(t) - e^{-t^2/2}\big|^2 w(t)\,dt}

Definition 2 (tagged SIGReg) and the Epps-Pulley equation in Sec. 4.2.3 of LeJEPA (tagged, not numbered). \hat\varphi_X(t) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_n e^{itX_n} is the empirical characteristic function of the projections; w(t) a Gaussian weight; the integral is a trapezoid sum over 17 points.

  • \mathcal{A}: the set of random unit directions, 1024 by default, drawn fresh at every step; the paper argues as few as 16 work because resampling covers the sphere over time.
  • T: any one-dimensional goodness-of-fit statistic; Epps-Pulley is chosen because its gradient is bounded by 4\sigma^2/N per sample for any input (Theorem 4), so it cannot blow up training.
  • Cost: linear in the batch size; no pairwise distances, no prototypes, no queue.

The whole method is then two terms. Views of an image (DINO-style: 2 global crops and several local ones) go through one encoder; every view's embedding is pushed toward the mean of the global views' embeddings (the prediction term), and SIGReg is applied to each view's batch of embeddings:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LeJEPA}} = \htmlData{step=1, link=hist}{\frac{\lambda}{V}\sum_{v=1}^{V}\mathrm{SIGReg}\big(\{z_{n,v}\}_{n=1}^{B}\big)} + \htmlData{step=2, link=cloud}{\frac{1-\lambda}{B}\sum_{n=1}^{B}\frac{1}{V}\sum_{v'=1}^{V}\big\lVert \mu_n - z_{n,v'}\big\rVert_2^2}

Equation tagged (LeJEPA) in Sec. 5.1, with the prediction term in its Eq. (7) form; \mu_n is the mean of sample n's global-view embeddings, V the number of views, B the batch. The single hyperparameter \lambda defaults to 0.05.

Note what is not there. No target encoder, no EMA, no stop-gradient: the gradient flows into the encoder from both sides of the difference. No predictor network: the paper argues it is "only justified when there exists an asymmetry". No prototypes, no register tokens, no whitening, no negatives. What stays: a projector head on the encoder, multi-crop views, and an ordinary learning-rate warm-up. The reference implementation is about 50 lines.

What it bought

The headline is stability rather than a record. The abstract reports 79% ImageNet-1k linear accuracy for a ViT-H/14; the tables give 77.1% for a ViT-L and 78.5% for a ConvNeXt-V2 Huge after 100 epochs (Sec. 6.4), and a ViT-L with 100 epochs transfers slightly better than I-JEPA's ViT-H with 300 (79.48 vs 78.50 average over 8 datasets, Table 2). The same recipe trains over 60+ architectures without per-model tuning, up to a 1.8B ViT-g with a smooth loss curve, and removing the predictor and teacher does not collapse it (Table 4). Two results matter more for practice. The training loss predicts downstream accuracy (Spearman about 85%), so you can select models without labels. And pretraining in-domain on small datasets beats transferring a frontier model: on Galaxy10 (11,000 images) a LeJEPA ResNet-34 reaches 78.17% frozen against 71.38% for DINOv3 (appendix Table 3); though on CIFAR-100 the transferred I-JEPA still wins, 86.93 to 70.44 (Table 5).

Two companions

Claim card: the anti-collapse term estimates the data density (Balestriero et al., 2025-10-07)

Claim. If a JEPA's embeddings are isotropic Gaussian, then by the change-of-variables formula the input density is, up to a constant, the product of the encoder's Jacobian singular values: \log p(x) \propto \sum_i \log \sigma_i(J_f(x)), the "JEPA-SCORE" (their Eq. (5)). A trained JEPA can be read as a density estimator for free.

How it was measured. On synthetic data with a known density, the Pearson correlation between the score and the true log density across dimensions 64, 128, 256, 512; on real data, qualitatively, by ranking ImageNet images and classes by the score under four public encoders.

Result. Correlations from 0.64 to 0.94 on the synthetic tests; low-score images are atypical. Falsified by: a correlation near zero on data with a known density, or by scores that do not change with the dataset.

Claim card: when does LeJEPA learn a world model? (Klindt, LeCun, Balestriero, 2026-05-25)

Claim. If the world's latents evolve under a stationary additive-noise process and the observations are a nonlinear function of them, LeJEPA's two terms recover the latents up to a linear map (linear identifiability), and the Gaussian is the unique latent distribution for which this holds. The optimum of the prediction term is a linear map because every nonlinear component loses correlation across a time step.

How it was measured. Synthetic worlds with known latents up to dimension 1024, measuring the R^2 of a linear regression from embeddings to true latents; a Reacher control environment; the main theorems checked in Lean with zero unproven steps.

Result. R^2 above 0.999 for SIGReg and VICReg-style regularisers in the synthetic worlds and 0.720241 for a contrastive one at 1024 dimensions. Falsified by: low R^2 under the stated assumptions, or linear recovery under a non-Gaussian latent prior.

Act IVWorld models you can plan in

Planning in latent space

A world model is a function that takes the current state and an action and predicts the next state. With one, you can act without ever being told what to do: imagine many sequences of actions, look at where each one ends, keep the one that ends closest to where you want to be. No reward, no policy, no trial-and-error in the real world. V-JEPA 2-AC did exactly this on a robot arm. This chapter is about the three design choices behind such planners: what the state is, how the model is trained, and how the search is run.

Three designs

Frozen features: DINO-WM (Zhou et al., first posted 2024-11-07). Take a pretrained DINOv2 encoder and freeze it; the state is its grid of 14x14 patch features of width 384. Train only a 19M-parameter transformer predictor to map the last few states and actions to the next state, with a latent consistency loss; a decoder is trained separately for pictures and never enters the loop. Plan with CEM inside model-predictive control (100 samples, 10 iterations). Success rates on offline, reward-free data: point maze 0.98, wall 0.96, reach 0.92, push-T 0.90, against DreamerV3's 0.30 on push-T (Table 1). Its Table 2 is the argument for patch features: swap in a single-vector encoder (DINO's class token) and push-T falls to 0.44; an ImageNet ResNet, 0.20. Spatial detail is what planning needs, and a global summary throws it away. Gradient descent as the planner instead of CEM collapses to 0.22 on the maze (Table 8): the energy landscape over actions is not smooth enough to follow downhill.

Claim card: a case for planning with latent dynamics (Sobal et al., PLDM, 2025-02-20)

Claim. Learn only a JEPA latent dynamics model from reward-free offline data (encoder plus action-conditioned predictor, trained end to end from 64-pixel frames with VICReg-style anti-collapse terms, no EMA, no pretrained encoder, about 2218672 parameters), then plan; the quality of the data's policy does not matter, and the planner transfers to new layouts and new tasks by changing only the cost.

How it was measured. Two-room and maze navigation, against six model-free and goal-conditioned RL methods on 23 datasets that vary data quality, quantity, trajectory length, the fraction of door crossings, and held-out layouts.

Result. On good data PLDM reaches 97.8% where a pixel-reconstruction world model gets 26.2% and DreamerV3 24.0% (Tables 2, 6); it generalises best to unseen layouts from as few as 5 training maps. It is not the winner everywhere: with no door crossings in the data, HILP reaches 100.0% and PLDM 34.4%; and it plans about 4x slower than model-free policies. Removing the variance term drops Two-Rooms to 13.4%: the anti-collapse terms are load-bearing.

What would falsify it. The pixel-reconstruction world model matching PLDM on good data, or PLDM failing on held-out layouts where the model-free methods succeed.

The generative contrast: Navigation World Models (Bar et al., first posted 2024-12-04, CVPR 2025). Not a JEPA, and useful for that reason. A 1B-parameter conditional diffusion transformer predicts the pixels (through a VAE) of the next egocentric view given a translation and a turn, trained on about 129 hours of robot navigation video (our sum of the per-dataset hours). Planning samples 120 straight-line trajectories, decodes each imagined final frame and scores it by perceptual similarity to the goal image. It beats the behaviour-cloning policy NoMaD on trajectory error (1.13 vs 1.93 ATE on RECON, Table 2) and can imagine traversals of places it has never seen. The cost is the cost of pixels: each simulated trajectory takes 30.3 seconds, and V-JEPA 2.1's latent planner later matched its error about ten times faster.

The recurring lesson

Every number in this chapter points the same way as chapter 1 did. Scoring a plan by a distance in embedding space works, scoring it in pixels is slow and fragile, and the embedding must keep where things are, not only what they are. What none of these three settle is whether the encoder itself can be trained for planning, from pixels, without a pretrained backbone and without the asymmetry tricks. That is the next chapter.

End to end from pixels, and up the hierarchy

DINO-WM needs a pretrained encoder; PLDM needs seven loss terms with six weights to tune. LeWorldModel (Maes et al., 2026-03-13) takes LeJEPA's answer and applies it to dynamics: an encoder and an action-conditioned predictor trained together from raw pixels with exactly two loss terms, next-embedding prediction and SIGReg. "We do not employ stop-gradient, exponential moving averages, or additional stabilization heuristics." The whole model has about 15M parameters (a ViT-Tiny encoder of 5M, a predictor of 10M) and trains on a single GPU in a few hours.

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LeWM}} = \htmlData{step=1, link=plan}{\big\lVert \hat{z}_{t+1} - z_{t+1}\big\rVert_2^2} + \htmlData{step=2, link=level1}{\lambda\,\mathrm{SIGReg}(Z)}, \qquad \hat{z}_{t+1} = \mathrm{pred}_\phi(z_t, a_t),\quad z_t = \mathrm{enc}_\theta(o_t)

Eqs. 1 to 3 of LeWorldModel, Sec. 3.1. The prediction is teacher-forced (the predictor always sees the true z_t); SIGReg is applied per time step over the batch; \lambda = 0.1 with 1024 random directions. The paper counts this as going from PLDM's six to one loss hyperparameters.

The state is a single vector: the encoder's class token, 192 numbers, passed through a small projector (the ViT's final layer norm "prevents our anti-collapse objective from being optimized effectively"). Actions enter the predictor through adaptive layer norm, zero-initialised so that conditioning switches on gradually. Without SIGReg, the paper states, the prediction loss alone "leads to representation collapse": every input maps to one constant. With it, the planner (CEM, 300 samples, 30 elites, horizon 5) reaches 96% on push-T against DINO-WM's 74% (92% with proprioception) and PLDM's 78%, and 86% on Reacher; it loses on the two-room world (87% vs 100%) and on cube manipulation (74% vs 86%) (Fig. 6). The number the paper leads with is speed: one state token instead of 196 patch tokens makes a full plan take 0.98 seconds where DINO-WM takes 47, a 48x gain (Fig. 3). The trade is the one chapter 10 warned about: a single vector keeps the agent's position well (linear probe error 0.052) but less of the object's pose than a patch grid does (Table 1).

H-JEPA: predicting farther with fewer details

The 2022 diagram had one more box: a second JEPA on top of the first, predicting further ahead from coarser latents. H-JEPA (Zhang et al., 2026-10-05) builds it. Level 1 is a LeWorldModel whose step spans 5 environment steps. Each level above encodes the level below's latent into its own (a two-layer MLP), pools the actions in between into a macro-action, and predicts its own next latent one stride further out; with stride 2, level \ell covers 5 \cdot 2^{\ell-1} environment steps. Every level has LeWM's two-term loss, the levels train jointly, and gradients from the upper losses flow down through the lower encoders.

Planning runs top-down. The top level optimises a short sequence of macro-actions toward the goal in its own latent; its first predicted state becomes the subgoal for the level below, which plans toward it (the cost is measured after lifting the lower prediction into the upper space); and so on until level 1 returns primitive actions. On visual AntMaze with goals 75 steps away, a flat LeWorldModel succeeds 18% of the time, a three-level H-JEPA 73%; on FourRoom with a moving distractor, 40.7% to 96.0%; on cube manipulation 32.0% to 60.0% (Fig. 6). The control that isolates the idea is a hierarchy whose upper encoders are identity maps, so that all levels plan in the level-1 latent: on AntMaze it gets 38.7%, about half. What the upper levels learn to forget is exactly what the 2022 sketch predicted: probes show the ant's leg pose and the distractor's position become unrecoverable at higher levels while the agent's position stays, and this selective forgetting appears only when the environment's fast and slow factors are separated by more than about 2x in frequency (push-T and the cube are not, and gain less). One honest caveat from the same paper: on real robot video (DROID) where the scene changes every episode, SIGReg alone lets the encoder keep the scene and drop the arm, the slow-feature trap of chapter 5 again, and an inverse-dynamics term had to be added. Without SIGReg at all, the effective rank of the latent falls to between 1 and 14 out of 384 (their Fig. 17): the regulariser is doing the work it was designed for.

How big is this field? Claim. At least 569 arXiv papers name JEPA in their title or abstract, and the monthly rate in 2026 is several times that of 2025.

How. The arXiv API, paginated to completion; the raw records with abstracts are kept in the project. What it misses: papers that use the idea without the name (DINO-WM, Navigation World Models and PLDM all needed to be added by hand), non-arXiv venues, and the title-keyword domain split cannot see a paper's method. What would change it: a different query, or a classifier run on the abstracts rather than the titles.

What the field is arguing about

Read the 2026 titles and five questions recur, each with a paper that names it.

  • One future or many? A deterministic predictor returns one latent successor "even when hidden intent, partial observation, or stochastic dynamics make several futures plausible" (Branch-JEPA, 2026-07-06; Var-JEPA and a variational JEPA, 2026-01-20, give the probabilistic answer).
  • Does a single frame know its velocity? "A single-frame embedding carries no velocity information, for any encoder, including the official released LeWM weights" (Frozen in a Frame, 2026-10-03): a world model that encodes one frame at a time cannot represent momentum.
  • Is forward prediction enough? Forward prediction "does not explicitly enforce reliable identifiability of robot-centric physical state" (Physical State Grounding, 2026-08-07); H-JEPA's inverse-dynamics fix on DROID is the same concern.
  • Is the latent distance the right cost? Training "optimizes short-horizon latent prediction, whereas planning requires a multi-step ranking of imagined futures by goal progress" (Temporal-Distance JEPA, 2026-07-28).
  • When does training collapse? A linearised gradient flow gives a per-mode stability ratio and a predicted phase boundary (Drive vs. Decay, 2026-10-01), the 2024 deep-linear theory of chapter 5 pushed further.

Summary

A JEPA hides part of its input and predicts the hidden part's embedding, not its pixels, so the encoder is free to drop what cannot be predicted. The 2022 position paper drew it as an energy-based model with two encoders and a predictor and warned that the picture collapses unless something pays for the shape of the energy landscape. I-JEPA paid with asymmetry (a slow-average target encoder, a predictor) and found that the mask is the design: a few large target blocks, one spread-out context. V-JEPA extended the mask through time so that nothing could be copied from the next frame; theory explained why the asymmetry prefers predictive, low-noise features and why that same preference traps the model on static backgrounds. Image World Models showed that telling the predictor what changed turns it into a simulator; V-JEPA 2 scaled the video model, froze it, trained an action-conditioned predictor on sixty-two hours of robot video and planned with it on a real arm; V-JEPA 2.1 made the features dense by grading every token. LeJEPA replaced the asymmetry with one regulariser derived from what a downstream probe needs, an isotropic Gaussian, and LeWorldModel and H-JEPA used it to train planners end to end from pixels, flat and then hierarchical, closing the loop on the 2022 diagram. Around that spine, several hundred papers a year now apply the recipe to audio, graphs, molecules, language and medicine, and argue about what a single deterministic latent predictor can and cannot represent.
