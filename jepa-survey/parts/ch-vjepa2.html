V-JEPA 2: scale, then act

V-JEPA 2 (Assran et al., 2506.09985v1, June 2025) is two papers in one. The first scales the V-JEPA recipe until a frozen encoder understands video well enough to anticipate what a person will do next. The second freezes that encoder, bolts on a small predictor that reads robot actions, trains it on under 62 hours of robot video, and plans with it on a real arm in a lab the model had never seen. The second part is where the predictor becomes a world model you can use.

Part A: four ways to scale

From 2 million to 22 million samples (over 1 million hours of video, including 1M images tiled into still clips): 84.2 to 85.2. A ViT-g encoder of 1B parameters instead of ViT-L: 86.7. Training for 252K steps instead of 90K, with a learning rate held constant until the probes stop improving: 87.5. Then the trick that makes the last step affordable: only the final 12K "cooldown" steps are run at 64 frames (16 seconds at 4 fps) and 384 pixels: 88.2. Training at that size throughout would have cost roughly 60 GPU-years; progressive resolution is 8.4x cheaper. The recipe underneath is V-JEPA's: tubelets of 2x16x16, 3D rotary positions, masks spanning all frames, an L1 loss on masked tokens, a fixed EMA momentum of 0.99925, and a predictor of 22M parameters that stays the same size for every encoder.

The frozen ViT-g at 384 pixels reads motion better than anything before it: 77.3 on Something-Something v2 and 90.2 on Diving-48 (Table 4). On Epic-Kitchens action anticipation (what will this person do 1 second from now, judged by recall at 5), the encoder plus its predictor scores 39.7 against 27.6 for the best prior model, a 44% relative gain (Table 5). Attached to a language model (Llama 3.1 8B, 88.5 million alignment samples) it averages 59.5 across seven video question-answering benchmarks (Table 8), though an image-text-trained encoder still wins two of them.

Claim card: intuitive physics emerges from video prediction (Garrido et al., 2502.11831v1; tests V-JEPA, 2024, not V-JEPA 2)

Claim. A video JEPA, with no training on physics, can be read as a "surprise" meter: run it over a clip, measure the error between its predicted and actual future embeddings, and physically impossible clips surprise it more. This is the violation-of-expectation protocol used on infants.

How it was measured. Pairs of videos that differ only in one physical violation (an object vanishing behind an occluder, passing through a wall, changing shape), from three benchmarks: IntPhys (3 properties, synthetic), GRASP (10 properties, synthetic) and InfLevel (real videos). The metric is how often the impossible video of a pair gets the higher surprise; chance is 50%. Baselines: a pixel-prediction model (VideoMAEv2), two multimodal LLMs, and 20 untrained networks.

Result. V-JEPA ViT-H: 98% on IntPhys, 66% on GRASP, 62% on InfLevel; the only method significantly above untrained networks on all three (their Fig. 1). On the IntPhys private test set its pairwise error on object permanence is 0.28% against 12.5% for human raters (Table S4). A model trained on only 128h of video keeps over 70%.

What would falsify it. The pixel-prediction baseline or the untrained networks matching V-JEPA on IntPhys, or surprise failing to separate the pairs once the context length is fixed rather than tuned per property (the paper checks this: a fixed context of 2 frames still works).

Part B: V-JEPA 2-AC, a predictor that reads actions

Now freeze the ViT-g and throw away its mask-filling predictor. In its place train a new predictor of 300M parameters (24 layers, width 1024) on the Droid dataset: 23k robot trajectories, successes and failures alike, no labels, no rewards, cut into 4 second clips of 16 frames. Each frame goes through the frozen encoder on its own and comes out as a 16x16x1408 feature map. Each time step also contributes the arm's end-effector state (a 7D vector: position, orientation, gripper) and the action, which is the change in that state to the next frame. The predictor's job: given everything up to step k, predict the encoder's feature map of frame k+1:

Eqs. 2 and 3 in V-JEPA 2, Sec. 3.1; summed as the objective, minimised over the predictor's weights \phi only. Teacher forcing feeds the true feature maps as inputs with T = 15; the rollout term feeds the predictor its own output, with T = 2 so the gradient crosses one recurrent step. There is no EMA teacher here: the target is the same frozen encoder.

  • E: the frozen V-JEPA 2 encoder, brown. It never changes in this stage.
  • a_t, s_t: action and end-effector state at step t; each becomes one token through its own small linear map.
  • P_\phi: the action-conditioned predictor; attention is block-causal, so step k's tokens see steps up to k only.

Planning: search the actions, not the pixels

To act, the robot is given a goal image. Encode the current frame and the goal with the frozen encoder; then search for the action whose predicted next feature map is closest, in L1, to the goal's feature map. The search is the Cross-Entropy Method: sample 800 candidate actions from a Gaussian, keep the top 10, refit the Gaussian, repeat 10 times, execute the mean, then look again. The horizon in the robot experiments is 1 step (longer "worked reasonably well" but costs time); each decision takes 16 sec on one RTX 4090.

In two labs the model had never seen, with 10 trials per task (Table 2): reaching, 100%; grasping a cup, 65%; grasping a box, 25%; pick-and-place of the cup, 80%, of the box, 65%. Pick-and-place is given two sub-goal images on the way. Octo, a behaviour-cloning policy pretrained on 1M trajectories, grasps the cup 15% of the time in the same setting; a planner built on Cosmos, a pixel-generating video model, takes 4 min per decision and grasps the cup 0% of the time (Table 3). Zero-shot here means no data from these labs; the arm, a Franka, is the one in Droid. The limits are honest too: the inferred actions rotate with the camera angle (about 1.6 cm of error on 5 cm moves), and the goal must be given as an image.
