V-JEPA: adding time

A video is an image with a third axis. V-JEPA (Bardes et al., 2404.08471v1, first posted 2024-02-15) keeps the I-JEPA recipe and changes only what gets masked: the hidden blocks become tubes that run through every frame. The reason is the trap from the last chapter. If a block were hidden in one frame only, the cheapest prediction copies it from the next frame; a static background would be the perfect slow feature. A tube removes that shortcut, so the predictor has to model what moves.

What changes, and what does not

The clip is 16 frames sampled with a stride of 4 (so about 3 seconds of raw footage, by the paper's Sec. 3.4), resized to 224 pixels. A 3D convolution turns every 2 x 16 x 16 block of pixels into one token, which gives 1568 tokens with 3D sin-cos positions. From here the picture is I-JEPA's: the context encoder sees the visible tokens, the target encoder (EMA, momentum 0.998 to 1.0) sees the whole clip, and a predictor of depth 12 and width 384 fills the holes with mask tokens that carry the hidden positions. Two things differ. Each clip gets 2 masks, one short-range and one long-range, and the context encoder and predictor run once per mask while the target encoder runs once. And the distance is an L1, "which we found to be more stable":

Eq. (1) in V-JEPA, Sec. 3.1; \mathrm{sg} is the stop-gradient, \overline{E}_\theta the EMA target encoder, \Delta_y the positions of the masked tokens (the only thing the predictor is told; the next chapter adds more).

The masked fraction is high: the paper reports "an average masking ratio of ~90%". Its Table 4 is the argument for tubes. On a ViT-L/16, the multi-block tube mask gives 72.9 on Kinetics-400 and 67.4 on Something-Something v2 (frozen probe, single view); random tubes at the same ratio give 51.5 and 46.4; a causal mask that lets the context see only the first 6 of the 16 frames (the other ten are hidden) gives 61.3 and 49.8. And the page's recurring question, Table 1: the same model trained to predict pixels instead of features drops from 73.7 to 68.6 on Kinetics-400 (frozen), while the gap vanishes once the whole network is fine-tuned (85.6 vs 85.4). Feature prediction makes frozen features better; fine-tuning can repair either.

Reading frozen features: the attentive probe

One evaluation detail is worth knowing because every later paper uses it. Averaging 1568 token vectors into one and training a linear classifier throws away where things are; V-JEPA instead trains a one-layer attentive probe, a learned query that attends over the tokens, then a classifier. On Kinetics-400 that is +17.3 points over average pooling (56.7 to 73.7, Table 3). The frozen encoder knew; the readout had been losing it.

What it bought

Trained on about 2 million videos (VideoMix2M: Kinetics, Something-Something v2 and HowTo100M) for 90K steps (batch 3072 at 224 pixels, 2400 at 384), the frozen ViT-H/16 at 384 pixels reaches 81.9 on Kinetics-400, 72.2 on Something-Something v2 and 77.4 on ImageNet (Table 6; the abstract's 77.9 uses a two-layer probe). The telling number is Something-Something, a dataset of hand motions where the direction of movement is the label: image models such as DINOv2 ViT-g score 50.6 there against 83.4 on Kinetics, where appearance is enough. V-JEPA's lead on motion is about 21 points; its deficit on still images against DINOv2 (86.2) is the price of not having been trained on them, which V-JEPA 2.1 later pays off.
