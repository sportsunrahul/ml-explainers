The energy view (LeCun, 2022)

Before I-JEPA existed, the recipe was written down as a diagram in a A Path Towards Autonomous Machine Intelligence (Version 0.9.2, 2022-06-27), and taught in a set of lectures that became an arXiv tutorial (Dawid and LeCun, 2306.02572v1), which is the source this chapter quotes. The idea rests on one change of viewpoint: stop thinking of a model as a function that outputs an answer, and think of it as a function that scores how well an answer fits.

Energy: a score, not an answer

An energy-based model assigns a number F(x, y) to every pair of an input x and a candidate y. Low means "these go together"; high means "they do not". To answer a question, you do not run the network forward once: you search for the y with the lowest energy. The tutorial puts it in one line (Sec. 3):

\htmlData{step=1, link=surface}{F(x, y)} \;\text{low for compatible pairs},\qquad \htmlData{step=2, link=marker}{\check{y} = \arg\min_y F(x, y)}

Sec. 3 of the tutorial (Dawid and LeCun, 2023), the highlighted definition; the energy is used for inference only, not as the training loss.

  • x: what you are given (the context, the visible patches, the past frames).
  • y: a candidate for what you are asked about (the hidden block, the next frame).
  • F: the scalar score; the surface in the figure. Inference walks downhill on it.

Why bother? Because a function can only return one answer, but many questions have several good answers: the hidden block could contain any of a hundred arrangements of leaves. An energy surface can have several valleys. The tutorial's second move handles the leftover ambiguity with a latent variable z: a small extra input that "captures the information in y that is not readily available in x" (Sec. 3.2). The energy of a pair is then the best energy over all choices of z:

\htmlData{step=1, link=surface}{F(x, y)} = \htmlData{step=2, link=valley}{\min_{z} E(x, y, z)}

Sec. 3.2 of the tutorial (written there with argmin; the minimised form, as in its Fig. 4). The tutorial warns that the capacity of z must be limited, or the model can hide the whole answer inside it.

Collapse, and the two ways out

Here is the trap. If the energy is computed by comparing two embeddings, F(x, y) = D(s_x, s_y), nothing stops both encoders from outputting the same constant: every pair then has zero energy, the surface is flat (press the button above), and the model has learned nothing. The tutorial's Fig. 5 contrasts this "joint-embedding architecture (can collapse!)" with plain regression, which cannot collapse because it must output actual pixels. The price of predicting in embedding space is that you must pay for the shape of the landscape.

Two families pay for it (Sec. 4 of the tutorial). Contrastive methods pick specific wrong answers and push their energy up, as in the hinge loss of Eq. (3); negative log-likelihood turns out to be a contrastive method too, pushing up every other answer at once (Eq. (4)). The cost: in a high-dimensional space there are too many wrong answers to push. Regularised methods instead limit the total volume of low-energy space, by architecture (a bottleneck, a small code) or by a term in the loss that measures that volume. VICReg's variance term is the tutorial's worked example. Hold on to this split: every anti-collapse trick on this page, from the EMA teacher to LeJEPA's single regulariser, is one of these two.

JEPA, as first drawn

Now put the pieces together. Two encoders turn x and y into embeddings; a predictor, given the embedding of x and a latent z, guesses the embedding of y; the energy is the distance between the guess and the real thing. That is the Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture of the tutorial's Fig. 9:

\htmlData{step=1, link=surface}{F(x, y, z)} = D\big(\htmlData{step=3, link=marker}{\htmlClass{t-green}{\tilde{s}_x}},\ \htmlData{step=2, link=valley}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{s_y}}\big), \qquad \htmlClass{t-green}{\tilde{s}_x} = \mathrm{Pred}(\htmlClass{t-blue}{s_x}, z),\quad \htmlClass{t-blue}{s_x} = \mathrm{Enc}_x(x),\quad \htmlClass{t-yellow}{s_y} = \mathrm{Enc}_y(y)

Labels of Fig. 9 in the tutorial (the energy is not a numbered equation there). The two encoders may differ and share no weights; x and y may even be different modalities.

  • s_x: the context embedding; blue throughout this page.
  • s_y: the target embedding, produced by the other encoder; yellow.
  • \tilde{s}_x: the predictor's guess of s_y; green.
  • D: a distance; the loss, red, lives here.

Three things follow from the picture, and each becomes a paper. First, the target encoder may discard: if some detail of y is unpredictable from x, the cheapest way to lower the energy is for \mathrm{Enc}_y to stop representing it. That is a feature when the detail is noise (the leaves) and a bug when the encoder discards everything (collapse). The training loss drawn in the tutorial's Fig. 10 therefore has four terms: the prediction error, a cost on the information in z, and two terms that keep s_x and s_y informative about their inputs. Second, with x = the past and y = the future, the predictor is a world model (Act IV). Third, JEPAs can be stacked: a hierarchical JEPA (Fig. 11) runs a second JEPA on top of the first's embeddings, predicting further ahead with fewer details, "since which information is important may be scale-dependent". That diagram waited four years for a working version (chapter 12).
