Act IIICleaning up the recipe

LeJEPA: one regulariser instead of the heuristics

Everything so far avoided collapse with an asymmetry: a slow copy of the encoder, a stop-gradient, a predictor on one side only. It works, but nobody can say what embedding distribution it is aiming at, and every new setup needs its schedules re-tuned. LeJEPA (Balestriero and LeCun, 2025-11-11) starts from the other end. First ask: what should the embeddings look like for any downstream task? Then write a single term that pushes them there, and drop the rest.

What the embeddings should look like

The argument runs through the probe. After pretraining, someone will fit a linear classifier, or a nearest-neighbour rule, or a kernel, on the frozen embeddings, for a task nobody knows yet. Section 3 of the paper asks which embedding distribution makes that probe's error smallest in the worst case over tasks, holding the total variance fixed. Two lemmas show anisotropy (some directions stretched, some squashed) can only increase a linear probe's bias and does increase its variance; Theorem 1 (with Theorems 7 and 8 in the appendix) shows that for nearest-neighbour and kernel probes, among all distributions with that variance, the isotropic Gaussian is the unique minimiser of the integrated squared bias. The reason, in one phrase from our reading: at fixed covariance, the Gaussian has the least Fisher information, the least "structure" for a probe to be misled by.

SIGReg: a statistical test as a loss

So the target is known: embeddings should be distributed as \mathcal{N}(0, I). Testing that in a thousand dimensions is hopeless, but a classical trick makes it cheap. A distribution is Gaussian if and only if its projection onto every direction is Gaussian (Cramer-Wold), so sample a few random unit directions, project the batch onto each, and run a one-dimensional test. The test is Epps-Pulley: compare the empirical characteristic function of the projections with the Gaussian's, under a Gaussian weight:

\htmlData{step=1, link=dir}{\mathrm{SIGReg}_T(\mathcal{A}, \{f_\theta(x_n)\}) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}} T\big(\{a^\top f_\theta(x_n)\}_{n=1}^N\big)}, \qquad \htmlData{step=2, link=hist}{T = \mathrm{EP} = N\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\big|\hat\varphi_X(t) - e^{-t^2/2}\big|^2 w(t)\,dt}

Definition 2 (tagged SIGReg) and the Epps-Pulley equation in Sec. 4.2.3 of LeJEPA (tagged, not numbered). \hat\varphi_X(t) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_n e^{itX_n} is the empirical characteristic function of the projections; w(t) a Gaussian weight; the integral is a trapezoid sum over 17 points.

  • \mathcal{A}: the set of random unit directions, 1024 by default, drawn fresh at every step; the paper argues as few as 16 work because resampling covers the sphere over time.
  • T: any one-dimensional goodness-of-fit statistic; Epps-Pulley is chosen because its gradient is bounded by 4\sigma^2/N per sample for any input (Theorem 4), so it cannot blow up training.
  • Cost: linear in the batch size; no pairwise distances, no prototypes, no queue.

The whole method is then two terms. Views of an image (DINO-style: 2 global crops and several local ones) go through one encoder; every view's embedding is pushed toward the mean of the global views' embeddings (the prediction term), and SIGReg is applied to each view's batch of embeddings:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LeJEPA}} = \htmlData{step=1, link=hist}{\frac{\lambda}{V}\sum_{v=1}^{V}\mathrm{SIGReg}\big(\{z_{n,v}\}_{n=1}^{B}\big)} + \htmlData{step=2, link=cloud}{\frac{1-\lambda}{B}\sum_{n=1}^{B}\frac{1}{V}\sum_{v'=1}^{V}\big\lVert \mu_n - z_{n,v'}\big\rVert_2^2}

Equation tagged (LeJEPA) in Sec. 5.1, with the prediction term in its Eq. (7) form; \mu_n is the mean of sample n's global-view embeddings, V the number of views, B the batch. The single hyperparameter \lambda defaults to 0.05.

Note what is not there. No target encoder, no EMA, no stop-gradient: the gradient flows into the encoder from both sides of the difference. No predictor network: the paper argues it is "only justified when there exists an asymmetry". No prototypes, no register tokens, no whitening, no negatives. What stays: a projector head on the encoder, multi-crop views, and an ordinary learning-rate warm-up. The reference implementation is about 50 lines.

What it bought

The headline is stability rather than a record. The abstract reports 79% ImageNet-1k linear accuracy for a ViT-H/14; the tables give 77.1% for a ViT-L and 78.5% for a ConvNeXt-V2 Huge after 100 epochs (Sec. 6.4), and a ViT-L with 100 epochs transfers slightly better than I-JEPA's ViT-H with 300 (79.48 vs 78.50 average over 8 datasets, Table 2). The same recipe trains over 60+ architectures without per-model tuning, up to a 1.8B ViT-g with a smooth loss curve, and removing the predictor and teacher does not collapse it (Table 4). Two results matter more for practice. The training loss predicts downstream accuracy (Spearman about 85%), so you can select models without labels. And pretraining in-domain on small datasets beats transferring a frontier model: on Galaxy10 (11,000 images) a LeJEPA ResNet-34 reaches 78.17% frozen against 71.38% for DINOv3 (appendix Table 3); though on CIFAR-100 the transferred I-JEPA still wins, 86.93 to 70.44 (Table 5).

Two companions

Claim card: the anti-collapse term estimates the data density (Balestriero et al., 2025-10-07)

Claim. If a JEPA's embeddings are isotropic Gaussian, then by the change-of-variables formula the input density is, up to a constant, the product of the encoder's Jacobian singular values: \log p(x) \propto \sum_i \log \sigma_i(J_f(x)), the "JEPA-SCORE" (their Eq. (5)). A trained JEPA can be read as a density estimator for free.

How it was measured. On synthetic data with a known density, the Pearson correlation between the score and the true log density across dimensions 64, 128, 256, 512; on real data, qualitatively, by ranking ImageNet images and classes by the score under four public encoders.

Result. Correlations from 0.64 to 0.94 on the synthetic tests; low-score images are atypical. Falsified by: a correlation near zero on data with a known density, or by scores that do not change with the dataset.

Claim card: when does LeJEPA learn a world model? (Klindt, LeCun, Balestriero, 2026-05-25)

Claim. If the world's latents evolve under a stationary additive-noise process and the observations are a nonlinear function of them, LeJEPA's two terms recover the latents up to a linear map (linear identifiability), and the Gaussian is the unique latent distribution for which this holds. The optimum of the prediction term is a linear map because every nonlinear component loses correlation across a time step.

How it was measured. Synthetic worlds with known latents up to dimension 1024, measuring the R^2 of a linear regression from embeddings to true latents; a Reacher control environment; the main theorems checked in Lean with zero unproven steps.

Result. R^2 above 0.999 for SIGReg and VICReg-style regularisers in the synthetic worlds and 0.720241 for a contrastive one at 1024 dimensions. Falsified by: low R^2 under the stated assumptions, or linear recovery under a non-Gaussian latent prior.
