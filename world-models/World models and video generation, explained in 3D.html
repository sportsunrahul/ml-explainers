Simulating the world, one frame at a time

A world model predicts what happens next, given what it has seen and what an agent does. Video generators predict what the world looks like over time. The two ideas are merging into models that can be played like games, used to train robots, and queried like simulators.

1

Two traditions, converging

In reinforcement learning, a world model is a learned model of the environment's dynamics: given the current state and an action, predict the next state and reward. An agent that has one can plan or practise “in its imagination” instead of acting in the costly real world.

In generative modelling, video models learn to produce realistic clips from text or images. To do that well they must implicitly learn a lot about the world: objects persist, things fall, light reflects. Condition such a model on actions and it becomes a world model. This is the bet behind interactive systems such as Genie and GameNGen, and behind “world foundation models” for robotics.

2

World models in reinforcement learning

Ha and Schmidhuber's World Models (2018) compressed game frames with a VAE, predicted the next latent with a recurrent network, and trained a tiny controller entirely inside the model's “dream”, then transferred it to the real game. PlaNet (2019) and the Dreamer series learned latent dynamics from pixels and optimised behaviour by imagining rollouts; DreamerV3 (2023) used one set of hyperparameters across many domains and was the first to collect diamonds in Minecraft from scratch. MuZero (2019) learned a model that predicts only what matters for planning (value, policy and reward) rather than observations, and matched AlphaZero at Go, chess and shogi without knowing the rules.

The central difficulty is compounding error. A model that is slightly wrong per step becomes very wrong after many steps, and a planner will happily exploit those errors, choosing actions that only look good inside the model.

3

How video generators work

Modern video generators are diffusion or flow models (see the diffusion guide) extended in time. Video Diffusion Models (Ho et al., 2022) extended the image U-Net with factorised attention: spatial attention within each frame, then temporal attention across frames at each location. Imagen Video (2022) chained a base model with spatial and temporal super-resolution stages; Make-A-Video (2022) started from a text-to-image model and learned motion from unlabelled video. Latent video diffusion (Align your Latents, 2023; Stable Video Diffusion, 2023) moved the process into an autoencoder's latent space.

Current systems follow the recipe popularised by Sora (OpenAI, 2024). A 3D causal VAE compresses video in space and time (commonly 8× per side and 4× in time). The latent is cut into spacetime patches, each a small block of height × width × time, which become tokens for a diffusion transformer trained with diffusion or flow matching, conditioned on text through a large text encoder. Movie Gen (Meta, 2024), HunyuanVideo (2024), CogVideoX (2024), Wan (2025) and Google's Veo models are variants of this design; Veo 3 (2025) also generates synchronised audio.

4

Temporal consistency

The hardest part of video is keeping things the same over time: an object's shape, identity and position must evolve smoothly, and something that leaves the frame should come back unchanged. Generating frames independently fails immediately. Generating all frames jointly with attention across time works for short clips. Generating long videos piece by piece (autoregressively) lets errors accumulate and drift, so long-video systems add memory, re-anchoring to earlier frames, and noise-augmented training to make the model robust to its own mistakes.

5

Interactive world models

Add actions to a video model and you can play it. Genie (DeepMind, 2024), an 11B-parameter model trained on unlabelled videos of 2D platform games, learned a small set of latent actions on its own by asking which discrete code best explains the change between consecutive frames. Users could then “control” any image, even a sketch. GameNGen (2024) ran a diffusion model conditioned on past frames and key presses to simulate the game DOOM in real time at about 20 frames per second. DIAMOND (2024) trained RL agents inside a diffusion world model of Atari games. Genie 2 (2024) extended this to 3D worlds, and Genie 3 (2025) generates explorable environments in real time at 720p for several minutes, with memory of what was seen earlier.

The loop is the same in all of them: encode recent frames into tokens, feed them with the action to a dynamics model, generate the next frame's tokens, decode, repeat.

6

World models for robotics

Robot data is scarce and expensive; video of the world is abundant. World models offer three uses. As simulators: UniSim (2023) learned an action-conditioned video simulator from diverse datasets to train policies; NVIDIA's Cosmos (2025) released open world foundation models and video tokenizers meant to be fine-tuned into simulators for robots and vehicles. As planners: UniPi (2023) generated a video of the task being done and then inferred the actions to follow it; Meta's V-JEPA 2 (2025) learned video representations by prediction, then an action-conditioned version planned robot-arm motions toward image goals. As data generators: synthetic rollouts augment real demonstrations.

A parallel line builds vision-language-action (VLA) models that map images and instructions straight to actions: RT-2 (2023) fine-tuned a VLM to output actions as tokens, OpenVLA (2024) is an open 7B version, and π0 (2024) adds a flow-matching action head for smooth, high-frequency control. VLAs are policies rather than world models, but they share the same pretrained vision and language backbones, and hybrid systems use a world model to check or improve a VLA's plans.

7

Predict pixels, or predict meaning?

There is a live debate about what a world model should predict. Generative models predict pixels (or latents that decode to pixels). That is directly checkable and lets people watch the model's imagination, but spends capacity on details that don't matter for decisions, like the exact texture of leaves. Predictive-representation models, championed by Yann LeCun as JEPA (joint-embedding predictive architecture), predict only abstract features of the future, ignoring unpredictable detail. They are cheaper and may generalise better for planning, but are harder to inspect and evaluate. MuZero sits at the extreme of predicting only what matters for reward. Which approach wins, or how they combine, is open.

Strengths, limits and open problems

What they can do

  • Generate short, photorealistic, controllable video from text or images.
  • Simulate game-like environments interactively in real time.
  • Let agents practise and plan without costly real-world trials.
  • Turn the internet's video into a training signal for physical understanding.

What is still hard

  • Physics: objects merge, liquids misbehave and counts change; realism is not correctness.
  • Long horizons: memory, object permanence and compounding error over minutes or hours.
  • Control: precise, reliable action-conditioning and causal effects of actions.
  • Cost: training and generation are extremely compute-hungry.
  • Evaluation: metrics like FVD and VBench capture look, not whether the world behaves correctly.
  • Misuse: convincing fake video raises provenance and consent concerns.
