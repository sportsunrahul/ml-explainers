Show, do not tell: visual prompts

When a part has no good name, box an example

Prompt

The best name you have

The tray holds three copies of an odd part that has no common name. The closest word is bracket. A text encoder trained on web captions knows what "bracket" means on the web: the angle bracket. So the prompt claims the angle bracket and misses all three odd parts.

The stage is a toy: each part has a made-up embedding, and a score above one half claims the box. Click any part to look for it instead; its everyday name becomes the prompt. The washer, the nut and the bolt have good names, and the names work.

Draw a box around one example

A is a box on one example. The model turns the image inside the box into a prompt vector, in the same space as the name vectors, and scores every other box against it. The odd part that looks like the example is found. The dark one, turned on its side, is missed: one example shows one look.

Averaging the name vector and the box vector, as T-Rex2 does, finds the third part and lifts the angle bracket a little. Press Both, averaged, then click a washer and try all three: for a part with a good name, the name alone is best. The stage is still the toy.

Not a free win

T-Rex2 reports box prompts beating names on rare classes. Its boxes are not one example, though: it averages 16 labelled boxes per class from the benchmark's training set, so it is an setting. And the win depends on the split: on the smaller minival split of , the names win.

A box helps when the name fails, but only with a good box encoder and enough examples to cover how the part varies. Test the model you will deploy, on your own parts.
