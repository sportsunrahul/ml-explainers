OWLv2: label the web with yourself

Where text enters: the same place as in OWL-ViT, the final score. OWLv2 changes the training data and the training cost, not the graph; at inference it is the same model (Sec. 3.2).

OWL-ViT trained on about 2 million images with human boxes, and chapter 5 ended on the suspicion that data was its limit. The web has billions of captioned images and no boxes, so OWLv2 lets OWL-ViT draw the boxes and trains a new detector on them.

Three ways to make billions of images affordable

Self-training only pays if the student sees far more images than any human-labelled set holds. OWLv2 makes each training example cheaper in three ways (Sec. 3.2):

  • Drop flat patches. A ViT's tokens form an unordered set, so some can be left out. OWLv2 drops the 50% of patches with the lowest pixel variance: sky, plain walls and padding. It does this only in training.
  • Grade only likely objects. A new objectness head scores each token, and boxes, class scores and losses are computed only for the top tokens, about 10%. For L/14 at 1008 pixels the image has 5184 tokens and the top 512 are kept (Table A2). At inference all tokens are used.
  • Pack many images into one. Mosaics with grids up to 6x6 put 13.2 web images into one training example on average.

Together these cut training FLOPs by about 50% and raise throughput 2x: for L/14 at 840 pixels, 11945.4 GFLOPs per example for OWL-ViT against 5357.9 for OWLv2. The paper says that at inference "the model is identical to the original OWL-ViT".

The chart is the evidence for dropping half. Rare-class AP is 31.8 with no dropping and 30.8 with half the patches gone, but the no-drop runs alone vary with a standard deviation of 1.16. At 70% the loss is real: AP falls from 33.3 to 30.4 and rare-class AP to 28.2 (LVIS val). Remember the last button: a deployed OWLv2 drops nothing.

Self-training alone beats human boxes on rare classes

All numbers here are LVIS val with standard AP (OWLv2 Table 1). No human ever drew a box for a rare class at any stage, for the teacher or the student. Self-training alone lifts the B/16 model from 20.6 to 29.6 rare-class AP, 9 points above OWL-ViT. The self-trained models even do better on rare classes than on all classes (29.6 against 27.0 for B/16); the paper guesses that web text about an unusual object tends to be about that object. The self-trained G/14 reaches 37.5, above the best earlier open-vocabulary result in its table, F-VLM at 32.8.

Fine-tune, or not

An optional last step fine-tunes the student on LVIS base, the LVIS boxes minus the rare classes, for 1.1M images, about 100 LVIS epochs (Sec. 4.1). Rare-class AP on LVIS jumps. The paper also tested every model on ODinW, a set of 35 small datasets from other domains (drones, aquariums, potholes, hard hats). Its main table, like most papers, reports only a 13-dataset subset.

Guess first. The chart starts with the self-trained models. Fine-tuning on LVIS base raises LVIS rare-class AP at every size. Before you press + Fine-tune on LVIS, guess what happens to the median AP over the 35 ODinW datasets.

LVIS rare-class AP rises at every size: B/16 29.6 to 36.2, L/14 34.9 to 44.6, G/14 37.5 to 47.2. The median zero-shot AP over the 35 ODinW datasets falls at every size: 11.6 to 6.0, 16.2 to 6.3, 17.5 to 9.5 (Table A4). The usual 13-dataset mean hides most of this: it goes from 53.0 to 50.1 for L/14, and for G/14 it even rises, from 49.9 to 50.1. The paper's own verdict: LVIS rare-class AP "only measures a narrow concept of open-vocabulary performance" (Sec. 4.6).

Why does the median tell a different story from the mean? For the self-trained L/14, the mean over the 13 usual datasets is 53.0, the mean over all 35 is 24.4, and the median is 16.2. A few easy datasets carry the mean, and the 13 favour them. Chapter 12 plots all 35 datasets one by one.

The paper's remedy is weight averaging: average the weights of the self-trained and the fine-tuned checkpoints, with weight 0.4 on the fine-tuned one. It reports this for one model only, the L/14 trained with N-grams plus a curated list of 2520 class names (Table 1 row 21). On the 13-dataset mean the average scores 56.3, the best in the paper, against 48.7 for that model fine-tuned. On the median over 35 it scores 10.6: better than the fine-tuned 7.0, still below the self-trained L/14's 16.2. No table gives its LVIS AP. So the average recovers part of the loss, not all of it.

Two caveats. WebLI was deduplicated against common benchmarks but not against ODinW (Sec. 4.1), so web images close to ODinW's could have reached the student (our reading of what that allows). And the paper names its own limits (Sec. 5): self-training needs a great deal of compute and data, and a fine-tuned model's scores for out-of-distribution queries "may be poorly calibrated and may depend on the precise wording of the query". Chapters 12 and 13 take both points to factory data.
