How they fail on rare industrial objects

Chapter 12 measured how far a prompt falls short on unfamiliar data. This chapter asks why. The papers point to seven separate causes, and each has a different fix, so it pays to know which one you are hitting.

Guess first. You type porosity, the welder's word for small gas holes in a weld. Where will its text vector land: near the image features of weld pores, or somewhere else? Press Type the jargon name and see.

1. The name is jargon, an acronym or a word with another meaning

A text encoder learned each word from web captions. "Porosity" in a caption usually means rock or skin, not a weld. RF100-VL chose its datasets to make exactly this hard: it lists context-dependent names such as "Thunderbolt", a defect on a wire rope, scientific acronyms, and catch-all classes such as "Soft Plastic" (RF100-VL Fig. 2). The cheapest fix is to describe the look in common words, and GLIP measured it.

On the Mushrooms dataset the original labels were the species names Cow. Chanterelle, which scored 13.6 AP. The prompt flat mushroom. yellow mushroom scored 73.8. hand of a person instead of hand took EgoHands from 22.1 to 50.0. Rewriting does not always help: Pothole stayed at 17.8 to 17.7. When the labels are not words at all, a prompt has nothing to work with: MM-Grounding-DINO scores 0.4 AP zero-shot on a brain-tumour dataset whose classes are numeric codes, and 47.5 after fine-tuning. Grounding DINO's own worst ODinW case is PlantDoc, with names such as "Tomato leaf mosaic virus": 0.36 AP for the T model trained with Cap4M.

2. Your label policy is not the web's meaning

A class name is short; an annotation guide is long. The Foundational Few-Shot paper shows the gap on nuImages, a driving dataset with 18 classes: its guide boxes only the cab of a truck, and counts a rider as part of a bicycle (2312.14494v4, Fig. 1). A model that learned "truck" from the web boxes the whole vehicle and is scored as wrong. Every inspection line has rules like this: where a scratch ends, whether two touching pores are one defect, which side of a part counts. No prompt carries them; a few labelled examples do.

3. A defect is often a texture, not an object

Every model on this page learned to box things with edges: people, cars, pallets. Its proposals, queries or objectness scores were trained on such boxes. A stain, a corrosion patch or a faint surface scratch has no clear boundary and often fills the whole view. our reading: expect such targets to be missed or boxed arbitrarily, and treat them as a segmentation or anomaly-detection problem rather than a prompt problem. The sources measure this only indirectly; ViLD's most common failure on rare LVIS classes is a missed object, not a wrong name (ViLD App. A).

4. Small defects vanish when the image is resized

These models do not see your camera's full resolution. OWL-ViT B/32 resizes to 768 pixels and reads 32 pixel patches; OWLv2 L/14 uses 1008 pixels and 14 pixel patches; GLIP-T tests at 800 by 1333 with a finest stride of 8 in its official config; YOLO-World's released models take 640 pixels (official code).

A twenty-pixel defect in a 4,096-pixel frame becomes a few pixels at the model's input, a small fraction of one patch. The token that should report it is mostly background. The fix is not a better prompt: crop or tile the image so the defect stays large, or fine-tune at a higher resolution.

5. The score does not tell you when to stop

An inspection system needs a threshold: above it, reject the part. Open-vocabulary scores make that hard. With no threshold, a detector always returns its best boxes, so it fires on every image, including parts with no defect. Described Object Detection measured what a threshold buys for Grounding DINO.

Without a threshold, false alarms on clean images are at 100.0%. At 0.5 they fall to 60.6% while mAP drops from 20.7 to 18.4; at 0.9 they reach 8.5%, and mAP collapses to 3.8. No threshold gives both. The papers add two warnings. CLIP's score for a crop does not reflect how well the box fits (ViLD App. B). Fine-tuned models' scores on unfamiliar queries "may be poorly calibrated and may depend on the precise wording of the query" (OWLv2 Sec. 5). our reading: a threshold tuned for one prompt will not transfer to the next one, so set it per class, on your own labelled images.

6. Attributes and "not" are mostly ignored

Claim
Open-vocabulary detectors find the noun and ignore the modifiers, and ordinary AP hides it.

For inspection this matters directly. scratched panel, panel without scratches and panel will tend to light up the same boxes. Ask for the defect itself, not for the part in a state.

7. Fine-tuning fixes your classes and costs the rest

Chapter 12's last card is the seventh cause. Once you fine-tune on your defects, the model's open-vocabulary behaviour on other domains drops: OWL-ST L/14's median over 35 ODinW datasets fell from 16.2 to 6.3. If you need both, keep the original weights next to the fine-tuned ones.

What to do on a new inspection task

our reading, combining the seven causes:

  1. Label a test set first: a few hundred images including clean parts, so false alarms are counted.
  2. Try the prompt as a plain description of the look, one class per prompt and one at a time, and measure.
  3. Crop or tile so the smallest defect covers at least a patch at the model's input.
  4. If the prompt falls short, label about ten boxes per class and fine-tune an open-vocabulary detector; it was the best few-shot learner in RF100-VL.
  5. Set thresholds per class on your clean and defective images, never once for all prompts.
