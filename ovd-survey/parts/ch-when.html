When can a prompt replace a custom detector?

Every model on this page can be run on your images today with nothing but a list of names. The question is how close that gets you to a detector trained on your own labels. Four independent measurements answer it, and they agree.

Guess first. On industrial images, how many labelled boxes per class does a fine-tuned detector need before it beats the best prompt-only model? A hundred? A thousand? Pick a number, then look at the second column of the chart.

Read the Industrial column from left to right. The best prompt-only model, Grounding DINO with every class name in one prompt, averages 10.3 AP. The same model fine-tuned on 10 boxes per class reaches 37.8. Even YOLOv8n, a small detector with no text encoder at all, trained on those same ten boxes, scores 28.1, which beats every model that only reads a prompt. The last two columns come from closed-set detectors (YOLOv8, YOLOv11 and LW-DETR), because RF100-VL did not run Grounding DINO with more labels. With 10% of the training images labelled they reach 50.7 to 52.9, and with all labels 64.3 to 68.2. So the answer to the guess is ten. Ten labelled boxes per class beat the prompt by a wide margin, and every further step up in labels still pays. Read the columns as label budgets, not as one model's learning curve: the units differ (ten boxes per class, a random tenth of the training images, all of them), and a single 10-shot run is noisy.

Card 1: on unfamiliar domains, ten labelled boxes beat any prompt

Claim
On concepts that are rare on the web, no prompt-only detector comes close to the same detector fine-tuned on a handful of boxes.
Measured
RF100-VL collects 100 Roboflow datasets chosen because their classes are uncommon in internet pretraining. They are grouped into 7 domains by their CLIP embeddings. Every method is scored with COCO-style AP on the same fully labelled test split; "10 boxes per class" is the paper's 10-shot split. Its "GroundingDINO" is MM-Grounding-DINO-L, prompted with all class names at once; OWLv2 gets one class per prompt.
Result
Across all 100 datasets: prompt only 15.7, 10-shot fine-tune 33.6, all labels 55.4 to 59.8. The same Grounding DINO scores 49.2 on ODinW-13 according to RF100-VL; the benchmark you pick decides how good "zero-shot" looks.
Would falsify
A prompt-only model within a few AP of the 10-shot fine-tune on these domains.

One caution about the word "Industrial". It is a cluster of 22 datasets that CLIP embeddings put together, not a curated factory benchmark. It holds printed circuit boards (deeppcb), surface defects (defect-detection, wheel-defect-detection, cable-damage), small parts (smd-components, screwdetectclassification), but also aircraft-turnaround-dataset, conveyor-t-shirts and water-meter (RF100-VL Appendix R). It is the closest public evidence to an inspection line, and it is still only a proxy for yours.

The average you usually see hides the failures

Most papers report ODinW-13, the mean over 13 small datasets. ODinW has 35. The OWLv2 paper prints every one of them, and the picture changes when you look at all of them.

Card 2: the median dataset is much harder than the reported mean

Claim
Strong zero-shot means on a familiar subset coexist with near-zero AP on many datasets.
Measured
Zero-shot AP on each of the 35 ODinW datasets, as printed by the papers themselves; no fine-tuning on any of them.
Result
OWL-ST L/14: mean over the usual 13 53.0, median over all 35 16.2. Grounding DINO L: median over 35 18.4, mean 26.1.
Would falsify
A median over 35 close to the mean over 13.

What a text encoder is worth in labels

GLIP measured the trade directly. It fine-tuned its own models and a closed-set detector of the same family on 1, 3, 5 and 10 examples per class and on all data, for each of the 13 ODinW datasets.

Card 3: a prompt is worth a few labels, not a dataset

Claim
Grounding pretraining buys the equivalent of a handful of labelled examples per class; labels still add a lot on top.
Measured
ODinW-13 average AP after full fine-tuning on k examples per class (3 random draws each), same evaluation for all models.
Result
GLIP-T with only a prompt: 46.5; DyHead-T with 5 examples per class: 46.4. GLIP-L with one example: 59.9, against DyHead-T on all data: 60.8. GLIP-T itself climbs to 64.9 with all data.
Would falsify
Fine-tuning GLIP adding little over its prompt-only score.

Fine-tuning has a price

Once you fine-tune, the model gets better at your classes and worse at everything else. OWLv2 measured it on LVIS and on the 35 ODinW datasets at once.

Card 4: fine-tuning on your classes erodes the open vocabulary

Claim
Fine-tuning raises accuracy on the fine-tuned label space and lowers it on unrelated domains.
Measured
OWL-ST models before and after fine-tuning on LVIS base classes; LVIS val rare-class AP (classes never fine-tuned on) and zero-shot ODinW over 35 datasets.
Result
CLIP L/14: LVIS APr 34.9 to 44.6, while the ODinW median over 35 falls from 16.2 to 6.3. Averaging the weights before and after fine-tuning (fine-tuned weight 0.4) gives the best ODinW-13 mean, 56.3, but its median over 35 is 10.6, still below the model that was never fine-tuned.
Would falsify
A fine-tuned model whose ODinW median holds steady.

LVIS rare classes improved even though no rare class was in the fine-tuning set. The paper's explanation: rare LVIS classes look like frequent ones ("heron" next to "bird"). Your defect classes probably do not look like anything in LVIS, so expect the ODinW behaviour, not the LVIS one.
