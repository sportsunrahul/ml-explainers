YOLO-World: fold the vocabulary into the weights

Where text enters YOLO-World: in the neck, where the prompts gate the features of every cell and a small attention lets the text look back at the image, and again at the score. For deployment the text encoder runs once, and its output becomes ordinary convolution weights.

Every earlier detector in this chart runs at a few frames per second or less, as YOLO-World's authors measured them (chart below). YOLO-World starts from a real-time detector, YOLOv8, and asks how little it must change to read a prompt. The figure walks through the answer in three steps.

What YOLO is, from scratch

The two-stage detector of chapter 3 first proposes boxes, then names each one. A one-stage detector skips the proposals. The network turns the image into grids of feature vectors, and every cell of every grid predicts a box and class scores at once. The name says it: YOLO is short for "You Only Look Once". YOLO-World builds on YOLOv8, and its paper names the parts that matter here:

  • Three feature maps. The backbone, a convolutional network called Darknet, produces maps C3, C4 and C5, each a coarser grid than the one before. For an input of 640x640 pixels (the size of the released models, official code; the paper states no input size) they are 80x80, 40x40 and 20x20 cells our reading, from the YOLOv8 convention of strides 8, 16 and 32.
  • A neck that mixes the maps. A path aggregation network (PAN) runs a top-down path, which passes coarse, high-level features down to the fine map, then a bottom-up path, which passes fine detail back up. Its outputs are P3, P4 and P5, on the same three grids.
  • Dense predictions. Every cell of P3, P4 and P5 makes one prediction: 6400 + 1600 + 400 = 8400 per image at that size our reading. Compare 900 queries in Grounding DINO.
  • A decoupled head. Each cell feeds two separate branches, each with two 3x3 convs (the paper says "two 3x3 convs"; two per branch is the YOLOv8 convention and our reading). One branch regresses the box as four distances from the cell to the box edges; the other gives the class output.
  • Which cells should fire. Thousands of cells and a handful of objects: training must decide which cells are positives. DETR answers with Hungarian matching, exactly one query per object (DETR family, matching). YOLOv8 uses task-aligned label assignment: for each object it ranks the cells by how good both their class score and their box overlap are, and the top few become positives; every other cell learns "no object". Several cells can claim one object, so duplicates are removed after prediction.
  • Box losses. An IoU loss penalizes low overlap with the true box. A distribution focal loss (DFL) lets the head predict each edge distance as a small probability distribution instead of one number, and pushes that distribution toward the true distance.
  • Mosaics. Training images arrive as mosaics: 4 images tiled into one, plus color changes, random affine warps and flips.

YOLO-World names task-aligned assignment, the IoU loss, DFL and the mosaic, but does not restate how they work. The one-line descriptions above, and the duplicate removal, come from the YOLOv8 line of work and are background, not claims of this paper.

Press 1 Plain YOLO. The green dots are the positives that assignment picks for each object; the selected cell on the forklift predicts a box (blue corners, with its four edge distances dashed). Its class branch has one score per class row, and no row is "forklift", so nothing fires. This is chapter 1's closed-set problem, now in a real-time detector.

Text in the neck

The first change is the one every chapter makes. The class branch becomes an embedding branch, and the class rows become text vectors. YOLO-World encodes the prompts with CLIP's text encoder, \htmlClass{t-yellow}{W} = \texttt{TextEncoder}(T) \in \mathbb{R}^{C\times D}: one D-dimensional row for each of the C nouns. The paper calls it CLIP-base (ViT-base) and keeps it frozen during pretraining. Its width D is not stated, so this page keeps it symbolic.

During training, each mosaic gets its own vocabulary: all the nouns of its 4 images, plus random negative nouns from the same dataset, at most 80 in all. Captions and referring expressions are cut into noun phrases first.

The second change is new. Inside the neck, the text gets a say in which image features survive. After the last bottleneck of each C2f block, YOLO-World multiplies every cell's feature vector by one number between 0 and 1: the sigmoid of that cell's best similarity to any prompt. The block is renamed a text-guided CSPLayer (T-CSPLayer), and the operation is a max-sigmoid gate:

\htmlData{step=4, link=features}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{X_l'} = \htmlClass{t-blue}{X_l}\cdot} \htmlData{step=3, link=gate}{\delta\Big(} \htmlData{step=2, link=max}{\max_{j\in\{1..C\}}} \htmlData{step=1, link=sims}{\big(\htmlClass{t-blue}{X_l}\htmlClass{t-yellow}{W_j}^{\top}\big)} \htmlData{step=3, link=gate}{\Big)^{\top}}

Eq. 2 in YOLO-World (2401.17270v3).

  • \htmlClass{t-blue}{X_l}: the image features at level l \in \{3,4,5\}, an H\times W\times D map: one D-vector per cell.
  • \htmlClass{t-blue}{X_l}\htmlClass{t-yellow}{W_j}^{\top}: every cell's dot product with prompt j, an H\times W map. The figure's bars show these numbers for the selected cell.
  • \max_j: per cell, keep only the best-matching prompt.
  • \delta: the sigmoid, which turns that score into a gate between 0 and 1.
  • \htmlClass{t-blue}{X_l'}: the gated features. A cell that matches any prompt keeps its features; a cell that matches none is damped. The gated map is then concatenated with the block's cross-stage features, as in a plain C2f block.

Press 2 Text in the neck. The blue fill is the gate: it is bright on the four objects and dim on the shelf and the floor. Then press Drop forklift. The forklift's cells lose their best match, their gate falls, and the neck damps them before the head ever sees them. This is the difference from late fusion (chapter 8): the image features now depend on the prompt.

The text also looks back at the image. Image-Pooling Attention (I-Pooling) max-pools each of the three maps to a 3x3 grid, which gives 27 image tokens, and lets the text vectors attend to them:

\htmlData{step=1, link=text}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{W'} = \htmlClass{t-yellow}{W}} + \htmlData{step=2, link=pooled}{\texttt{MultiHead-Attention}(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{W}, \htmlClass{t-blue}{\tilde{X}}, \htmlClass{t-blue}{\tilde{X}})}

Eq. 3 in YOLO-World (2401.17270v3).

  • \htmlClass{t-yellow}{W}: the text vectors, used as the attention queries.
  • \htmlClass{t-blue}{\tilde{X}}: the 27 pooled image tokens, a 27\times D matrix, used as keys and values. Pooling first keeps the attention tiny: 27 tokens instead of thousands of cells.
  • \htmlClass{t-yellow}{W'}: image-aware text vectors, added back as a residual. The head scores against these.

Last, the head scores each prediction against each prompt, much as in chapter 1, but with both vectors normalized and a learned scale and shift:

\htmlData{step=3, link=score}{\htmlClass{t-green}{s_{k,j}} = \alpha\cdot} \htmlData{step=1, link=cells}{\texttt{L2-Norm}(\htmlClass{t-blue}{e_k})} \cdot \htmlData{step=2, link=text}{\texttt{L2-Norm}(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{w_j})^{\top}} \htmlData{step=3, link=score}{+ \beta}

Eq. 1 in YOLO-World (2401.17270v3).

  • \htmlClass{t-blue}{e_k}: the object embedding of prediction k, from the embedding branch. There is one per cell.
  • \htmlClass{t-yellow}{w_j}: the vector of prompt j. With both vectors scaled to unit length, their product is a cosine.
  • \alpha, \beta: a learned scale and shift. The paper says the normalization and this affine step "are important for stabilizing the region-text training".
  • \htmlClass{t-green}{s_{k,j}}: the score of prediction k for prompt j, the tags on the figure's boxes.

The training loss adds the box losses to a region-text contrastive loss: \mathcal{L}(I) = \htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{con}}} + \lambda_I\cdot(\htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{iou}}} + \htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{dfl}}}). The contrastive term is a cross entropy between the scores and the assignment, which gives each positive cell the index of its noun. \lambda_I is 1 for detection and grounding images and 0 for image-text data, whose boxes are noisy. That image-text data is 246k CC3M images that GLIP-L labelled automatically, a teacher-student loop like OWLv2's (chapter 6).

Both neck paths pay off, a little. In the paper's ablation (YOLO-World-L pretrained on Objects365 alone, zero-shot LVIS minival AP), adding both paths raises AP from 22.4 to 23.5; with GQA grounding data added, from 29.7 to 31.9 (Table 4). Freezing the text encoder matters more, on either metric: on AP the neck paths add 1.1 while fine-tuning the encoder costs 3.1, and on rare classes the neck adds 1.7 while fine-tuning costs 5.9. In the same setting without the neck paths, fine-tuning CLIP's text encoder drops rare-class AP from 14.5 to 8.6 (Table 5): training on 365 class names erodes what CLIP knows about the rest. Chapter 12 finds the same trade in OWLv2. our reading: these ablations use a shorter setting than the main results, so their numbers do not compare with the chart below.

Fold the vocabulary into the weights

At deployment the user's vocabulary is fixed for a while: the same twenty part names, all shift. YOLO-World calls this "prompt-then-detect". The text encoder runs once on the prompts, offline, and the resulting \htmlClass{t-yellow}{W} is stored. Stored text vectors are just numbers, and a dot product of every cell with each of C stored vectors is exactly what a 1x1 convolution with C filters computes.

Guess first. Press 2 Text in the neck and note the gate of the selected forklift cell. When the text encoder is folded into a 1x1 convolution, will that gate change? Decide, then press 3 Fold into weights.

It does not change. Folding moves where \htmlClass{t-yellow}{W} is computed, not its value, because the text encoder never sees the image our reading. The paper writes the folded gate this way, reshaping \htmlClass{t-yellow}{W} to C\times D\times 1\times 1, the weights of a 1x1 convolution:

\htmlData{step=4, link=features}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{X'} = \htmlClass{t-blue}{X} \odot} \htmlData{step=3, link=gate}{\texttt{Sigmoid}(} \htmlData{step=2, link=max}{\texttt{max}(} \htmlData{step=1, link=conv}{\texttt{Conv}(\htmlClass{t-blue}{X}, \htmlClass{t-yellow}{W})} \htmlData{step=2, link=max}{, \texttt{dim=1})} \htmlData{step=3, link=gate}{)}

Eq. 4 in YOLO-World (2401.17270v3), Appendix A.1.

  • \texttt{Conv}(\htmlClass{t-blue}{X}, \htmlClass{t-yellow}{W}): a 1x1 convolution whose C filters are the stored text vectors. It outputs C similarity maps, one per prompt, for a batch of B images: B\times C\times H\times W.
  • \texttt{max}(\cdot, \texttt{dim=1}): the max over the prompts, per cell. \texttt{Sigmoid}: the gate.
  • \odot: the paper calls it "matrix multiplication with reshape or transpose"; in effect it scales each cell's features by its gate. \htmlClass{t-blue}{X} and \htmlClass{t-blue}{X'} are B\times D\times H\times W.

The classifier folds the same way: after fine-tuning, the paper stores the class text vectors "into the weights of the classification layers" (Appendix A.2). One part does not fold. I-Pooling reads the 27 pooled tokens of the current image, so the update to the text vectors still runs on every image after folding (Appendix A.1, Eq. 5 and 6). Eq. 6 is printed with an unbalanced parenthesis and does not match Eq. 3, so this page quotes neither and does not repair it. The deployed network is a YOLO with a few extra 1x1 convolutions and one tiny attention, and no text encoder.

What folding buys

The paper measures every model on one V100 GPU without TensorRT, and evaluates zero-shot on LVIS minival with Fixed AP, the LVIS protocol that MDETR, GLIP and DetCLIP report (the GLIP and Grounding DINO rows are copied from their own papers, which do not say they used Fixed AP: treat those two rows as approximate), keeping at most 1000 predictions per image. Branch: YOLOE takes the text back out of the neck

YOLOE (2025) keeps a plain YOLO and puts text back at the classifier only. It caches the CLIP text vectors, refines them with a small network that folds into the last 1x1 convolution after training, and removes YOLO-World's fusion in the neck. Its roadmap table measures that removal on its own baseline, YOLO-Worldv2-L trained for 30 epochs: standard AP (not Fixed AP) on LVIS minival falls from 31.9 to 30.0, a loss of 1.9 AP, while speed on a T4 GPU with TensorRT rises from 80.0 to 102.5 FPS, 1.28 times faster (YOLOE Table 5; not comparable with the chart above). The same head also takes a box as the prompt, or no prompt at all, with names retrieved from a built-in list of 4585; chapter 11 returns to those modes.
