DN-DETR and DINO: teach with noisy answers

Even with better queries, DETR-style training has a quieter problem. Early on, the Hungarian matching is unstable: for the same image, a slot matched to the dog this epoch may be matched to the car the next. Its training target keeps flipping, so it keeps unlearning. DN-DETR measures this directly, counting how many slots change their assigned object between consecutive epochs.

The fix is a piece of teaching folklore: give the student problems whose answer is known. Alongside the normal queries, DN-DETR feeds the decoder noised copies of the ground-truth boxes, shifted and rescaled a little, sometimes with a wrong label, and trains them to recover the original. These queries skip matching entirely; their target is fixed by construction. The model learns to refine a nearby box, a skill the matching queries then share, and the whole denoising part is dropped at test time, so inference costs nothing extra.

In DN-DETR's words, a box is shifted so its center moves by |\Delta x| < \lambda_1 w/2 and |\Delta y| < \lambda_1 h/2, and rescaled so its width lies in [(1-\lambda_2)w, (1+\lambda_2)w] (height likewise), with 0.4 for both scales. Labels are flipped to a random class at rate 0.2, which forces the model to use the box to recover the class. There are 5 noised groups by default.

One detail is load-bearing: an attention mask. The matching queries must not see the denoising queries (they would just copy near-perfect boxes), and one noised group must not see another (another noisy copy of the same object leaks the answer). Denoising without the mask does not just fail to help; it collapses to 24.0 AP against 43.4 with it.

DINO then stacks the best ideas so far, DAB-style box queries, DN-style denoising and Deformable attention, and adds three of its own:

  • Contrastive denoising. Plain denoising only teaches "fix a nearby box". DINO adds hard negatives: noised boxes a bit further out that must be answered with "no object". That teaches the model to reject a near-miss, which cuts duplicates. Toggle DINO's noise above to see the two shells.
  • Mixed query selection. The decoder's anchor boxes start from the encoder's top-scoring locations for this image, while the content part of each query stays a learned vector. The encoder's rough features are good at where, not yet at what.
  • Look forward twice. In box refinement, layer i's correction is normally trained only by layer i's loss. DINO lets the refined box of the next layer also send gradient back, so a layer is judged partly by how much it helps its successor.

In DINO's ablation (ResNet-50), engineering improvements and query selection borrowed from Deformable DETR first lift DN-DETR from 43.4 to a strong baseline of 46.5 AP; DINO's own three additions then take it to 47.9. DINO-4scale reaches 49.0 AP in only 12 epochs (4 feature scales, no extra data). The famous 63.3 AP on COCO test-dev needs a Swin-L backbone, pre-training on Objects365 and test-time augmentation, so it is a different kind of number.

Act IIIMaking DETR run fast

RT-DETR: DETRs beat YOLOs once you count NMS

By 2023 DETRs trained fast enough, but they were still too slow at inference for real-time use, where YOLO dominated. RT-DETR starts by pointing out something odd about the comparison: YOLO speed numbers usually leave out NMS. And NMS time is not a constant. It depends on how many boxes survive the confidence threshold, and on the IoU threshold. Step through the thresholds the paper measured.

Lower thresholds keep more boxes and slightly more AP, and they cost more NMS time: at the paper's best-accuracy setting YOLOv8's NMS kernel alone takes 2.36 ms. A set predictor has no such knob to trade.

RT-DETR's own changes attack the remaining cost, the encoder. It runs self-attention only on the coarsest feature map, where each cell already stands for a whole object part (the paper calls this AIFI), and fuses the finer scales with a cheap convolutional path (CCFF). Its queries are chosen from the encoder's best features, with a training loss that makes a high class score also mean a well-placed box; in the ablation that is worth 48.7 versus 47.9 AP. And because every decoder layer is supervised, you can drop layers at inference to trade accuracy for speed without retraining: stopping one layer early costs 0.1 AP and saves half a millisecond.

The result, measured end to end with NMS included for the YOLOs, on a T4 GPU with TensorRT FP16 at 640 x 640: RT-DETR-R50 reaches 53.1 AP at 108 FPS, against YOLOv8-L at 52.9 AP and 71 FPS. Barely more accurate, about half again as fast.

RF-DETR: train once, pick your latency

Real-time detectors usually come as a hand-designed ladder of sizes, N, S, M, L, X, each trained separately and tuned on COCO. RF-DETR asks why. It takes a DETR with a pretrained DINOv2 vision-transformer backbone and trains one weight-sharing network per backbone size: at every training step it samples a random patch size, input resolution and number of attention windows, so the same weights learn to work under all of them. The number of decoder layers and queries is cut only at inference.

After training, a grid search over thousands of configurations measures accuracy and latency for each, and keeps the best trade-offs. Picking a model is then picking a latency budget. Slide the budget.

Two numbers stand out. The nano model reaches 48.0 AP at 2.3 ms, 5.3 AP above D-FINE's nano at a similar latency. And the 2XL model is the first real-time detector above 60: 60.1 AP at 17.2 ms. It does not win everywhere; turn on the baselines and you can see D-FINE ahead at the M, L and XL sizes on COCO.

There are really two base networks here, one on a small DINOv2 backbone (N to L) and one on a larger one (XL to Max), each trained once. Watch the readout as you raise the budget: on the small backbone the patch size stays at 16 while resolution and decoder depth grow; the large backbone switches to patch 20, and Max changes again to patch 12. The paper notes that accuracy tracks the number of spatial locations, resolution divided by patch size, more closely than either alone. The search itself is paid for once: about 10,000 GPU hours on T4s to evaluate the configurations. On the 100-dataset RF100-VL benchmark, the paper finds that COCO-tuned architectures already transfer remarkably well, and a per-dataset search adds a further 0.1 to 0.7 AP at most sizes.

Notice what never changed from 2020 to here: a fixed set of queries, one-to-one matching, and no NMS. Everything else, the attention, the queries, the training signal, the backbone and the search, was rebuilt around that idea.

Summary

DETR reframed detection as predicting a set: a fixed number of query slots, trained with a one-to-one Hungarian matching so that duplicates are punished by the loss itself, which removes anchors and NMS. The price was dense attention over every pixel, which made training long and small objects hard. Deformable DETR made attention sparse and multi-scale; DAB-DETR made each query an explicit box refined layer by layer; DN-DETR and DINO stabilized training with denoising queries and hard negatives, reaching strong accuracy in a dozen epochs. RT-DETR then showed that, once NMS is counted, a DETR can beat YOLO at real-time speed, and RF-DETR trained a single weight-sharing network from which a whole family of speed-accuracy trade-offs can be chosen after training.
