What it cost: long training and small objects

The headline result was a clean tie. DETR with a ResNet-50 reached 42.0 AP on COCO, the same as a carefully tuned Faster R-CNN with FPN (42.0 AP), with no anchors and no NMS. But the tie hides two very different profiles.

On large objects DETR was much better: 61.1 versus 53.4 APL. On small objects it was much worse: 20.5 versus 26.6 APS. And it needed 500 epochs of training for the headline number, against 109 epochs for the baseline.

The small-object problem traces back to the encoder. Every one of the HW feature-map cells attends to every other, so the cost grows with the square of the number of cells. DETR can only afford the coarse stride-32 map, where a small object may cover a single cell. The long training has more than one suspect, and the next chapters meet them: attention over image features that starts out nearly uniform over thousands of pixels (Deformable DETR's diagnosis), and object queries that carry no clear notion of position (DAB-DETR's). Doubling the resolution (the paper's DC5 variant) multiplies encoder self-attention cost by 16x and drops speed from 28 to 12 FPS. Slide the stride to feel the square.

In the paper's own accounting (Appendix A.3), one encoder self-attention layer costs

\mathcal{O}\big(d^2\,\htmlClass{t-blue}{HW} + d\,(\htmlClass{t-red}{HW})^2\big)
  • d: the feature width (d = 256). The first term is the linear projections, one per cell.
  • The second term is every cell comparing itself with every other cell. Once HW is in the thousands it dominates, and it is the one that explodes when you ask for finer features.

The paper names the long schedule and small objects as the "new challenges" of its design. Everything in Act II is an answer to them.

Act IIMaking DETR train fast

Deformable DETR: look at a few points, not every pixel

Here is the idea that fixed attention over image features, in the encoder and in the decoder's cross-attention alike. A query does not need to compare itself against every pixel. Give it a reference point, and let it predict a handful of sampling points near it: where to look, and how much to trust each look. No query-key dot products at all; the offsets and the weights both come from the query through one linear layer.

Deformable DETR uses M = 8, K = 4: eight heads, each sampling four points, on each of L = 4 feature levels. That is 128 per query sampled points, no matter how large the feature maps are. Drag the reference point around.

Written out, deformable attention for one query (Deformable DETR Eq. 2) is

\mathrm{DeformAttn}(\mathbf{z}_q, \mathbf{p}_q, \mathbf{x}) = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \mathbf{W}_m \Big[\sum_{k=1}^{K} \htmlClass{t-green}{A_{mqk}} \cdot \mathbf{W}'_m\, \mathbf{x}\big(\mathbf{p}_q + \htmlClass{t-blue}{\Delta\mathbf{p}_{mqk}}\big)\Big]
  • \mathbf{z}_q: the query's feature; \mathbf{p}_q: its reference point.
  • \htmlClass{t-blue}{\Delta\mathbf{p}_{mqk}}: where head m's k-th point looks, relative to the reference point, predicted from \mathbf{z}_q. Fractional, so the feature is read by bilinear interpolation.
  • \htmlClass{t-green}{A_{mqk}}: how much that look counts; the K weights of a head sum to 1. Also predicted from \mathbf{z}_q, not from comparing with keys.
  • \mathbf{W}'_m, \mathbf{W}_m: the usual value and output projections.

The multi-scale version (Eq. 3) is the same sum with an extra loop over the L levels, and one softmax across all L x K points of a head, so a head can weigh fine and coarse evidence against each other. That is FPN-style fusion done by attention itself; adding an actual FPN on top gave no gain in the paper's ablation.

The effect on training was dramatic. Deformable DETR reached 43.8 AP in 50 epochs, against DETR's 42.0 AP in 500 epochs, and small-object AP rose to 26.4 APS. Sparse attention also starts out focused, instead of spread uniformly over thousands of pixels, which the paper gives as a reason for the faster convergence. Two variants mattered for the future: iterative box refinement, where each decoder layer corrects the previous layer's box (45.4 AP), and a two-stage version whose queries start from the encoder's best proposals (46.2 AP). The cost: it runs 25% slower than Faster R-CNN at similar FLOPs, because scattered sampling is unfriendly to memory.

DAB-DETR: a query is a box

Back to that loose end: what is an object query? DAB-DETR's answer starts by splitting it in two. A decoder query has a content part (what it has seen so far) and a positional part (where it is looking). In DETR the positional part is an opaque learned vector, and the paper's visualizations show it spreads attention over several unrelated regions at once, which it conjectures is a root cause of the slow training.

So make the positional part literally a box. Each of the 300 queries owns an anchor (\htmlClass{t-blue}{x}, \htmlClass{t-blue}{y}, \htmlClass{t-blue}{w}, \htmlClass{t-blue}{h}). Its center steers where attention looks, its width and height stretch attention into an oval of the right size, and after every decoder layer the box is nudged toward the object. Drag the box, change its size, and watch the positional attention follow.

The anchor becomes the positional query in two steps (DAB-DETR Eq. 1-2): encode each of the four numbers with sines and cosines, then map the concatenation through a small MLP shared by all layers.

P_q = \mathrm{MLP}\big(\mathrm{PE}(\htmlClass{t-blue}{A_q})\big),\qquad \mathrm{PE}(\htmlClass{t-blue}{A_q}) = \mathrm{Cat}\big(\mathrm{PE}(\htmlClass{t-blue}{x_q}), \mathrm{PE}(\htmlClass{t-blue}{y_q}), \mathrm{PE}(\htmlClass{t-blue}{w_q}), \mathrm{PE}(\htmlClass{t-blue}{h_q})\big)

The oval comes from Eq. 6: the similarity between an image position (x, y) and the anchor center is computed separately along x and along y, and each part is divided by the anchor's size relative to a reference size.

\mathrm{ModulateAttn} = \Big(\mathrm{PE}(x)\cdot \mathrm{PE}(\htmlClass{t-blue}{x_{\rm ref}})\,\frac{w_{q,\rm ref}}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{w_q}} + \mathrm{PE}(y)\cdot \mathrm{PE}(\htmlClass{t-blue}{y_{\rm ref}})\,\frac{h_{q,\rm ref}}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{h_q}}\Big) \big/ \sqrt{D}
  • A wide anchor (large \htmlClass{t-blue}{w_q}) shrinks the x term, so attention flattens and spreads horizontally. A narrow one sharpens it.
  • This happens before the softmax, so it reshapes where the query looks, not what it does with what it finds.

DAB-DETR also found that the standard temperature T = 10000 in the sine encoding, designed for integer word positions, is a poor fit for box coordinates between 0 and 1; it uses T=20. Flip between the two temperatures above to see why: at 10000 the attention is nearly flat.

After each of the six decoder layers a shared head predicts a correction, and the anchor is updated in logit space, \sigma(\sigma^{-1}(\text{old}) + \Delta): the rule Deformable DETR prints in its Appendix A.4, which DAB-DETR says it follows. In the paper's ablation, removing this layer-by-layer update costs 1.7 AP. The full model reaches 45.7 AP in 50 epochs (ResNet-50 with dilated last stage and 3 pattern embeddings).
