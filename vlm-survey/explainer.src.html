Act IA shared space for pictures and words

Classifiers only know their labels

A classic image classifier can only answer with one of the labels it was trained on; to teach it a new concept you collect new labeled photos. CLIP's fix was to stop predicting labels and instead play a matching game: given a pile of photos and a pile of captions, say which caption goes with which photo.

The wall: a fixed list of answers

Before CLIP, the strongest vision models were trained to pick one entry from a predetermined list of object categories. The CLIP paper names the cost directly: this restricted form of supervision limits their generality and usability since additional labeled data is needed to specify any other visual concept. A model that knows "dog" and "cat" has nothing to say about "a corgi wearing sunglasses", and it cannot even be asked.

The internet, meanwhile, is full of images that already come with text: alt-text, titles, captions. That text is messy, but it describes an open-ended world. CLIP gathered 400 million (image, text) pairs from the internet, found by searching for 500,000 different queries, and asked a model to learn from the text directly.

The matching game

CLIP has two encoders. The image encoder (a ResNet or a vision transformer) turns a photo into a vector; the text encoder (a transformer) turns a caption into a vector. A learned linear projection maps both into the same space, and each vector is scaled to length one, so every photo and every caption becomes a direction. In the figure the space is a circle and each direction is a point on it: blue dots are photos, yellow diamonds are captions.

How similar two directions are is measured by the cosine of the angle between them: 1 when they point the same way, 0 at right angles, -1 when opposite. Take a batch of N photo-caption pairs and compute the cosine of every photo against every caption. That is the N \times N table on the right. The correct answers are all on the diagonal: photo 1 goes with caption 1, and so on. Every off-diagonal cell is a negative, a pairing that is wrong, and the batch provides them for free.

The task is then a classification problem inside each row: out of the N captions, pick the one that belongs to this photo. And the same inside each column: out of the N photos, pick the one that belongs to this caption. Press Train. Every point moves at once. Each photo is pulled toward its own caption and pushed away from all the others, so the pairs close up and spread apart around the circle. The lines show, for each photo, which caption it currently sits closest to: green if that is its own caption, red dashes if not.

CLIP played this game with a batch of 32,768, so each training step filled in a table of 32,768 × 32,768 ≈ 1.07 billion similarities, almost all of them negatives. Keep that number in mind: the next chapter is about what it costs.

The loss, term by term

For one row (one photo), turn the row's similarities into probabilities with a softmax and ask for a high probability on the correct caption. The loss for photos is the average, over all rows, of the negative log of that probability:

\htmlData{step=4}{\htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{img}}} = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}} \log \frac{\htmlData{step=1, link=pos}{\htmlClass{t-green}{\exp(s_{ii}/\tau)}}}{\htmlData{step=2, link=neg}{\sum_{j=1}^{N} \exp(s_{ij}/\tau)}} \qquad \htmlData{step=5}{\htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\big(\htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{img}}} + \htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{txt}}}\big)} \qquad \htmlData{step=3, link=sim}{s_{ij} = \htmlClass{t-blue}{u_i}^{\top}\htmlClass{t-yellow}{v_j}}

Written out from the Fig. 3 pseudocode of CLIP v1, which has no numbered equation; ALIGN v2 Eq. 1 and 2 are the two halves in the same form.

  • \htmlClass{t-blue}{u_i}, \htmlClass{t-yellow}{v_j}: the unit-length embeddings of photo i and caption j (a blue dot and a yellow diamond).
  • s_{ij}: their cosine similarity, the number printed in cell (i, j) of the table.
  • \htmlClass{t-green}{\exp(s_{ii}/\tau)}: the score of the correct pair, the green diagonal cell.
  • The denominator: the same score summed over every caption in the row, the right one and all the negatives. The fraction is the softmax probability of the correct caption; it reaches 1 only when the right caption beats every wrong one by a wide margin.
  • \tau: the temperature, which divides every similarity before the softmax (below).
  • \htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{txt}}}: the same thing down each column (for each caption, find its photo). CLIP averages the two: loss = (loss_i + loss_t)/2.

Notice what the denominator does: the loss for photo i depends on its similarity to every caption in the batch. That coupling is what makes the game informative, and, as the next chapter shows, what makes it expensive.

Temperature: how sharp the verdict is

Cosines only range from -1 to 1, so a softmax over raw cosines is mushy: even a perfect layout gives the right caption only a modest lead. Dividing by a small temperature \tau stretches the gaps before the softmax. Drag the temperature slider and watch the table's colors: at high temperature every row is washed out; at low temperature each row snaps to its best match.

Now predict, then check: what will training do differently when it is hot? Press Train hot, then Train cold. Hot, the pairs still end up on top of their captions, but the loss stalls high and the table stays washed out: when cosines are divided by 1, even a perfect layout cannot earn a confident probability. Cold, the loss falls to almost zero within a few dozen steps; once every photo's own caption wins clearly, the gradient all but vanishes, so from this start two pairs (car and pizza) are still slightly open when the run ends (their diagonal cells read 0.91, not 1.00). CLIP does not hand-tune this: the temperature is directly optimized during training as a log-parameterized multiplicative scalar, starting from the equivalent of 0.07 and clipped to prevent scaling the logits by more than 100.

Backward: where the gradient goes

Stage 8 said the gradient "splits" at the table. Here is what that means for one photo. The arrows show which way training pushes the dog photo's vector, one arrow per caption.

Press Sitting on "cat". Only one caption pushes: "cat", the one the photo is currently confused with. The other three wrong captions barely register, because their softmax probability is already close to zero. The loss spends its effort on the negatives that are hard right now, and (our reading) that is one reason a huge batch helps: more captions per step means more chances that one of them is a hard negative for each photo. Press Next to its caption: once the right caption wins its row and column, the pull shrinks to almost nothing too.

\frac{\partial \htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial \htmlClass{t-blue}{u_i}} = \htmlData{step=3, link=tau}{\frac{1}{N\tau}} \sum_{j=1}^{N} \Big( \htmlData{step=2, link=neg}{\htmlClass{t-brown}{\tfrac{p_{ij} + q_{ij}}{2}}} \htmlData{step=1, link=pos}{\htmlClass{t-green}{{} - \delta_{ij}}} \Big)\, \htmlClass{t-yellow}{v_j} \qquad \htmlData{step=4, link=radial}{\frac{\partial \htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial x_i} = \frac{1}{\lVert x_i \rVert}\big(I - \htmlClass{t-blue}{u_i}\htmlClass{t-blue}{u_i}^{\top}\big)\frac{\partial \htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial \htmlClass{t-blue}{u_i}}}

Derived here from the loss above (CLIP v1 prints no gradient); u_i = x_i / \lVert x_i \rVert, where x_i is photo i's vector before normalization.

  • \htmlClass{t-green}{-\delta_{ij}}: minus one for the photo's own caption (j = i), zero otherwise. Gradient descent moves against the gradient, so this term pulls u_i toward its own caption v_i: the green arrow.
  • \htmlClass{t-brown}{p_{ij}}: the probability that photo i's row gives to caption j. \htmlClass{t-brown}{q_{ij}}: the probability that caption j's column gives to photo i. Caption j pushes the photo away in proportion to how confused the two are: p_{ij} is how much of the photo's row the wrong caption steals, q_{ij} how much of that caption's column the photo steals. These are the brown arrows. All the arrows shrink toward zero as the photo wins its own row and column outright.
  • 1/(N\tau): the same for every arrow. A small temperature enlarges this factor, but it also saturates the softmax, so easy pairs get almost no gradient. (The paper's stated reason for capping the logit scale is only that it was necessary to prevent training instability.)
  • The second formula is the normalization step on the way back. The factor I - u_i u_i^{\top} deletes the part of the gradient that points along u_i itself: changing a vector's length cannot change a cosine. Press Apply the normalization: the dashed arrow is the raw sum, the grey arrow is the part that gets deleted, and the bold arrow is what is left, which can only slide the photo around the circle.

From \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial x_i on it is ordinary backpropagation: x_i = W_i^{\top} f_i, so W_i gets \sum_{n=1}^{N} f_n\,(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial x_n)^{\top}, and each f_n (the [class] output) passes the rest down through the vision transformer. Captions get the mirror image: each v_j is pulled toward its own photo and pushed by the photos it is confused with (the same p and q terms, seen from the caption's side), and that gradient flows down the text lane to the [EOS] position and, through masked attention, to every word before it. The temperature's gradient is the sum over the whole table of each logit's gradient times the logit itself: it gets sharper when sharpening would lower the loss.

Press Take gradient steps from any starting point. The photo slides around the circle toward its caption and slows as it arrives, the same saturating gradient as the cold training run in the first figure.

Zero-shot: the text is the classifier

Here is the payoff. To classify a photo into any set of classes, write one caption per class, embed the captions, and pick the caption whose direction is closest to the photo. No new training, no labeled examples: the class names themselves become the classifier. Press Zero-shot a new photo: an unseen photo lands near the trained dog photo (that placement is the toy's assumption, standing in for a trained image encoder), and the bars show its softmax over the five prompts.

The wording of those captions matters. Alt-text is rarely a single word, so CLIP wraps each class name in a sentence. The template A photo of a {label}. alone improves accuracy on ImageNet by 1.3%, and averaging the embeddings of 80 differently worded prompts per class adds a further 3.5%.

The headline: with no ImageNet training images at all, CLIP's best model (ViT-L/14 at 336 pixels) reaches 76.2% top-1 zero-shot on ImageNet, which matches the performance of the original ResNet-50 despite using none of the 1.28 million crowd-labeled training examples that ResNet-50 was trained on. The 336-pixel version is the 224-pixel model with one extra step: the authors pre-train at a higher 336 pixel resolution for one additional epoch, an early sign of how much input resolution will matter later on this page.

ALIGN: the same game on noisier data

In the same month, Google's ALIGN played the same game with far less cleaning. It used 1.8B image-text pairs of raw alt-text with only simple frequency filters, for example exclude alt-texts shared by more than 10 images and discard texts shorter than 3 unigrams or longer than 20 unigrams. Its point was that the scale of the corpus can make up for its noise: in a controlled comparison, its noisy data outperforms Conceptual Captions with only 4x size, and it edged CLIP on zero-shot ImageNet, 76.4 vs 76.2.

The loss is the same shape with small differences: the ALIGN authors minimize the sum of two losses rather than their average, add label smoothing of 0.1, and learn the temperature from an initial 1.0 (in the paper's ablations, on a smaller EfficientNet-B5 and BERT-Base model, it converged to about 1/64).

What CLIP and ALIGN leave behind is an image encoder whose vectors already mean something in words. Almost every later model on this page starts from an encoder trained this way; the exceptions are BLIP (Act II), whose ViT starts from weights pre-trained on ImageNet, and Chameleon (Act VI), which has no image encoder at all. But the matching game only ever says "which caption?"; it cannot write a sentence. Getting from matching to talking is Act II. First, the loss itself hit a wall.

The softmax needs the whole batch

CLIP's loss asks every photo to pick its caption out of the whole batch, so no single cell of the table can be scored without looking at its entire row and column. SigLIP replaced that softmax with a plain yes/no question per cell, and the same five photos and captions from the last chapter show what changes.

The wall: every cell depends on the whole batch

Look again at CLIP's loss for one photo: a softmax over its row. The probability of the right caption is its score divided by the sum of the scores of every caption in the batch. Press Softmax view and pick any cell: the loss that cell feeds into spans its whole row, and the mirror-image loss for captions spans its whole column. One similarity is tied to 2N - 1 cells (9 of the 25 here).

At toy size that is harmless. At CLIP's batch of 32,768, the photos and captions are spread across many accelerators, each holding a slice of the batch. To finish even one row, a device needs every caption embedding from every other device (an all-gather), and the textbook implementation then builds the full table of pairwise similarities in memory. Even the numerics want the whole batch: a softmax is usually stabilised by subtracting the row's maximum first, which requires another pass over the full batch.

A yes/no question per cell

SigLIP (Sigmoid loss for Language-Image Pre-training) drops the competition. Each cell asks its own question: do this photo and this caption belong together? The diagonal cells should say yes, every other cell should say no, and each answer is scored by a logistic (sigmoid) loss, the same loss as any binary classifier. The paper's phrase is that the loss processes every image-text pair independently: the batch of N pairs becomes N^2 separate binary examples. Press Sigmoid view. The table now shows, for each cell, the probability the model gives to "match", computed from that cell alone, and the red outline shrinks to the single cell you picked.

There is a catch hiding in that table: in a batch of N pairs there are only N yes-cells and N^2 - N no-cells. The softmax never noticed, because each row had exactly one right answer. The sigmoid sees 20 no-cells for every 5 yes-cells here, and a real batch is far more lopsided.

Predict: what if the bias starts at 0?

SigLIP adds a learnable bias b to every cell's score before the sigmoid, and starts it at -10, with the scale t (the inverse temperature) starting at log 10 (t = 10) in its log form. A cosine is at most 1, so at t = 10 a score is at most 10, and with the bias at -10 every cell starts out saying "no": the prior, correct for all the negatives. With the bias at 0, a cell at right angles is a coin flip and every cell with a positive cosine says "yes". Before pressing, guess which start ends with all five pairs matched.

Press Train, bias starts at -10. Nothing in the table says yes at first (the readout counts 0 of 25), so the only strong signal is the five diagonal cells asking to be pulled together; they close up within a few dozen steps, the pairs spread around the circle, and all five match. Now press Train, bias starts at 0 from the same start. 12 of the 25 cells begin as "yes", 9 of them wrongly, and the twenty negatives dominate the first steps: the fastest way to silence them is to push everything apart, correct pairs included. Three photos and their three captions end up clumped in two lumps, the bias has only crawled part of the way down, and those three photos can no longer tell their captions apart.

That is the toy's picture of what the paper describes: without the bias, the heavy imbalance coming from the many negatives dominates the loss, leading to large initial optimization steps attempting to correct this bias, while the -10 start starts roughly close to the prior and does not require massive over-correction. The toy exaggerates the effect (it is two-dimensional and keeps t fixed). In the paper's ablation (Table 4, a Base model), ImageNet zero-shot accuracy is 62.0 vs 63.0 for no bias against the -10 start, and a bias started at 0 gives 61.7; with the scale t also started small (t' = \log 1, so t = 1: a hot start in chapter 1's terms), it falls to 53.7.

The two losses, side by side

First the softmax loss, as SigLIP writes it for CLIP, with t the inverse of the temperature \tau from chapter 1. Each term is a whole row or a whole column:

\htmlData{step=3}{\htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{soft}}} = -\frac{1}{2|B|}\sum_{i=1}^{|B|}\Big(} \htmlData{step=1, link=row}{\log \frac{e^{t\,\htmlClass{t-blue}{x_i}\cdot \htmlClass{t-yellow}{y_i}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{|B|} e^{t\,\htmlClass{t-blue}{x_i}\cdot \htmlClass{t-yellow}{y_j}}}} + \htmlData{step=2, link=col}{\log \frac{e^{t\,\htmlClass{t-blue}{x_i}\cdot \htmlClass{t-yellow}{y_i}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{|B|} e^{t\,\htmlClass{t-blue}{x_j}\cdot \htmlClass{t-yellow}{y_i}}}} \htmlData{step=3}{\Big)}

Unnumbered, Sec. 3.1 of SigLIP v4 (the same loss as CLIP's Fig. 3 pseudocode, with t = 1/τ).

Then the sigmoid loss. One term per cell, and nothing in the term mentions any other cell:

\htmlData{step=4, link=pairs}{\htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{sig}}} = -\frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{i=1}^{|B|}\sum_{j=1}^{|B|}} \log \sigma\big(\htmlData{step=1, link=label}{\htmlClass{t-green}{z_{ij}}} \cdot \big(\htmlData{step=2, link=logit}{t\,\htmlClass{t-blue}{x_i}\cdot \htmlClass{t-yellow}{y_j}} \htmlData{step=3, link=bias}{+\, b}\big)\big)

Unnumbered, Sec. 3.2 of SigLIP v4, written in the form of Algorithm 1 (logits = t x.y + b). The equation printed in Sec. 3.2 has the opposite sign on b; with b starting at -10, only the Algorithm 1 form makes every cell start at "no", so we follow Algorithm 1.

  • B: the batch of photo-caption pairs; |B| is its size, the N of the toy.
  • \htmlClass{t-blue}{x_i}, \htmlClass{t-yellow}{y_j}: the unit-length embeddings of photo i and caption j, so x_i \cdot y_j is the cosine in cell (i, j).
  • \htmlClass{t-green}{z_{ij}}: the label, +1 on the diagonal (a true pair) and -1 everywhere else.
  • t: the inverse temperature, learned as t = e^{t'}; it stretches cosines from [-1, 1] into scores from -t to t.
  • b: the learned bias, shared by every cell; it sets where "no" turns into "yes".
  • \sigma(u) = 1/(1 + e^{-u}): the sigmoid, the probability of the answer the label asks for. Its log is 0 when the cell answers confidently and correctly, and very negative when it is confidently wrong.
  • The sum runs over all |B|^2 cells but is divided by |B|, not |B|^2.

Why this lets the batch grow

Because each cell's term stands alone, the table never has to exist all at once. SigLIP splits the loss by device. With the batch spread over D devices, each device holds its own slice of photos and captions, the per-device batch b_{\text{dev}} = |B|/D (the paper calls this b too, a different quantity from the bias). Each device scores its own photos against its own captions, which contains all its positives, then passes its captions to the next device and scores the new block, which is all negatives, and so on around the ring until every photo has met every caption. A final sum across devices adds up the pieces.

Press The paper's mock setup: it is the example in SigLIP's Fig. 1, 3 devices and a global batch size of 12. The captions hop around three times and every cell gets scored, yet at any moment each device holds only one block of size 4 x 4, and there are no all-gathers. Press Softmax: full table for comparison, then drag the devices slider. The paper states the scaling: memory at any moment falls from |B|^2 to b^2 (b = |B|/D per-device batch), a factor of D^2. The paper adds that "Usually b is constant as scaling |B| is achieved by increasing the number of accelerators", so the per-device block stays the same size while the full table grows with the square of the device count. Passing captions around is cheap too: the paper notes that D collective permutes is typically faster than two all-gathers between D devices.

\htmlData{step=1, link=devices}{\htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{sig}}} = -\frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{d_i=1}^{D}} \htmlData{step=2, link=swap}{\sum_{d_j=1}^{D}} \htmlData{step=3, link=block}{\sum_{i=b_{\text{dev}} d_i}^{b_{\text{dev}}(d_i+1)}\ \sum_{j=b_{\text{dev}} d_j}^{b_{\text{dev}}(d_j+1)} \htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}_{ij}}}

Unnumbered, Sec. 3.3 of SigLIP v4; the paper writes the per-device batch as b, we write bdev to keep it apart from the bias.

  • First sum, over devices d_i: every device works in parallel on its own photos; the final cross-device sum adds their results.
  • Second sum, over d_j: the captions of device d_j visit each device in turn (the paper's label: "swap negs across devices").
  • Inner double sum: one b_{\text{dev}} \times b_{\text{dev}} block of cells, the only part of the table in memory at that moment; \mathcal{L}_{ij} is the per-cell term of the sigmoid loss above.

The softmax cannot be split this simply: each row's sum needs every caption before that row's term can be finished. In practice the saving is concrete. The paper reports that four TPU-v4 chips fit a batch of 4096 vs 2048 for a Base SigLIP model against the corresponding CLIP model.

What the bigger batches bought

The surprise is that SigLIP's authors, having made huge batches cheap, found they barely need them. The sigmoid loss wins most clearly at small batch: when the batch is smaller than 16 k it beats the softmax by a large margin, for example ImageNet zero-shot at batch 512: 51.5 vs 47.7 (SigLIP, sigmoid first) and 77.2 vs 74.6 for SigLiT (below). As the batch grows the gap closes. SigLIP performs best at batch size 32 k, whereas the softmax loss required 98 k, and the softmax did not beat it even there. The abstract's conclusion: the benefits of growing batch size quickly diminish, and a batch of 32 k is enough.

The extreme experiment was run on SigLiT, a cheaper variant: the image tower is a frozen, already-trained ViT whose embeddings are precomputed (the "Locked-image Tuning" setup), and only the text tower learns. SigLiT trained at a batch of one million; performance saturated around 32 k and the model peaks at 256 k. No from-scratch SigLIP was trained at a million. The frozen tower also makes SigLiT very cheap. In the abstract's words, "with only four TPUv4 chips, we train a SigLiT model that achieves 84.5% ImageNet zero-shot accuracy in two days."

Inside SigLIP: forward, then backward

SigLIP keeps CLIP's two-lane machine and changes one box: the loss. Step through it below and watch for the three differences, the per-cell sigmoid with its learnable t and b, a backward pass in which no cell's gradient waits for its row, and (in the SigLiT variant) an image lane that is frozen solid.

Backward: no cell waits for its row

Stage 7 claimed that a cell's gradient depends only on that cell. Here is the check. Both tables start mostly solved; then one wrong cell, the dog photo against the caption "cat", rises. Before you press, guess: in each table, which cells' gradients will change?

Press Dog photo drifts toward "cat". On the softmax side the red cell turns brown, as expected, but it drags others with it: the dog photo's own cell and the caption "cat"'s own cell suddenly pull hard, because they share a row or column with the intruder and their softmax probability has dropped. Every cell of that row and column changes a little. On the sigmoid side only the red cell changes. The true pairs were already being pulled (a cosine of 0.80 is still below the "yes" line that b = -10 draws), and they are pulled exactly as hard as before.

\frac{\partial \htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{sig}}}}{\partial \ell_{ij}} = \frac{1}{|B|}\big(\htmlData{step=1, link=cell}{\sigma(\ell_{ij})} \htmlData{step=2, link=own}{\htmlClass{t-green}{{} - \delta_{ij}}}\big) \qquad \htmlData{step=3, link=rowcol}{\frac{\partial \htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{soft}}}}{\partial \ell_{ij}} = \frac{1}{|B|}\Big(\htmlClass{t-brown}{\tfrac{p_{ij} + q_{ij}}{2}} \htmlClass{t-green}{{} - \delta_{ij}}\Big)} \qquad \htmlData{step=4}{\frac{\partial \htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{sig}}}}{\partial \htmlClass{t-blue}{x_i}} = t \sum_{j} \frac{\partial \htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{sig}}}}{\partial \ell_{ij}}\, \htmlClass{t-yellow}{y_j}}

Derived here from the Algorithm 1 form of the loss (SigLIP v4 prints no gradient); \ell_{ij} = t\,x_i \cdot y_j + b. The softmax version is the one derived in chapter 1, with t = 1/\tau.

  • \sigma(\ell_{ij}): the probability that cell (i, j) says "yes". It is computed from that one logit, so nothing else in the batch can change it. Write u = z_{ij}\ell_{ij}; the cell's loss is -\log\sigma(u), whose derivative is -(1 - \sigma(u))\,z_{ij}, and for z = +1 and z = -1 both cases come out as the formula shown.
  • \htmlClass{t-green}{-\delta_{ij}}: minus one on the diagonal, the pull toward "yes" for a true pair. It fades only as that pair's own \sigma approaches 1.
  • \htmlClass{t-brown}{p_{ij}}, \htmlClass{t-brown}{q_{ij}}: the row and column softmax of CLIP's loss. Each has a denominator summed over the whole row or column, which is why raising one wrong cell changed the gradients of its neighbours.
  • The last formula is the step into the image lane. Photo i still collects from every caption in its row: its gradient is a sum over j. What changed is that each term of that sum is known the moment its own cell is computed, so the sum can be built one block at a time as captions pass between devices (our reading: the paper describes the chunked forward pass, not its backward). The bias gets the plain sum of all cell gradients, and t gets \sum_{ij} (\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\ell_{ij})\, x_i \cdot y_j.

After this chapter we have what the rest of the page builds on: an image encoder and a text encoder whose vectors line up, trained with a loss that works at any batch size. SigLIP-trained image encoders turn up again in later chapters, feeding language models. But so far the image encoder has been a black box that turns a photo into one vector. Next: what it actually looks at, the grid of patches a vision transformer cuts every image into.

How an encoder sees: images as tokens

A transformer reads a sequence of vectors; a photo is a grid of pixels. The Vision Transformer (ViT) joins the two in the bluntest way available: cut the image into small squares and treat each square as one token.

Cut, flatten, project

ViT takes an image of H \times W pixels with C color channels and cuts it into squares of P \times P pixels. That gives N = HW/P^2 patches, and N is the length of the sequence the transformer reads. Each patch is then flattened: its pixels are laid end to end into one long list of P^2 C numbers (the strip in the figure). A single trainable linear projection, the matrix E, maps every strip to a vector of width D. From here on a patch is just a token, and the rest of the model is an ordinary transformer encoder. Press Flatten one patch to follow one square through these steps; click any other patch (or use the arrow keys on the figure) to follow that one instead.

The grid is the whole story of the cost. At the standard pretraining size of 224 pixels with 16-pixel patches, the image becomes 14 × 14 = 196 tokens. The paper spells out the consequence: the sequence length is inversely proportional to the square of the patch size. So check your prediction. Halving the patch side from 32 to 16 multiplies the token count by four, from 49 to 196, and because attention compares every token with every other, the number of pairs grows by sixteen, from 2,401 to 38,416. Raising the resolution does the same thing from the other side: double the image side at a fixed patch size and you again get four times the tokens and sixteen times the pairs.

The big TOTAL line survives to quite low resolutions, but the fine print under it (the card number) dissolves into grey long before that; drag the slider up and the fine print comes back, while the red bar shoots toward the millions. That is the trade every later model on this page negotiates: sharp enough to read, few enough tokens to afford.   • \htmlClass{t-blue}{\mathbf{x}^i_p}: patch number i, flattened into a row of P^2 \cdot C numbers. For 16-pixel RGB patches that is 16 × 16 × 3 = 768 numbers.
  • \mathbf{E}: one matrix, shared by every patch, with P^2 \cdot C rows and D columns. It turns each strip into a D-dimensional patch embedding. Only this matrix depends on the patch size; the transformer after it does not.
  • \htmlClass{t-blue}{\mathbf{x}_{\text{class}}}: one extra learned vector put in front of the patches, the [class] token (slot 0 in the figure's sequence). It sees no pixels of its own; after the last layer, its output is the summary of the whole image that a classifier reads.
  • \mathbf{E}_{pos}: the position embeddings, a learned table with one row for each of the N+1 slots, added to the tokens. Attention by itself treats its input as an unordered set, so without this table the model could not tell the top-left patch from the bottom-right one.
  • \htmlClass{t-blue}{\mathbf{z}_0}: the resulting sequence of N+1 vectors, which goes into a standard transformer (the paper's Eq. 2 and 3 are the usual pre-norm attention and MLP blocks, and Eq. 4 reads the image representation off the [class] token).

Two details of that position table matter later. First, it is one-dimensional: ViT uses standard learnable 1D position embeddings, simply numbering the patches in reading order, and at the start of training they carry no information about the 2D positions of the patches; which patches are neighbors has to be learned from data. Second, it has a fixed number of rows. The transformer itself can handle arbitrary sequence lengths (up to memory constraints), but a bigger image needs more rows than the table has, so to fine-tune at a higher resolution ViT performs 2D interpolation of the pre-trained position embeddings, stretching the table to the new grid. Act IV meets a model that throws this table away.

Names and sizes

ViT names its models by size and patch: ViT-L/16 means the "Large" variant with 16x16 input patch size. The three sizes, borrowed from BERT except for the added Huge, have 86M (Base), 307M (Large) and 632M (Huge) parameters. ViT-Base has width D = 768, which for 16-pixel RGB patches happens to equal the length of a flattened patch, so its \mathbf{E} is square.

All ViT pretraining runs at 224 pixels, but the best results come from fine-tuning at 512 and 518 pixels (for L/16 and H/14). Press ViT-H/14 fine-tuned at 518 px: the same model now reads 37 × 37 = 1,369 patch tokens, seven times the tokens of the 224-pixel B/16, and about 1.9 million attention pairs. CLIP, from chapter 1, trained its ViT-L/14 at two input sizes, 224 vs 336 pixels, which is 256 vs 576 patch tokens. The 336-pixel one is the encoder that LLaVA-1.5 later plugs into a language model, and 576 tokens per image is the price it pays.

No built-in eyes, so it needs data

A convolutional network is built on assumptions about images: nearby pixels matter together, and a cat is a cat wherever it appears. ViT has almost none of that. Only the cut into patches (and the stretching of the position table when the resolution changes) knows the image is two-dimensional; everything else, down to which patches are neighbors, is learned. The price shows up on small datasets: trained on ImageNet alone, ViTs land a few percentage points below ResNets of comparable size. Act II is about making a model write, and it starts with a problem you already met in chapter 1: the captions on the web are noisy.

Act IITeaching a language model to see

Web captions are noisy

The text scraped alongside web images is often a file name, an advert or a holiday note, and a model trained on it learns that noise too. BLIP's fix was to let the model clean its own data: a captioner writes a new caption for every web image, and a filter throws out every caption, old or new, that does not match its picture.

The wall: alt-text is not a caption

CLIP and ALIGN showed that scale can paper over noisy text when all you want is a matching score. BLIP wanted more: a model that can also match finely and write captions, trained on the same kind of web data. Its authors put the problem plainly: human-annotated pairs such as COCO are expensive and few, while the web pairs used instead are plentiful, and the alt-texts often do not accurately describe the visual content of the images. Press Raw web pairs: of the five toy images, one is labeled with its file name, one with a sofa advert and one with a holiday date.

Throwing more of this at a model does not fix it; every bad pair teaches a wrong association. What BLIP needed was a way to judge each pair and to replace bad text with good text, at the scale of millions of images, without hiring annotators. The trick is that one model can learn both skills, judging and writing, from the same data.

One model, three jobs

BLIP is a single model with a vision transformer for the image and one text transformer that runs in three modes. Each mode is trained by its own objective, and all three are trained together on every image-text pair. Press the buttons below to see which parts each one uses.

ITC, image-text contrastive. This is the chapter 1 matching game. The text encoder reads the caption on its own, with no access to the image, and its [CLS] vector is scored against the image's vector; matched pairs are pulled together and the batch's other pairs pushed apart. BLIP borrows one refinement from an earlier model, ALBEF: a momentum encoder is introduced to produce features, a slowly updated copy of the model whose soft scores soften the hard "only the diagonal is right" targets, since some of the "negatives" in a web batch are in fact fine captions.

ITM, image-text matching. A yes-or-no question: does this caption belong to this image? Now the text transformer does see the image: a cross-attention layer inserted into every block lets each word look at the image features. The answer comes from an ITM head (a linear layer) on the output of a special [Encode] token. Easy negatives would teach little (a dog photo against a caption about spreadsheets), so BLIP mines hard ones. In the paper's words, "negatives pairs with higher contrastive similarity in a batch are more likely to be selected". ITC's scores make the wrong captions that look most right the likeliest picks, and ITM learns to tell them apart word by word.

LM, language modeling. Write the caption. The same stack, with causal self-attention so each word sees only the words before it (and the image through cross-attention), predicts the caption one token at a time, starting from a [Decode] token.

The sharing is the clever part. Encoding and decoding differ mainly in how words look at each other, bidirectionally or only backward, so BLIP's text encoder and decoder share all parameters except for the SA layers, between the encoder and the decoder: the paper's reason is that the differences between the encoding and decoding tasks are best captured by the SA layers. Cross-attention, feed-forward layers and embeddings are one set of weights, used by every pass that has them. The paper tested the alternatives (Table 3, 14M-image pre-training): sharing everything gives 224M parameters but the worst COCO image retrieval (R@1 59.5, the two jobs fight over one self-attention); sharing nothing costs 361M and scores 60.5; sharing all but self-attention costs 252M and scores best, 60.7.

Captioning and filtering

Here is the bootstrap, which the paper calls CapFilt. First pre-train a BLIP model on the noisy corpus. Then make two copies of it and give each one job, each finetuned on its own with COCO's human-written captions:

  • the captioner is the image-grounded decoder, finetuned with the LM loss. It writes one caption per image for every web image. Press Captioner writes.
  • the filter is the image-grounded encoder, finetuned with ITC and ITM. It judges both the original web text and the new synthetic caption, and a text is considered to be noisy if the ITM head predicts it as unmatched to the image. Press Filter judges.

Watch what the filter does in the toy. The file name, the advert and the holiday note are struck out; so is one synthetic caption, which invented a camper and snow. Web texts that genuinely describe their pictures survive. Press Cleaned set: the kept texts, together with the human-annotated pairs, form a new dataset, and a new model is pre-trained on it from scratch.

Why two separately finetuned copies instead of one model doing both? If the captioner and filter share weights, the filter tends to approve its own twin's mistakes. The paper measures this confirmation bias (Table 4): with shared parameters the filter flags only 8% of synthetic captions as noise, against 25% when decoupled, and the downstream model is worse (COCO image-retrieval R@1 62.2 vs 63.1).

Predict: safe captions or surprising ones?

The captioner can decode in two ways. Beam search keeps the few most probable partial sentences at each step and returns the single most likely caption: fluent, safe, generic. Nucleus sampling draws each word at random from the smallest set of candidates whose probabilities add up to p (BLIP uses p = 0.9): livelier, more specific, and sometimes wrong. The filter catches more of the sampled captions, so nucleus sampling leaves the noisier synthetic data.

Before you press anything: which captioner should train the better model, the safe one or the noisy one? Press Captioner: beam search, then Captioner: nucleus sampling, and compare. In the toy, every beam caption passes the filter, but they are short and alike ("a dog on the beach", "a car parked on the street"); the sampled captions lose one to the filter yet mention frisbees, basil leaves and reflections, and the readout counts almost twice as many different content words. The noisy one wins. The paper's explanation, offered as a hypothesis, is in its section title, "Diversity is Key for Synthetic Captions": sampled captions are more diverse and surprising, so they carry more new information, while beam search tends to produce safe captions that are common in the dataset and adds little the model did not already know. The filter makes the gamble affordable: bold captions that are wrong get thrown away, and bold captions that are right stay.

What cleaning buys

BLIP pre-trains on 14M images (COCO and Visual Genome with human captions; Conceptual Captions, Conceptual 12M and SBU with web text) and on a 129M-image set that adds 115M LAION web images. On the 14M set (Table 1), COCO image retrieval R@1 goes from 60.7 without CapFilt to 61.5 with the filter alone, 62.0 with the captioner alone, and 63.1 with both. The gain survives scale: on the 129M set it goes from 62.0 to 64.3.

Two ideas from this chapter carry forward. Web data can be cleaned by a model trained on it, and BLIP-2, two chapters on, reuses BLIP's captioner for exactly that. And one text transformer can both judge and write, with only its self-attention layers kept separate; BLIP-2 goes one step further: its three objectives share the same weights and differ only in the attention mask.

So far, though, the model only writes captions. It cannot answer a question, follow an instruction or reason about what it sees, because its text side is a BERT-sized transformer (initialized from BERT-base), far smaller than the language models that can hold a conversation. The obvious move is to take a big pretrained language model that already can do all that, and bolt vision onto it without breaking it. That is the next chapter.

Don't break the language model

A large pretrained language model already knows how to write; teaching it to read pictures by retraining it risks erasing what it learned from text. Flamingo freezes the language model and slots new layers in between its old ones, each behind a gate that starts shut, so that on the first training step the whole network is the original language model and vision is dialled in from there.

The wall: a model too valuable to retrain

Flamingo is built on the Chinchilla language models, at 1.4B / 7.1B / 70B parameters, and on an NFNet-F6 image encoder of 435M parameters that was first trained with a CLIP-style contrastive loss (the matching game of the first chapter). Both are frozen. The finished models, Flamingo-3B, Flamingo-9B and Flamingo-80B, have 3.2B / 9.3B / 80B parameters in total.

Why not just train everything? The authors tried. In their ablations (on the smallest model, scored by an overall score that divides each benchmark result by the state of the art and averages, with 4 shots), fine-tuning the pretrained language model instead of freezing it scored 62.7 vs 70.7. They name the effect catastrophic forgetting: the model progressively forgets its pretraining while training on a new objective. So the language model stays frozen, and every new ability has to come from layers added around it.

A gate that starts shut

The new layers are cross-attention layers: the text tokens are the queries, and the visual tokens (blue dots in the figure) are the keys and values. Each is followed by a new feed-forward layer. Both outputs are added to the text token's vector on the residual stream, the running sum that every transformer layer reads from and writes to. In the middle panel, the yellow arrow is one text token's vector, the green arrows are what the new layers add, and the brown arrow is the frozen language model layer that follows. The brown dot is where the frozen language model alone would have put the token.

A freshly initialised layer outputs noise. Added to the residual stream at full strength, that noise knocks the frozen model off course before it has learned anything. Flamingo's fix is to multiply each new layer's output by \tanh(\alpha), where \alpha is one learnable number per layer, initialised to 0. Since \tanh(0) = 0, the new layers contribute nothing at first; in the paper's words, at initialization, the model output matches that of the pretrained LM.

Now answer the figure's question. Press Step 0, gated: both gate dots sit at the origin of the tanh curve, the green arrows have zero length, the model's dot lands on the brown one, and the next-word bars fill the frozen model's brown boxes exactly (the distance between the two distributions, their KL divergence, reads 0.000). Then press Step 0, no gates: the same random layers added at full strength drag the token far away, and the toy model's favourite next word flips from "cat" to "car". Finally drag training progress, or press Train: the gates open, the green arrows grow, and "dog" (the word the image supports) takes over.

The gate matters in practice: removing the tanh gating scored 66.5 vs 70.7 with it. There is also a neat consequence you can read straight off the equation below (it follows from the algebra; the paper does not spell it out). When \alpha = 0, the new layer's weights get zero gradient, because their output is multiplied by \tanh(0) = 0. The gate itself does get a gradient, because the slope of tanh at zero is 1. So the first thing training can change is the gates: vision is let in by opening them, and only then do the new weights start to learn.

The gate scales the new layer's output, not its input, and it sits on the side branch, never on the main path: the residual stream itself is never scaled, and the frozen weights never change. What changes is the input the frozen layer receives. Put \alpha_{\text{xattn}} = \alpha_{\text{dense}} = 0 into the first two lines and they reduce to y \leftarrow y, which is the whole trick.

How many blocks? Inserting one in front of every language model layer is best but expensive, so the bigger models space them out. Flamingo-3B adds one before every LM block (24 layers); Flamingo-9B adds one every 4th block (10 layers over 40); Flamingo-80B adds one every 7th block (12 layers over 80). Even spaced out, the new layers are large: the paper's appendix says the 80B model's blocks account for 10B additional learned parameters.

Any image or video, 64 tokens

The visual tokens \htmlClass{t-blue}{x} need a fixed, small size: every text token cross-attends to them, in every inserted block. But the image encoder produces a grid of features whose size depends on the resolution, and a video produces one grid per frame. The Perceiver Resampler sits between the two and always hands over 64 tokens.

Whatever arrives, 64 vectors leave, and the "64 out" label never changes. Which image does each word see?

Flamingo reads documents where images and text alternate: a photo, a sentence about it, another photo, another sentence. If every text token could cross-attend to every earlier image, a caption could just as easily describe the wrong picture. Flamingo's rule is stricter: a text token cross-attends only to the visual tokens of the last image before it. Text that comes before any image sees none. The bottom panel shows the rule on a short prompt: each text chunk has a green cell under the one image it may see. Press Ablation: all earlier images to see the alternative fill the table's lower triangle.

Earlier images are not forgotten: they still reach a token indirectly, through the frozen self-attention over the earlier text, which did see them. The rule has a practical payoff. The model is trained with up to 5 images per sequence, yet it can use up to 32 pairs of images and texts at test time, since no text token ever faces more than one image. And it scored better: last image only against all earlier images, 70.7 vs 63.5. One training detail: for web pages, the text is mapped to the next image instead of the previous one with probability 1/2, a small data augmentation; at test time the image always comes first.

Few-shot, by prompting

Interleaving is what makes Flamingo usable without fine-tuning. To teach it a new task, write a prompt the way you would for a text model: a few example images each followed by its answer, then the query image. The model continues the pattern. To learn this, Flamingo trained on M3W, text and images scraped in place from 43.3M web pages, mixed with three image-text and video-text pair datasets (1.8 billion ALIGN pairs, 312 million LTIP pairs, 27 million video clips); how the four datasets are weighted and batched is the subject of the training-step chapter. Without M3W, the overall score fell to 53.4 vs 70.7.

The result: across 16 benchmarks, Flamingo-80B beat every earlier zero-shot and few-shot method, and on six tasks it beat models fine-tuned on each task's full training set while seeing only 32 task-specific examples (for example OK-VQA, 57.8 vs 54.4). Backward: why the first step moves only the gates (almost)

Stage 8 ended on a claim: at step 0 the gradient reaches the gates but not the weights behind them. Here is that gradient for a toy block (the new layers only; the toy has no <EOC> row), one bar per group of parameters.

Press Step 0: gates at 0. Three bars are exactly zero: the cross-attention weights, the feed-forward weights and the visual tokens. The last one matters most: the visual tokens are the resampler's output, so on the first step the resampler learns nothing either. The two gate bars are not zero. And the bottom bar, the gradient the block passes down to the frozen layers below it, lands exactly on the brown tick: at step 0 the block is invisible in the backward pass too. Drag the slider: as soon as the gates leave zero, the weight, visual-token and down-pass bars leave zero too, and the gate bars change (they do not simply grow). Press Gates opened and notice the sign: in this toy, opening the cross-attention gate in the positive direction raises the loss, so training would push \alpha_{\text{xattn}} negative instead.

The reason is one line of calculus. Write the first line of the block as y_1 = y + \tanh(\alpha)\,A, where A = \mathrm{Attn}(q{=}y,\ kv{=}x;\ W) is the new layer's output, and let g = \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial y_1 be the gradient arriving from above. The chain rule then gives:

\begin{aligned} \htmlData{step=1, link=weights}{\frac{\partial \htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial \htmlClass{t-green}{W}}} &= \htmlData{step=2, link=gate}{\htmlClass{t-green}{\tanh(\alpha)}}\, \Big(\frac{\partial A}{\partial \htmlClass{t-green}{W}}\Big)^{\!\top} g, \qquad \htmlData{step=3, link=visual}{\frac{\partial \htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial \htmlClass{t-blue}{x}} = \htmlClass{t-green}{\tanh(\alpha)}\, \Big(\frac{\partial A}{\partial \htmlClass{t-blue}{x}}\Big)^{\!\top} g} \\ \htmlData{step=4, link=alpha}{\frac{\partial \htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial \htmlClass{t-green}{\alpha}} = \big(1 - \tanh^2\alpha\big)\; g^{\top} A} \qquad\quad \htmlData{step=5, link=down}{\frac{\partial \htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial \htmlClass{t-yellow}{y}} = g + \htmlClass{t-green}{\tanh(\alpha)}\, \Big(\frac{\partial A}{\partial \htmlClass{t-yellow}{y}}\Big)^{\!\top} g} \end{aligned}

Derived here from the Fig. 4 pseudocode of Flamingo v2 (the paper prints no gradient). The feed-forward line, with \alpha_{\text{dense}} and \mathrm{FFW}(y) in place of \alpha and A, gives the same four formulas without the x term.

  • \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial \htmlClass{t-green}{W}: the new layer's weights. Every path from them to the loss goes through the multiplication by \tanh(\alpha), so the whole gradient carries that factor; at \alpha = 0 it is exactly zero.
  • \htmlClass{t-green}{\tanh(\alpha)}: the gate, the factor on every new-layer term (the whole of the first two formulas, the second term of the fourth).
  • \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial \htmlClass{t-blue}{x}: the visual tokens, and through them the resampler. Same factor, same zero.
  • \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial \htmlClass{t-green}{\alpha}: the gate's own gradient. Its factor is the slope of tanh, which is 1 at zero, times g^{\top}A: how much the layer's current output, noise included, points along the direction that would lower the loss. The gate opens (or goes negative: tanh runs from -1 to 1) according to whether the new layer's output helps.
  • \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial \htmlClass{t-yellow}{y}: what the block passes down to the frozen layers below. At \alpha = 0 it is just g, the frozen model's own gradient.

Now the prediction. Press What if W also started at 0? Then A = 0, so g^{\top}A = 0 and the gate's gradient vanishes too: the gate waits for the weights and the weights wait for the gate, and the cross-attention can never start. (The feed-forward gate still moves, because its own layer is still random.) So the recipe needs both halves: a gate at zero so that the output starts as the language model's, and random weights behind it so that the gate has something to measure. That pairing is our reading of the design; the paper states only the zero gate and cites ReZero for it. How long the gates lead depends on the optimizer (our derivation, not the paper's). Under plain SGD the weights' gradient stays proportional to \tanh(\alpha), so the gates lead for a while. Under AdamW, which divides each step by the gradient's own running size, the gates lead by one step only: from the second step the weights already move at nearly full speed (about 0.74 of the learning rate at step 2 with the default Adam betas, against 1.0 for the gates). Two caveats: Flamingo's warm-up starts at a learning rate of 0, so the literal first update moves nothing, and decoupled weight decay of 0.1 shrinks the weights slightly even when their gradient is zero.

Flamingo showed that a frozen language model can learn to see without forgetting how to write. But its bridge is not small: the 80B model adds the 10B additional learned parameters quoted above, trained on 1536 TPUv4 chips for 15 days. The next chapter asks how small the bridge between a frozen image encoder and a frozen language model can be.

A small bridge between two frozen giants

Flamingo kept its language model frozen, but it still had to thread new cross-attention layers through it, and those layers alone run to billions of trainable parameters. BLIP-2 leaves both giants untouched: a small transformer with a few dozen learned "query" vectors reads the frozen image encoder, and its outputs are handed to a frozen language model as if they were the first words of the prompt.

The wall: the bridge is where the cost goes

By now there are excellent parts on the shelf: image encoders trained the CLIP way, and large language models that already write, reason and answer. Neither has ever met the other. Flamingo, in the previous chapter, showed one way to connect them without breaking the language model: freeze it, and grow gated cross-attention layers inside it, fed by a Perceiver Resampler that squeezes the image into 64 tokens. It works, but the new layers sit inside the LM, and every one of them is trained.

BLIP-2 asks for less. Keep the image encoder frozen and the language model frozen, and train only a small module in between, the Querying Transformer or Q-Former, whose job is to pull out of the image exactly what the language model will need. The hard part is that the frozen LLM has never seen an image, so whatever the bridge produces must already look like something a language model can use. BLIP-2 gets there in two stages: first teach the bridge to extract visual features that are about text (stage 1, with the frozen image encoder only), then teach it to speak to the LLM (stage 2).

Thirty-two questions for the image

The Q-Former is a BERT-sized transformer, initialized from BERT-base with freshly initialized cross-attention layers, 188M parameters in all. Its input is not the image and not a caption but a small set of learned vectors, the queries: 32 of them, each 768 numbers wide. They are parameters, trained like weights, and the same for every image. In each block they talk to each other through self-attention, and in every other transformer block they look at the frozen image features through cross-attention: each query asks the image a question it has learned to ask.

That makes the Q-Former a bottleneck by design. A frozen ViT-L/14 hands over 257 feature vectors of width 1024; the Q-Former hands on 32 of width 768, and that number does not change with the image's resolution. Whatever the language model will learn about the picture must fit through those vectors, which is the point: the training objectives force them to carry what is most relevant to text. (The frozen encoders are CLIP's ViT-L/14 and the larger ViT-g/14 from EVA-CLIP, with the last layer removed; the Q-Former reads the second last layer.)

One transformer, three masks

Stage 1 borrows BLIP's three objectives from two chapters back, with a twist. The Q-Former has an image half (the queries, which see the image) and a text half (which reads a caption), and the two halves share the same self-attention layers. Queries and words sit in one sequence, so whether they can see each other is decided entirely by the self-attention mask. The figure starts on the simplest one.

ITM, image-text matching, uses a bi-directional mask: every query and every word may look at every other. The queries' outputs now mix the image with the caption, and each one goes through a two-class linear classifier; the average of the logits over all queries is the match score. Hard negatives are mined the BLIP way.

ITC, image-text contrastive, is the chapter 1 game again, so the two sides must be encoded apart. Press ITC mask: the uni-modal mask blanks both off-diagonal blocks, queries see only queries and words see only words. If they could see each other, the image side could simply copy the caption, and matching would be trivial; the paper calls this avoiding information leak. The image-text similarity is then the highest of the 32 query-output similarities with the caption's [CLS] vector. Because the image encoder is frozen, more pairs fit on each GPU, so BLIP-2 uses plain in-batch negatives instead of BLIP's momentum queue.

ITG, image-grounded text generation, trains the Q-Former to write the caption. Predict before you press ITG mask: the words must now predict the next word, so which cells have to go dark? In particular, may the queries look at the text?

They may not. Each word may look at all the queries and at the words before it, a triangle in the text block, like any decoder. But the queries must stay blind to the text: a query that had read the whole caption would pass the fourth word to the first. And since the text half has no cross-attention of its own, the only way image information reaches the words is through the queries, which forces them to capture everything the caption needs. The first text token becomes [DEC] to signal the task. The paper calls this a multimodal causal mask, after UniLM.

All three objectives run on the same weights; only the mask changes. In equation form, the mask is added to the attention scores before the softmax:

\htmlData{step=1, link=open}{s_{ij} = \frac{q_i^{\top} k_j}{\sqrt{d}}} \qquad \htmlData{step=2, link=mask}{\htmlClass{t-brown}{M_{ij}} = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if token } i \text{ may look at token } j \\ -\infty & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}} \qquad \htmlData{step=3, link=queries}{h_i = \sum_{j} \operatorname{softmax}_j\big(s_{ij} + \htmlClass{t-brown}{M_{ij}}\big)\, v_j}

Standard masked self-attention. BLIP-2 v3 describes its three masks in prose (Sec. 3.2) and draws them in Fig. 2; it has no numbered equation for them.

  • i, j: positions in the sequence [queries | text], the rows and columns of the grid.
  • q_i, k_j, v_j: the usual attention query, key and value vectors at those positions (not to be confused with the Q-Former's learned queries, which are the tokens in the first block).
  • \htmlClass{t-brown}{M_{ij}}: the mask, a lit cell (0) or a dark one (minus infinity). Adding minus infinity gives that pair zero weight after the softmax, so token i simply cannot see token j.
  • h_i: the new representation of token i, built only from what it was allowed to see.

Stage 2: hand the queries to a frozen LLM

After stage 1 the queries carry text-relevant visual information, but in the Q-Former's own vector space. Stage 2 plugs the Q-Former (with its frozen image encoder still attached) into a frozen language model through one fully connected layer, which linearly projects the query outputs to the LLM's embedding size. The projected vectors are placed in front of the text embeddings, where they act as soft visual prompts: a prefix of "words" that no tokenizer could produce, which the LLM reads like any other input. Press Stage 2: decoder-only LLM.

Written from the prose of BLIP-2 v3 Sec. 3.3 (no numbered equation).

  • \htmlClass{t-green}{Z}: the Q-Former's output, one row per query.

What is learned from depends on the LLM. With a decoder-only model (OPT), the loss is ordinary language modeling: the frozen LLM must generate the text given the visual prompt. With an encoder-decoder model (FlanT5), the text is cut in two; the prompt plus the first part (the prefix) go into the encoder, and the decoder must generate the rest (the suffix). Press Stage 2: encoder-decoder LLM and watch the red loss line move to the suffix. In both cases the image encoder and the LLM stay frozen, so gradients flow back through the LLM only to reach the FC layer and the Q-Former. The paper argues that because the Q-Former already filters the image down to language-relevant information, the LLM has less to adapt to, mitigating the catastrophic forgetting problem that plagues fine-tuning a language model on images.

What it costs, and what it buys

BLIP-2 pre-trains on the same 129M images as BLIP, cleaned by BLIP's own CapFilt trick, turned up: BLIP's large captioner writes 10 captions for each web image, a CLIP ViT-L/14 ranks them together with the original web caption, and the top-two captions per image are kept. Stage 1 runs 250k steps and stage 2 80k. Because both giants are frozen, the largest model trains on a single 16-A100(40G) machine in less than 6 days for stage 1 and less than 3 days for stage 2.

The headline comparison is with Flamingo80B. On zero-shot VQAv2 (test-dev), BLIP-2's largest model scores 65.0 against Flamingo80B's 56.3, a gain of 8.7 points. The paper writes this as "8.7%", but it is an absolute difference in accuracy points, not a relative one.

The paper also says it does this with 54x fewer trainable parameters. That ratio divides Flamingo80B's 10.2B trainable parameters by the Q-Former's 188M. Yet the paper's own Table 2 lists 103M to 108M trainable parameters for its BLIP-2 models, and dividing by 108M instead would give about 94x. The paper does not reconcile the two counts; one plausible reading (ours, not the paper's) is that 188M counts the whole Q-Former including its text half, which stage 2 does not use. Either way the right panel makes the point that matters: by either count BLIP-2 trains far fewer parameters than Flamingo80B, and both train a small fraction of what they run.

Frozen giants have limits too. On OK-VQA, whose questions need outside knowledge, Flamingo80B still leads, 50.6 to 45.9; the paper's explanation is that Flamingo's much larger frozen language model simply knows more. The bridge can only deliver the picture; the facts have to be in the LLM already.

BLIP-2 still needs a 188M-parameter bridge, three pre-training objectives and two stages, and it was pre-trained only on images with captions, never on conversations. The next chapter goes the other way on both counts: the simplest possible bridge, a single linear layer, and all the effort spent on the data, conversations written for the purpose.

Act IIIFrom describing to conversing

Chat needs conversations to learn from

Flamingo and BLIP-2 learned from captions and web pages, not from instructions. Flamingo can be prompted into multi-image dialogues, but in LLaVA's side-by-side tests BLIP-2 and OpenFlamingo (an open-source version of Flamingo) mostly describe the scene when given an open request ("What is unusual about this image?", "Can you explain this meme in detail?"). LLaVA's bet was that the bridge can be almost trivially simple if the training data is the right kind: conversations about images, written for it by a language model that was never shown the pictures.

The wall: nobody had written the conversations

By 2023 the web had offered up billions of image-caption pairs, and the previous chapters showed how to learn from them. A caption, though, is one flat sentence. It never asks a follow-up question, never explains its reasoning, never answers "why". A model trained only on captions learns to caption. What a visual assistant needs is data in the shape of the task: a person asks something about an image, an assistant answers, and the exchange goes on. Collecting that from human annotators at scale is slow and expensive, so almost none existed.

A model that cannot see writes the data

LLaVA's trick was to have GPT-4, used as a text-only model, write the conversations. GPT-4 never looks at the pixels; as the paper's caption puts it, the visual image is not used to prompt GPT. Instead each COCO image is described to it in words, two ways: the human-written captions COCO already has, and the list of labeled bounding boxes, each an object name with four normalized coordinates. From that text, GPT-4 invents what an assistant who could see the image would say. For each kind of output the authors wrote a few examples by hand, which the paper calls the only human annotations we have during data collection, and passed them to GPT-4 as in-context demonstrations.

The simplest possible bridge

With the data taken care of, the connector could be nearly trivial. LLaVA takes the frozen CLIP ViT-L/14 encoder from the first chapter, reads out its grid of patch features, and multiplies every patch vector by one learned matrix. That is the whole bridge, a simple linear layer, and it outputs vectors the same size as the language model's word embeddings, so to the language model each patch looks like one more word:

One training step, end to end

Under their different connectors, most models from here on train the same way: turn the image into tokens, put them in one sequence with the text, and ask the language model to predict the next token. What decides whether that works is the plumbing, which this chapter walks through one stage at a time: which examples are drawn, how they are spliced and batched, which tokens are graded, and which weights are allowed to move.

Two ways to learn from pictures and words

Press A. That is the step from the first chapter: two encoders run side by side, one on the images and one on the captions, and the loss is a table of every image against every caption. Nothing is generated; the model only learns to say which caption goes with which photo. The batch is doing real work here, because every other caption in it serves as a negative. CLIP's batch of 32,768 is part of the method, not just an efficiency choice.

Now press 1 Sample and step through with Next. The generative step is different in kind. Each example becomes one sequence, the language model reads it, and the loss asks it to write the right words. Examples in a batch never see each other: padded rows are independent, and packed rows are masked apart. Apart from how the loss is averaged (see the Molmo note below), the batch size mainly sets how noisy each gradient is and how busy the GPUs are. Everything on the rest of this page, from LLaVA to Qwen3-VL, trains with this second kind of step.

Where the examples come from

A step starts by drawing examples, and even that simple act is a design choice. LLaVA says only that its three kinds of instruction data are uniformly sampled in training, not whether per kind or per example. LLaVA-1.5 concatenates its ten splits, which are then sampled with the same probability; after concatenation that makes every example equally likely, so the big splits dominate. Molmo samples each dataset at a rate proportional to the square root of their size, then adjusts by hand (down-weighting some very large synthetic sets, up-weighting pointing data); the square root alone gives small datasets a larger share than their size would.

Flamingo does not mix inside a batch at all: each update takes a fixed-size batch from every dataset, and the mixture is written into the loss:

\htmlData{step=4, link=sum}{\sum_{m=1}^{M}} \htmlData{step=3, link=lambda}{\lambda_m} \cdot \htmlData{step=1, link=dataset}{\mathbb{E}_{(x, y)\sim \mathcal{D}_m}} \Big[ \htmlData{step=2, link=nll}{\htmlClass{t-red}{-\sum_{\ell=1}^{L} \log p(y_\ell \mid y_{<\ell}, x_{\le \ell})}} \Big]

Eq. 2 in Flamingo v2.

  • \mathcal{D}_m: the m-th of M = 4 datasets (interleaved web pages, two sets of image-text pairs, video-text pairs), the four rows in the lower half of stage 1.
  • The bracket: the usual next-token loss on one example's text, given its earlier text y_{<\ell} and the images before it x_{\le \ell} (the red bars).
  • \lambda_m: how much that dataset's loss counts: 1.0 / 0.2 / 0.2 / 0.03. The paper calls tuning them key to performance.
  • \sum_m: the weighted losses are added into one objective, and one update follows.

In practice the outer sum is a loop: We accumulate gradients over all datasets, one batch from each, before a single optimizer update. In the paper's ablation setup, 1 million gradient computations therefore make only 250,000 gradient updates. The obvious alternative, round-robin, is (in the words of Flamingo's appendix, which compares the same strategies for its contrastive encoder) to alternate batches of each dataset, updating the parameters on each batch. It sounds equivalent, but it scores 70.7 vs 62.9 in Flamingo's ablation, in favour of accumulating. The paper does not say why. One plausible reading (ours): when every update sees all the datasets at once, no single dataset gets to pull the weights its own way for a whole update.

From pixels and words to one sequence

Stages 2 to 4 turn one example into one row. The image side is the previous chapter's connector, run at LLaVA-1.5's size: a 336-pixel square, 14-pixel patches, 576 vectors out of the frozen encoder, each mapped by the connector to the LLM's embedding width. The text side is plain formatting: LLaVA's Table 2 wraps the conversation in a system message and alternating Human: / Assistant: turns, each closed by a <STOP> token (###), with an <image> placeholder in the first human turn. LLaVA's Eq. 2 even randomizes whether the placeholder comes before or after the first question; example A in stage 3 has it after.

Splicing is the moment the two modalities meet: the placeholder is replaced by the visual tokens, and from then on the language model just sees a sequence of vectors. With real-size images the picture dominates that sequence: our three short examples become 603, 617 and 599 tokens long, of which 576 each are the image.

Forming the batch

GPUs process a batch as one rectangular tensor, but the examples have different lengths. Press 5a: the simplest fix pads every row with dummy tokens up to the longest, and a pad mask keeps real tokens from attending to them. Guess how much of the batch is filler, then press 5b. In the toy it is 32 of 150 cells, 21%: every one of those cells is computed on and then thrown away.

Packing removes the filler: lay the examples end to end, then give the attention a block-diagonal mask so that each example can only see its own earlier tokens, as if it were alone. The same three examples fit in 118 cells, and the 32 free cells can take part of the next example. This general recipe is common practice across training code; the papers on this page describe their own variants of it.

At real scale the picture changes in an instructive way. With 576-token images, the same three examples waste only 1.7% to padding, because the image makes every row roughly the same length. Padding bites when lengths really spread. LLaVA-1.5 noticed that its text-only conversations are often longer than its image conversations, so it draws each batch from a single modality, which speeds up the training by 25%. It also shrinks the number of short sequences in the first place: QA pairs from the same training image are merged into a single conversation.

Press 5c for the most interesting variant. Many datasets ask several questions about the same image, and training on each question separately encodes that image again every time. Molmo puts the image once and every question after it, and masks attention so that each annotation's tokens attend to the image tokens, each other, but not to tokens from different annotations. The paper calls this equivalent to training on individual image-text pairs, and it ends up reducing the number of processed images by two-thirds and shortening training time by over half, at the price of only a 25% increase in sequence length for Molmo's data mix (Molmo caps sequences at 2304 tokens). Qwen2-VL, whose images turn into a varying number of tokens (a 224-pixel image becomes 66 tokens), says that images of varying resolutions are packed into a single sequence; the paper states this for inference, in its description of the vision encoder (packing image patches, in the style of NaViT), not for batching the language model's training examples.

Which tokens are graded

Press 6a. The language model reads the row in one pass and, at every position, predicts the next token; causal attention guarantees that no position can peek at its own answer. So every position followed by a word comes with a target, the next token along; positions followed by a visual token have none. Should all of them count? Make your guess, then press 6b.

They should not. The model is being trained as the assistant, so it is graded only on what the assistant says: LLaVA is trained to predict the assistant answers and where to stop. Grade it on the human's words too, and it would spend capacity learning to write questions; the visual tokens are never targets, since they are continuous vectors with no vocabulary entry to predict (the last visual position in example A is graded only because the token it predicts is the ### after the image). Look closely at the paper's Table 2 and you find one surprise: every <STOP> is marked as a target, including the ones that close the system message and the human's turns. The model learns where each turn ends, but never how the human fills it. The ignored positions (padding aside) still matter as context; they just contribute no loss. Over the whole toy batch, 47 of 150 cells (31%) carry loss; keep the same toy text but give each image its real 576 visual tokens and it is 2.5%. The exact figure depends on the toy's short answers, but the direction holds: most of what the model reads, it is never graded on.

Written as a probability, the objective is the chance of the answer given the image and the instructions, built one answer token at a time:

One subtle trap sits in how this loss is averaged. A batch is split across many GPUs, and if each GPU divides its summed loss by its own count of graded tokens, a GPU that happened to get short answers (like example C's single word) weights each of them more heavily. Molmo divides by the average count across all devices instead, and reports that captioning performance can drop by 0.5-1 points without this fix.

Which weights move

The backward pass sends the gradient of that loss back through everything that produced it, and the training recipe decides which weights actually change. Press 7a. In LLaVA's stage 1 both the encoder and the language model are frozen and only the connector \htmlClass{t-green}{W} learns, on 595K image-text pairs turned into one-turn chats (describe the image; answer: its caption), for 1 epoch with a learning rate of 2e-3 and a batch size of 128. Note what the gradient has to do: the loss sits at the top of the language model, and the connector sits below it, so the gradient must pass through every frozen LLM layer on its way down. Frozen means the weights do not change, not that the gradient stops. Below the connector nothing trains, so the encoder needs no backward pass at all; Flamingo makes the same point for its own frozen encoder: no backpropagation is performed through the frozen Vision Encoder.

Press 7b. Stage 2 unfreezes the language model and trains it together with the connector on the instruction data, for 3 epochs, with a learning rate of 2e-5 and a batch size of 32; the learning rate is a hundred times smaller than in stage 1. Skipping stage 1 was not free for LLaVA: on ScienceQA (fine-tuned on ScienceQA itself for 12 epochs), training without it reaches 85.81% against 90.92% with it. The lesson is not universal: Molmo, which pre-trains all of its weights on its dense captions, writes We find this step unnecessary when pre-training on PixMo-Cap. LLaVA-1.5 keeps the same two stages, with 558K pairs for the first and its 665K mixture for the second, but its connector is the 2-layer MLP drawn in stage 2 rather than the original single matrix \htmlClass{t-green}{W}, and its own schedule is 1 epoch per stage (learning rate 1e-3 and batch 256, then 2e-5 and batch 128).

That is the whole loop: sample, tokenize, splice, batch, mask, and send the gradient back to whatever is allowed to learn. Notice, though, that every image in this chapter's pipeline was a small square, 336 pixels on a side at most. A photo of a dog survives that. A page of a contract, a dense chart or a phone screenshot does not: its words shrink to a few pixels. Act IV is about what happens when the image has to be read, not just recognized.

Act IVSeeing sharply and pointing

A fixed small square cannot read a page

Every model so far squeezes each picture into one small square before its encoder sees it: CLIP's ViT-L/14 encoder comes in 224 vs 336 pixel versions, LLaVA-1.5 uses 336 and Qwen-VL 448. That is plenty for a photo of a dog and hopeless for a page of text, where the letters shrink to a pixel or two.

The wall: shrinking the page

Start where the last act left off. LLaVA-1.5 feeds its language model a 336-pixel square, chosen because it is the highest resolution available for CLIP, and that square becomes 576 tokens. The figure opens there, on a report page. Its body text is set in 16-pixel type; squashed into 336 × 336 it arrives 3.8 pixels tall, and the zoom panel shows what that means: grey smudges where words used to be. Press 224 square and it is worse (2.6 pixels). Try the wide screenshot too: a square also throws away the aspect ratio, so the code is squeezed 1.8 times harder across than down.

Qwen-VL's first move was the obvious one. Its authors increase the input resolution of the visual encoder from 224 x 224 to 448 x 448, reducing the information loss caused by image down-sampling. Doubling the side quadruples the patches: the encoder (initialized from OpenCLIP ViT-bigG) now runs at 448x448, ViT sequence length 1024. To keep the language model's share small, Qwen-VL adds an adapter in the spirit of BLIP-2's query tokens: a single-layer cross-attention module initialized randomly, whose 256 learned queries read the patch features and compresses the visual feature sequence to a fixed length of 256. Because compression scrambles where things were, 2D absolute positional encodings are added inside that cross-attention. Press 448 square (Qwen-VL): the type now arrives 5.1 pixels tall, better but still blurry, for about the same number of tokens as the 224 square: 258 against 256, because Qwen-VL brackets its 256 outputs with the two markers <img> and </img>.

Training runs in three stages, much like the recipes of the previous chapter: first at 224x224, ViT sequence length 256 with the language model frozen, then at 448 with nothing frozen on a multi-task mix, then instruction tuning for chat with the ViT frozen. On DocVQA, a benchmark of questions about scanned documents, Qwen-VL scored 65.1. A bigger square helps, but any fixed square is too small for some image and too big for another.

Tiling: more squares

A common fix, used in different forms by LLaVA-1.5-HD, Idefics2 and Molmo, keeps the encoder at its native size and gives it more squares. LLaVA-1.5-HD describes it as dividing the image into smaller image patches of the resolution that the vision encoder is originally trained for, encoding each crop separately and stitching the features back into one large map. Seen crop by crop, the model loses the big picture, so a shrunken copy of the whole image comes along to provide the LLM with the global context and to reduce the artifact of the split-encode-merge operation. Idefics2 turns each image into 4 crops and the original image, at a cost in visual tokens per image of 64 / 320 without and with splitting. In Molmo, the grid is chosen to require the least amount of up-scaling, and neighboring crops overlap so that patches at a seam still see their neighbors.

Press Tiles + thumbnail on the document page. The toy picks a 2 × 3 grid of 224-pixel crops, and the type now arrives 7.2 pixels tall: most words are legible in the zoom. The bill is 1,774 tokens (the grid's patches, one row-end token per row, and the thumbnail's 256). Tiling works, but it is a patch on a fixed-size encoder: the crops come in a few fixed shapes, the image still gets resized to fit them, and because the crop count is capped, a big screenshot is still shrunk to about a third of its width (switch to it: the code arrives 5.3 pixels tall).

Native resolution: let the image keep its size

Qwen2-VL removes the square altogether. The model can process images of any resolution, dynamically converting them into a variable number of visual tokens, using two changes to the recipe of chapter 3.

First, the ViT's position table goes. A learned table has one row per slot and must be interpolated for every new grid size; Qwen2-VL modified the ViT by removing the original absolute position embeddings and introducing 2D-RoPE, a rotary position code computed from each patch's row and column, so any grid size works without a table. Second, to pay for the extra patches, a small MLP after the ViT is used to compress adjacent 2x2 tokens into a single token. With the ViT's patch_size=14, one language-model token now stands for a 28 × 28 = 784-pixel square of the image. The paper's own example: a 224x224 image will be compressed to 66 tokens, which is 16 × 16 = 256 patches, merged four to one into 64, plus two marker tokens around them. As a formula:

T = \htmlData{step=1, link=patch}{\frac{\htmlClass{t-blue}{H'}}{14}\cdot\frac{\htmlClass{t-blue}{W'}}{14}} \;\htmlData{step=2, link=merge}{\cdot\;\frac{1}{2 \times 2}} \;\htmlData{step=3, link=special}{+\;\htmlClass{t-yellow}{2}} \qquad \htmlData{step=4, link=size}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{H'} \approx H,\;\; \htmlClass{t-blue}{W'} \approx W}

Positions in two dimensions, and in time: M-RoPE

The language model has the same kind of position problem the ViT had. With ordinary 1D RoPE every token gets the next integer, so the document page above at native size, a 35 × 50 grid of image tokens, is just 1,750 consecutive positions: the token directly below another sits 35 positions away, and a big image pushes the text after it far down the number line. Qwen2-VL's Multimodal Rotary Position Embedding (M-RoPE) splits the rotary embedding into temporal, height, and width parts, so each token carries three ids (t, h, w).

The rule has three parts. For text, the three components utilize identical position IDs, so a text-only prompt is numbered exactly as under 1D RoPE and the language model's reading of text is untouched. For an image, t stays constant across all its tokens, while h and w are the token's row and column. For a video, t goes up by one per frame. And across modalities, position numbering for each modality is initialized by incrementing the maximum position ID of the preceding modality by one. The paper's figure makes "maximum" concrete: after a video of three frames, each 3 × 4 tokens, numbered from 0, the text continues at (4,4,4), then (5,5,5), (6,6,6). The largest component in the block was a width id of 3, so text resumes at 4.

\begin{aligned} &\htmlData{step=1, link=text}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{\text{text token } n}:\quad (n,\; n,\; n)} \\ &\htmlData{step=2, link=img}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{\text{image token at row } r,\ \text{column } c}:\quad (s,\; s + r,\; s + c)} \\ &\htmlData{step=3, link=video}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{\text{video token in frame } f}:\quad (s + f,\; s + r,\; s + c)} \\ &\htmlData{step=4, link=next}{\text{next block starts at } \max(t, h, w) + 1} \end{aligned}

Not an equation in the paper: the rule stated in Qwen2-VL v2, Sec. 2.1, and drawn in its M-RoPE figure.

  • (t, h, w): the three ids. The rotary embedding's dimensions are split into three groups, and each group is rotated by one of the three ids instead of all by the same one.
  • n: a text token's ordinary running position, used for all three parts.
  • s: the first id of a visual block, one past the largest id before it. The tokens are the merged 28-pixel tokens, rows and columns counted from 0.
  • f: the frame number within a video.
  • \max(t, h, w) + 1: where the next block, text or visual, starts: one past the largest id used anywhere in the block, which is the block's start plus its longest side.

So check the prediction: the image starts at 3, its largest id is a width of 3 + 3 = 6, and "The" gets 7 under M-RoPE, not 15. The bar under the tokens shows the whole budget: under 1D RoPE the sequence uses ids 0 to 18, under M-RoPE only 0 to 10, because a visual block uses only as many ids as its longest side. Press Add a short video and the gap widens: 35 versus 18. The same holds for real videos, which Qwen2-VL samples at two frames per second and encodes with a 3D convolution of depth two, so that pairs of frames become one slice of tokens (for consistency each image is treated as two identical frames).

Small position ids are the point: the paper credits M-RoPE with letting the model run on longer inputs than it was trained on. Trained with at most 16384 tokens per video, it keeps working on video inputs up to 80K tokens. In the paper's ablation (a smaller model, 1D RoPE vs M-RoPE), the switch moves image scores such as MathVista 39.2 vs 43.4; MMB 58.6 vs 60.6 and video scores such as NextQA 43.9 vs 46.0; STAR 55.5 vs 57.9; several document benchmarks in the same table barely move.

Say where, not just what

Every model so far answers in words: it can say "a dog", but not which pixels are the dog. The fix in this chapter is almost embarrassingly simple: a box is four numbers, numbers can be written as text, and a language model already knows how to write text.

The wall: an answer with no address

Ask a LLaVA-style model "where is the ball?" and the best it can do is "at the bottom, next to the dog". That is fine for chat, but useless for a robot arm, a document parser that must say which box on a form holds the date, or a checker that wants to verify an answer against the image. Classic detectors do give coordinates, but through a separate head with its own loss, one head per task. The question this chapter answers: can the language model's ordinary next-token output carry a location?

A box is four numbers, and numbers are text

Qwen-VL's answer (the same model whose resolution work was the previous chapter) is to just write them. Each box coordinate is normalised to the range [0, 1000) and written in the string form (X_topleft,Y_topleft),(X_bottomright,Y_bottomright), between <box> and </box>. The phrase the box belongs to goes between <ref> and </ref>. A real training example from the paper's appendix reads <ref>the ear on a giraffe</ref><box>(176,106),(232,160)</box>. The digits are tokenised as ordinary text; the coordinates need no new vocabulary (only the markers <box>, </box>, <ref> and </ref> are added). Press Box the dog and read the first line: the model's answer is a short sentence of digits and brackets.

Why normalise? Because images come in every size and the model never sees them at that size (Qwen-VL resizes every image to 448 × 448), so a box in the original image's pixels would not match what the model sees. Dividing by the width and height turns "pixel 137 of 640" into "21.4% of the way across" (Qwen-VL writes 214), which means the same thing whatever the resolution; the thousandfold scale just makes it an integer. Trained on grounding data in this form, Qwen-VL reaches 89.36 / 92.26 / 85.34 on RefCOCO val / testA / testB, the task of boxing the object a sentence describes.

How precise is a token?

Writing a coordinate as an integer out of a thousand throws information away: every position inside one thousandth of the image is written the same way. How much does that cost? Drag the bins slider down, or press Coarse: 8 bins. The brown grid is the set of cells the image is now divided into; the yellow box can sit anywhere, but its written form can only name a cell, and the red dashed box, decoded back from the tokens, snaps to cell centres. With B bins across an image of width W, the worst error is half a cell:

k = \Big\lfloor \frac{x}{W}\,B \Big\rfloor, \qquad \hat x = \Big(k + \tfrac12\Big)\frac{W}{B}, \qquad |x - \hat x| \le \frac{W}{2B}.

Not from any of the papers: the bin rule written out; numbering bins from zero and decoding at the centre are the toy's conventions.

At 8 bins on the toy's 640-pixel width that is 40 px, a box that visibly misses the dog. At 1,000 bins it is 0.32 px, and even on a 3840-pixel-wide 4K frame only 1.92 px. None of the papers says why it chose about a thousand levels per axis, but the numbers make it a comfortable choice: fine enough to be invisible and, for Florence-2's one token per level, a small addition to the vocabulary.

Florence-2: every vision task is a sentence

Microsoft's Florence-2 takes the idea to its end. Instead of digits, it creates 1,000 bins and adds one new location token per bin to the tokenizer's vocabulary. The main text never spells a token out, but the appendix prompts (Fig. 16) print them as <loc_586> (angle brackets and an underscore), so that is the form the second line uses. A box is four location tokens (top-left corner, then bottom-right); a text region is a quadrilateral of eight, corners clockwise from the top-left; a segmentation mask is a polygon of 2n tokens for n vertices, also clockwise. Press Box the sign: with the slider at 1,000 the integers on the first two lines are identical, and only the spelling differs. Qwen-VL writes the number with digit tokens the model already had; Florence-2 gives each bin its own token.

With coordinates as tokens, every task becomes the same task. Captioning is "image + caption prompt → text". Detection is "image + prompt → each object's label with its four location tokens". Grounding, OCR with regions and referring segmentation are the same with quads or polygons. Molmo: point, then count

A box takes four numbers and a careful annotator. Molmo (from the Allen Institute for AI) asks for less: a single point, which a person can click in a second. Points pay off in an unexpected place: counting. Press Point, then count. Instead of blurting "3", Molmo first writes one numbered point per apple, in a fixed order (top to bottom, then left to right), and the count is just the last index it wrote. It is chain-of-thought, but the "thoughts" are locations you can check against the image. Molmo's ablation shows the order matters: point-then-count scores 89.4/86.3 (CountBenchQA / PixMo-Count), against 87.9/80.2 for answering with the count alone, and 81.5/77.6 for giving the count first and pointing afterwards.

Now predict before you press. Florence-2 adds special location tokens (an idea it credits to earlier sequence models such as Pix2Seq, Unified-IO and OFA); Qwen-VL and Molmo write plain digits. Press Molmo's answer. Plain digits win on both benchmarks, 89.4/86.3 against 85.8/80.9. A plausible reading, not one the paper tests: a thousand fresh tokens start with random embeddings and must each be learned from the grounding data alone, while digits arrive already ordered (the model knows 41 is next to 42) from all of its text pretraining. Keep the scope in mind, too: this is one ablation, on points and counting in one model family, not a verdict on Florence-2's boxes.

Look back at what this chapter did: a choice (digits or new tokens), a knob (how many bins), and one small experiment that settled the choice for one setting. Most of the choices in the earlier chapters (which language model, which encoder, which connector, how many image tokens, whose captions) never got even that. Act V is about the two papers that went back and ran the experiments.

Act VWhat actually matters

Most design choices were never tested

By 2024 every VLM was a stack of choices: which language model, which vision encoder, which connector, how many image tokens, whose captions. Most were inherited from the previous paper rather than tested, so two teams, Idefics2 (Hugging Face) and Molmo (Allen Institute for AI), went back and changed one thing at a time.

How to read an ablation

An ablation trains the same model twice with one thing changed and compares a score. Idefics2's runs are deliberately cheap: 6,000 steps each, scored by the average of four-shot results on four benchmarks (VQAv2, TextVQA, OKVQA and COCO captioning). Two cautions follow. A short run measures how fast a choice learns, not where it ends up. And the average mixes accuracies with a COCO captioning score on a different scale, so only differences within one table mean anything. Molmo scores its ablations with two numbers: a caption-quality metric right after pre-training, and the average over its 11 benchmarks after fine-tuning.

Backbones: the language model moved the needle more

Start on the Backbones tab. Holding the vision encoder fixed and swapping Llama-1-7B for Mistral-7B raises the average from 62.5 to 67.6. Holding the language model fixed and swapping the vision encoder moves it less, from 57.4 to at most 60.7. The paper's Finding 1: For a fixed number of parameters, the quality of the language model backbone has a higher impact on the performance of the final VLM than the quality of the vision backbone.

Be careful with that conclusion; the note under the chart says why. The two swaps are not the same size of step: the language models are 35.1% vs 60.1% on MMLU, a huge jump, while the encoders are 78.0% vs 83.2% on ImageNet. And the best encoder also ran at a higher resolution (384 against 224 pixels), which the paper itself credits for part of its TextVQA gain. So the evidence says "a much better LM helped more than a somewhat better encoder at a different resolution", which is weaker than the finding's wording. One more surprise: EVA-CLIP-5B, roughly ten times SigLIP's size, is no better; the authors suggest it may be under-trained.

Claim card: the language model matters more than the vision encoder (Idefics2, Finding 1)

Claim. At a fixed parameter count, a better language model helps a VLM more than a better vision encoder.

How it was measured. One-swap runs under the cross-attention architecture, each 6,000 steps, scored by the four-benchmark 4-shot average. Held fixed, in the paper's words: "We fix the size of the pretrained backbones, the data used for multimodal pre-training, and the number of training updates". Not held fixed: the vision encoder's size (CLIP-ViT-H, EVA-CLIP-5B and SigLIP-SO400M differ; the paper calls EVA-CLIP-5B ten times bigger in parameter counts than SigLIP-SO400M), how much better each replacement is on its own (MMLU 35.1% vs 60.1%, ImageNet 78.0% vs 83.2%) and the encoder's input resolution (224 vs 384).

Result. The LM swap yields a boost of 5.1 (62.5 to 67.6); the encoder swap yields a 3.3 increase (57.4 to 60.7).

What would have falsified it. The encoder swap giving the larger gain. Our reading: the design cannot tell "the LM matters more" from "this LM step was bigger", because the two steps are not matched in size; a fair test would compare swaps of equal standalone improvement at one resolution.

Cross-attention or one long sequence?

Two families of models have met on this page. Flamingo keeps the language model's sequence text-only and adds cross-attention layers that look at the image. LLaVA and its descendants simply put the image tokens into the sequence and let the LM's own self-attention do the work; Idefics2 calls this fully autoregressive. Which is better? Predict before you press: with both backbones frozen, cross-attention wins. What happens when the backbones are allowed to train?

Press Cross-attention or one sequence. Frozen (brown), cross-attention leads, 66.7 against 60.3, which makes sense: its new layers are extra trainable capacity, while the fully autoregressive model can only adapt through its connector. Trained (yellow), the order flips: fully autoregressive reaches 69.5 against 67.3. Finding 2, in the paper's words: The cross-attention architecture performs better than the fully autoregressive one when unimodal pre-trained backbones are kept frozen. However, when training the unimodal backbones, the fully autoregressive architecture outperforms the cross-attention one, even though the latter has more parameters.

"Allowed to train" hides an engineering detail. Unfreezing everything made the fully autoregressive runs diverge, and lowering the learning rate did not fix it. What worked was LoRA: the pretrained weights stay fixed and each gets a small trainable low-rank correction, which can be merged back in afterwards at no inference cost. One more honesty note: the paper's prose says frozen cross-attention leads by 7 points and that LoRA adds 12.9 points to the fully autoregressive model, but its own table gives 66.7 − 60.3 = 6.4 and 69.5 − 60.3 = 9.2 (only its 0.6 for cross-attention matches). The chart plots the table.

Claim card: which architecture wins depends on what is allowed to train (Idefics2, Finding 2)

Claim. Cross-attention beats one-sequence ("fully autoregressive") when the pretrained backbones are frozen; once they train, the one-sequence model wins despite having fewer parameters.

How it was measured. The same 6,000 steps protocol and four-benchmark average, both architectures, two training regimes. Frozen, only approximately 15% for the fully autoregressive architecture and 25% for the cross-attention of the parameters train. "Trained" means LoRA, used to adapt the pre-trained parameters while using standard full fine-tuning for the newly initialized ones, because full unfreezing diverged. Not held fixed: the data per step, since the cross-attention architecture feeds a single token per image to the language model, against 64 for the fully autoregressive architecture, so at the same batch and sequence length the two see different numbers of images and text tokens (the appendix names this and plots both against images and tokens seen, with the fully autoregressive model ahead throughout); and parameter count (cross-attention trains 1.3B more (2B trainable); 10% more flops).

Result. Frozen: 66.7 (cross-attention) vs 60.3. LoRA: 67.3 vs 69.5. The order flips.

What would have falsified it. Cross-attention still ahead, or tied, under LoRA. The flip is 2.2 points, with no repeats or error bars reported, so it is a clear direction rather than a precise margin.

The connector does matter

A common reading of LLaVA was that the connector hardly matters: a linear layer is enough. Idefics2's appendix does not test LLaVA's layer (which maps each patch separately); it compares ways of squeezing the patch sequence into a few tokens, and the Connector tab shows a wide spread: a linear layer over the flattened sequence scores 44.5, MapNet (a small learned mapping network) 51.8, and the Perceiver resampler 60.3. What does not matter is the Perceiver's depth: one, three or twelve layers all land within a point. (The appendix does not state the training setup. The MapNet and Perceiver values, 51.8 and 60.3, equal two frozen-backbone rows of the architecture table, which suggests they are the same runs; the paper does not say.) So the fair summary is: what the connector does matters; how big it is, much less.

Fewer image tokens, no loss

The Perceiver is also a compressor: it turns the encoder's patch grid into a fixed number of output tokens (Flamingo's resampler from Act II). Replacing no pooling with a Perceiver took each image from 729 to 64 tokens and raised the average by 8.5 points. Press 64 or 128 tokens: doubling the Perceiver's output to 128 tokens scores 71.2 against 71.7 for 64. For comparison, the other VLMs of the time used 576 (DeepSeek-VL) to 2890 (SPHINX-2k) tokens per image. For these benchmarks, most of those tokens were not buying anything.

Claim card: learned pooling to 64 tokens costs nothing (Idefics2, Finding 4)

Claim. Squeezing each image into a few learned tokens with a Perceiver both cuts compute and raises the score.

How it was measured. Two separate comparisons. (a) In the architecture table, the frozen fully autoregressive model with and without a Perceiver: 51.8 vs 60.3, which is where the paper's 8.5 points for 729 to 64 tokens comes from. (b) A separate pooling table, from a different fully autoregressive setup (its scores run about ten points higher), with 64 vs 128 Perceiver outputs. What would have falsified it. The 729-token run scoring higher, or 128 tokens clearly beating 64. The paper itself names the limit, "in a hypothetical scenario of infinite training on unlimited data, performance might eventually improve". Add that these are short runs and that only one of the four benchmarks, TextVQA, is about reading text, which is exactly where the next section finds that more tokens pay.

Split the image when you need to read

The exception is text. Idefics2 can also cut an image into four crops plus the whole image, each encoded to 64 tokens, 64 / 320 tokens per image without or with splitting. Press Image splitting: the same weights read better with the extra tokens, TextVQA 70.4 → 73.0 and DocVQA 67.3 → 74.0, while reasoning (MMMU) and general questions (MMBench) do not move. It is the resolution story of Act IV again: detail costs tokens, and only some questions need the detail. The resulting recipe is Idefics2 itself: SigLIP-SO400M + Mistral-7B-v0.1, 8 billion parameters, fully autoregressive, 64 visual tokens per image (320 with splitting), max image resolution 980 pixels.

Molmo: captions from people's voices

Molmo's question is about data. The strongest open models of 2024 were trained on detailed captions written by GPT-4V or GPT-4o, which, the authors argue, makes them distillations of closed models. Molmo's datasets were all collected without the use of external VLMs; "without any reliance on synthetic data from other VLMs, including proprietary ones". The trick for getting long, detailed captions from people was to have them talk: annotators described each image aloud for a minute or more, the audio was transcribed, and a text-only LLM cleaned the transcript. PixMo-Cap has 712k distinct images with 1.3M transcripts and captions, and its captions average 196 words vs 11 words vs 37 words (PixMo-Cap vs COCO vs Localized Narratives).

Did speaking beat distilling? Press Whose captions. Against ShareGPT4V+o, popular sets of GPT-4V and GPT-4o captions, PixMo-Cap wins clearly (cap F1 36.3 vs 54.1). But the fair test is the one where the images are the same: Molmo also had GPT-4o caption the PixMo images, and the result is a tie. Human captions win slightly on cap F1, GPT-4o captions win slightly on the benchmark average (76.9 vs 77.5), and people rated the two models almost the same, Elo 1014 vs 1018. So the claim the evidence supports is "human spoken captions match a frontier VLM's captions", not "beat" them. That is still the point: an open team can get distillation-quality data without distilling. (One wrinkle worth knowing: cap F1 itself uses GPT-4o as the judge, comparing a caption against human transcripts; that is evaluation, not training data.)

Claim card: spoken human captions match GPT-4o's captions (Molmo, Table 3b)

Claim. In the paper's words, "Our human annotated data performs on par with distilling GPT-4o by using it to caption the same set of images".

How it was measured. "Our ablations test modifications to the Molmo-7B-D model configuration": one run pre-trains on PixMo-Cap's human captions, one on GPT-4o captions of the same images, and both then go through the same instruction fine-tuning (our reading: the appendix spells the pipeline out only for another row of this table, a LAION pre-stage, which is "then trained on the dense captions and then our instruction tuning mixture as normal"). What would have falsified it. The GPT-4o-captioned run clearly ahead on all three measures. It is ahead on the two measures not graded against human transcripts (11-avg and Elo), by 0.6 on the average, 4 Elo points and a 55-45 head-to-head split, while the human run leads only on cap F1, the metric that favours it. So "on par" is the generous reading, and "better than distillation" is not supported.

The last tab, Caption count and crops, shows two more lessons from Molmo's tables. More human captions keep helping, slowly: going from none to the full set raises the 11-benchmark average by 2.0 points. And how the image is cut matters far more than the encoder brand (Molmo found CLIP, SigLIP and MetaCLIP roughly equal): a single low-resolution view scores 62.8, several crops 75.7, and crops that overlap, so patches at a crop's border can see their neighbours, 76.9.

What did careful ablation buy?

By the authors' own measures, a lot. In Molmo's human-preference study (pairwise votes turned into Elo ratings), Molmo-72B scores 1077, just behind GPT-4o at 1079 and ahead of Gemini 1.5 Pro (1074) and Claude 3.5 Sonnet (1069); on the average of its 11 academic benchmarks it is highest, 81.2 against 78.5, 78.3 and 76.7. Both of those are the authors' own evaluations. On the independent Chatbot Arena, the paper reports, Molmo-72B outperforms all open models but ranks lower than several proprietary models (e.g. GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet).

What the evidence supports, and what it does not

Solid: how the patch sequence is pooled matters (the Perceiver beats a linear layer and MapNet), and the Perceiver's depth does not; about 64 tokens per image is enough except for reading text, where splitting helps; human spoken captions match GPT-4o's captions and beat ShareGPT4V's; overlapping multi-crop beats a single view.

Confounded: "the LM matters more than the encoder" (unequal steps, different resolutions); cross-attention vs one sequence (depends on freezing, short runs, text-vs-table mismatch); anything compared across tables with different setups.

Step back and notice what every model so far has in common. Whatever the connector, whatever the captions, the recipe is the same: a vision encoder trained one way, a language model trained another, and a bridge bolted between them. The image is always translated into the LM's world at the door. Act VI asks the obvious next question: what if images were words from the start, drawn from the same vocabulary, read and written by one transformer?

Act VIOther paths, and where it lands

Why not make images words?

Every model so far treats the picture as a guest: an encoder turns it into vectors, a connector hands them to the language model, and only text ever comes out. Chameleon asks the opposite question: what if an image were cut into a short sentence of discrete codes, written in the same alphabet as text, so that one transformer could read and write both?

The wall: the image is always a guest

Look at the Late fusion path. Flamingo, BLIP-2 and LLaVA are all variations on this shape (Flamingo feeds the vision vectors in through cross-attention layers rather than the input sequence, but the division of labour is the same): a vision encoder makes continuous vectors, a connector reshapes them, and the language model reads them as if they were soft words. The Chameleon paper calls this late fusion, because the two modalities are encoded separately and only meet inside the language model. It has two limits built in. The language model's vocabulary contains only text, so it can describe an image but never draw one; and the image never lives in the same space as the words, it is translated into it by a bridge that has to be trained on its own.

Now press Early fusion. The encoder and connector are gone. In their place is an image tokenizer that turns the picture into a row of integers, and those integers sit in the same sequence as the text tokens. The paper's own summary of the idea: by quantizing images into discrete tokens, analogous to words in text, we can apply the same transformer architecture to sequences of both image and text tokens. The answer to the prediction: only the early path has an arrow that ends in pixels, because the transformer's next token can be an image id, and the tokenizer's decoder turns ids back into a picture.

A picture as a sentence of codes

Chameleon's tokenizer takes a 512x512 image and writes it as 1024 discrete tokens, each chosen from a codebook of 8192 entries. The paper does not say how the tokens are laid out; if they tile the image as a square, that is a 32 × 32 grid, so each token stands for about a 16 × 16 pixel region (this page's arithmetic), and each token carries log2 8192 = 13 bits: the whole picture is squeezed into 1024 × 13 = 13,312 bits.

How can 13 bits describe a patch? The codebook is a dictionary of typical patches. To tokenize, the tokenizer compares each region of the picture with the codebook, finds the closest entry, and writes down that entry's number; to draw, it looks each number up and turns the entries back into pixels. (A learned tokenizer makes that comparison on vectors from a learned encoder and draws with a learned decoder; the toy below compares raw pixels and pastes tiles.) The bottom half of the figure does the same thing in its simplest form, at toy scale: the second panel is the image as the model would read it, 64 numbers and nothing else; the third panel is everything that can be rebuilt from those numbers.

Predict before you press: with a codebook of only two tiles, which parts of the drawing survive? Press Tiny codebook. With K = 2 every patch must be either "dark" or "bright", so the sky and the door fall into the dark tile, the sun, the wall and the ground into the lighter one, and each roof patch, half roof and half sky, goes to whichever is closer. The red error map lights up everywhere the drawing had a shade in between. Drag K upward and the error drains away; at Big codebook the drawing is nearly back. The real tokenizer sits between these extremes: 8192 tiles, but one set shared by every image it will ever see, so fine detail that is rare across images is exactly what gets rounded off. The paper names the casualty: a core weakness of our tokenizer is in reconstructing images with a large amount of text, which caps how well Chameleon can read documents.

The standard vector-quantisation step (in a learned tokenizer it acts on encoder outputs; the toy applies it to raw pixels), written out for this page. Chameleon v2 does not give an equation for its tokenizer (Sec. 2.1 describes it in words and credits Make-A-Scene).

  • \htmlClass{t-blue}{e_k}: codebook entry k, one of the tiles in the bottom row.
  • \htmlClass{t-blue}{z_i}: the id of the closest tile, the integer printed in the second panel. This is the "word" the transformer reads.

One vocabulary, one transformer

Text goes through an ordinary byte-pair tokenizer, and the two are merged: the shared BPE vocabulary has 65,536 entries, a number that includes the 8192 image codebook tokens. So 1/8 of the vocabulary is image codes: the blue slice of the bar in the early-fusion path. From here on there is nothing special about images. The model is a LLaMa-2-style decoder, trained from scratch at 7B and 34B parameters, with the plainest objective there is: predict the next token, whatever kind it is.

\htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}} = \sum_{t} \Big[ \htmlData{step=1, link=seq}{-\log \frac{e^{o_t[x_t]}}{Z_t}} \;+\; \htmlData{step=3, link=zloss}{\htmlClass{t-red}{10^{-5} \log^2 Z_t}} \Big] \qquad \htmlData{step=2, link=vocab}{Z_t = \sum_{i=1}^{65{,}536} e^{o_t[i]}}

Next-token loss (described in words in Chameleon v2) plus the z-loss term written out in Sec. 2.3, Optimization (unnumbered).

  • x_t: token t of the mixed sequence, either a text token or an image id. Nothing in the loss tells them apart.
  • o_t: the logits the transformer computes for position t from the tokens before it (x_{<t}), one per vocabulary entry.
  • Z_t: the softmax's normaliser, summed over the whole shared vocabulary, text and image codes together.
  • \htmlClass{t-red}{10^{-5}\log^2 Z_t}: the z-loss, a small penalty that keeps \log Z_t near zero (below).

The price: training blows up late

This simplicity has a cost that took most of the paper's engineering. The authors write: It was challenging to maintain stable training when scaling the Chameleon models above 8B parameters and 1T tokens. And the trouble arrives late: divergences can manifest after as much as even 20-30% of training progress, which is the worst possible time, with weeks of compute already spent.

The diagnosis is about the softmax. A softmax does not care if every score is shifted by the same constant: \mathrm{softmax}(z) = \mathrm{softmax}(z + c). So nothing in the loss stops the scores from drifting upward together. Because every weight is shared between two modalities that behave very differently, the paper argues that each modality will try to compete with the other by increasing its norms slightly; harmless at first, fatal once the numbers leave the range that bf16, the 16-bit number format used for training, can represent. Two pieces of evidence: an ablation without image generation did not diverge, and the warning sign came early, as uncontrolled growth of output norms is strongly correlated with predicting future loss divergence. If you watch one number during such a run, watch the size of the last layer's outputs.

The fixes, each aimed at a place where a norm can grow:

  • QK-Norm: layer-normalise the query and key vectors inside attention, so the scores entering the attention softmax cannot grow without bound. Without it, Chameleon-7B diverges after approximately 20% of a training epoch. Both sizes use it.
  • z-loss: QK-Norm only protects the softmaxes inside attention, not the final one over the vocabulary. The extra term in the equation above penalises \log Z drifting away from zero, which pins down exactly the constant shift the softmax cannot see. It is the red term in the equation above, acting on the output softmax drawn at the right of the early-fusion path.
  • Dropout after attention and feed-forward layers, for the 7B model only.
  • Norm reordering, for the 34B model only: normalise each sublayer's output before adding it back to the residual stream, instead of its input. The paper's reason: it bounds the growth of the feed-forward block, whose SwiGLU activation multiplies two signals together.
\text{Llama-2: } h = x + \mathrm{attn}(\mathrm{norm}(x)) \qquad \text{Chameleon-34B: } h = x + \mathrm{norm}(\mathrm{attn}(x))

Sec. 2.3 of Chameleon v2 (unnumbered); the feed-forward sublayer is reordered the same way.

Put together from Sec. 2.3, the recipe is: 7B: dropout and z-loss (plus QK-Norm); 34B: z-loss only, plus QK-Norm and norm reordering. With the recipe in place, both models trained for 9.2 trillion (2.1 epochs) tokens.

Qwen3-VL is the late-fusion shape from the start of the page: a vision encoder, a merger, a language model. Almost every part of it is a fix you have already met in an earlier chapter; click a part to see which one, then look at the two ideas that are new.

A chain of fixes, assembled

Read the diagram left to right and it is a tour of this page. An image enters at its own size, the fix from the resolution chapter, instead of being squashed to a small square. DeepStack: more than the last layer

Almost every connector on this page hands the language model the features of one encoder layer, the last or the next-to-last (Molmo, which concatenates two, is the exception). And apart from the cross-attention designs, Flamingo and BLIP, they all put the resulting tokens into the language model's input sequence, so vision enters at its first layer and nowhere else. But a vision transformer's layers hold different things: the paper's words are that intermediate features range from low- to high-level representations (think strokes and edges at one end, objects at the other). Handing over only the top risks losing exactly the fine detail that small print needs. Press With DeepStack. Features from three levels of the encoder each pass through their own small merger, and the result is added to the hidden states of the visual tokens in the first three layers of the language model. Nothing is appended to the sequence, so the context length does not change.

Positions and time

Press Positions. Qwen2-VL's M-RoPE gave each token three position numbers, time, height and width, and gave each number its own block of rotary dimensions. Rotary dimensions run from fast-turning (high frequency, good for fine, nearby distinctions) to slow-turning (low frequency, good for long distances). With contiguous blocks, each axis sees only its own slice of that range, which the Qwen3-VL paper calls an imbalanced frequency spectrum and says hurts long-video understanding. The fix is to interleave the three axes across the dimensions so that each spatial-temporal axis is uniformly represented across both low- and high-frequency bands. That is the whole of what the paper says: there is no equation for the new layout and no ablation isolating it.

Press Video time. The previous model in this line, Qwen2.5-VL (not covered on this page), tied the time position of each video frame to the clock, which produced excessively large and sparse temporal position ids for long videos. Qwen3-VL instead writes the time into the sequence as ordinary text, <3.0 seconds>, in front of each group of frames. It is the same trick as the boxes and points of the grounding chapter (and Qwen3-VL still answers with two grounding modalities: bounding boxes and points): when a number is awkward to build into the architecture, let the language model read and write it as text.

The family

The language models come in four dense sizes, 2B / 4B / 8B / 32B (Sec. 2 says three dense variants (Qwen3-VL-2B/4B/8B/32B) but lists four; the introduction says four), and two mixture-of-experts sizes, 30B-A3B and 235B-A22B (235B total, 22B activated per token). The native context is 256K tokens (S3 sequence length 262,144), enough for long documents and long videos in one sequence; the final pretraining stage is trained at that full length.

Summary

A vision-language model is a vision encoder, a connector and a language model, and every part of that design is a fix for something that broke earlier. Contrastive training (CLIP, ALIGN) gave encoders whose vectors already mean something in words, and SigLIP made that training cheaper by judging each image-caption pair on its own. A vision transformer sees an image as a grid of patch tokens, so the token budget, not the pixels, is what every later model negotiates. To make a model write, BLIP cleaned its own data, Flamingo dialled vision into a frozen language model through gates that start at zero, and BLIP-2 shrank the bridge to a few dozen learned queries. LLaVA showed how far a plain projection goes once the data is right (conversations about images, with the loss on the answers only), though later ablations found the connector's design still matters. After that the walls were about seeing well and pointing: Qwen2-VL fed images at their own size with two-dimensional positions, and Qwen-VL, Florence-2 and Molmo wrote boxes and points into the output sequence (digits for Qwen-VL and Molmo, location tokens for Florence-2). Idefics2 and Molmo tested which choices actually matter (backbones, resolution, data), Chameleon asked whether images could simply be words, and Qwen3-VL assembles the chain: a SigLIP-family encoder at native resolution, merged tokens, rotary positions for time and space, features injected at several depths, and a context long enough for documents and video.
