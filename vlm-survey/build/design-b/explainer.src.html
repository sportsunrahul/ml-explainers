Act IA shared space for pictures and words

Classifiers only know their labels

A classic image classifier can only answer with one of the labels it was trained on; to teach it a new concept you collect new labeled photos. CLIP's fix was to stop predicting labels and instead play a matching game: given a pile of photos and a pile of captions, say which caption goes with which photo.

The wall: a fixed list of answers

Before CLIP, the strongest vision models were trained to pick one entry from a predetermined list of object categories. The CLIP paper names the cost directly: this restricted form of supervision limits their generality and usability since additional labeled data is needed to specify any other visual concept. A model that knows "dog" and "cat" has nothing to say about "a corgi wearing sunglasses", and it cannot even be asked.

The internet, meanwhile, is full of images that already come with text: alt-text, titles, captions. That text is messy, but it describes an open-ended world. CLIP gathered 400 million (image, text) pairs from the internet, found by searching for 500,000 different queries, and asked a model to learn from the text directly.

The matching game

CLIP has two encoders. The image encoder (a ResNet or a vision transformer) turns a photo into a vector; the text encoder (a transformer) turns a caption into a vector. A learned linear projection maps both into the same space, and each vector is scaled to length one, so every photo and every caption becomes a direction. In the figure the space is a circle and each direction is a point on it: blue dots are photos, yellow diamonds are captions.

How similar two directions are is measured by the cosine of the angle between them: 1 when they point the same way, 0 at right angles, -1 when opposite. Take a batch of N photo-caption pairs and compute the cosine of every photo against every caption. That is the N \times N table on the right. The correct answers are all on the diagonal: photo 1 goes with caption 1, and so on. Every off-diagonal cell is a negative, a pairing that is wrong, and the batch provides them for free.

The task is then a classification problem inside each row: out of the N captions, pick the one that belongs to this photo. And the same inside each column: out of the N photos, pick the one that belongs to this caption. Press Train. Every point moves at once. Each photo is pulled toward its own caption and pushed away from all the others, so the pairs close up and spread apart around the circle. The lines show, for each photo, which caption it currently sits closest to: green if that is its own caption, red dashes if not.

CLIP played this game with a batch of 32,768, so each training step filled in a table of 32,768 × 32,768 ≈ 1.07 billion similarities, almost all of them negatives. Keep that number in mind: the next chapter is about what it costs.

The loss, term by term

For one row (one photo), turn the row's similarities into probabilities with a softmax and ask for a high probability on the correct caption. The loss for photos is the average, over all rows, of the negative log of that probability:

\htmlData{step=4}{\htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{img}}} = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}} \log \frac{\htmlData{step=1, link=pos}{\htmlClass{t-green}{\exp(s_{ii}/\tau)}}}{\htmlData{step=2, link=neg}{\sum_{j=1}^{N} \exp(s_{ij}/\tau)}} \qquad \htmlData{step=5}{\htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\big(\htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{img}}} + \htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{txt}}}\big)} \qquad \htmlData{step=3, link=sim}{s_{ij} = \htmlClass{t-blue}{u_i}^{\top}\htmlClass{t-yellow}{v_j}}

Written out from the Fig. 3 pseudocode of CLIP v1, which has no numbered equation; ALIGN v2 Eq. 1 and 2 are the two halves in the same form.

  • \htmlClass{t-blue}{u_i}, \htmlClass{t-yellow}{v_j}: the unit-length embeddings of photo i and caption j (a blue dot and a yellow diamond).
  • s_{ij}: their cosine similarity, the number printed in cell (i, j) of the table.
  • \htmlClass{t-green}{\exp(s_{ii}/\tau)}: the score of the correct pair, the green diagonal cell.
  • The denominator: the same score summed over every caption in the row, the right one and all the negatives. The fraction is the softmax probability of the correct caption; it reaches 1 only when the right caption beats every wrong one by a wide margin.
  • \tau: the temperature, which divides every similarity before the softmax (below).
  • \htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{txt}}}: the same thing down each column (for each caption, find its photo). CLIP averages the two: loss = (loss_i + loss_t)/2.

Notice what the denominator does: the loss for photo i depends on its similarity to every caption in the batch. That coupling is what makes the game informative, and, as the next chapter shows, what makes it expensive.

Temperature: how sharp the verdict is

Cosines only range from -1 to 1, so a softmax over raw cosines is mushy: even a perfect layout gives the right caption only a modest lead. Dividing by a small temperature \tau stretches the gaps before the softmax. Drag the temperature slider and watch the table's colors: at high temperature every row is washed out; at low temperature each row snaps to its best match.

Now predict, then check: what will training do differently when it is hot? Press Train hot, then Train cold. Hot, the pairs still end up on top of their captions, but the loss stalls high and the table stays washed out: when cosines are divided by 1, even a perfect layout cannot earn a confident probability. Cold, the loss falls to almost zero within a few dozen steps; once every photo's own caption wins clearly, the gradient all but vanishes, so from this start two pairs (car and pizza) are still slightly open when the run ends (their diagonal cells read 0.91, not 1.00). CLIP does not hand-tune this: the temperature is directly optimized during training as a log-parameterized multiplicative scalar, starting from the equivalent of 0.07 and clipped to prevent scaling the logits by more than 100.

Some arithmetic makes the training run concrete. At 32,768 pairs per step, one pass over 400 million (image, text) pairs is about 12,200 steps, and 32 epochs is about 390,000 optimizer steps. To fit that in memory the authors used Mixed-precision training plus gradient checkpointing, half-precision Adam statistics, and half-precision stochastically rounded text encoder weights. The largest vision transformer took 12 days on 256 V100 GPUs.

Backward: where the gradient goes

Stage 8 said the gradient "splits" at the table. Here is what that means for one photo. The arrows show which way training pushes the dog photo's vector, one arrow per caption.

Press Sitting on "cat". Only one caption pushes: "cat", the one the photo is currently confused with. The other three wrong captions barely register, because their softmax probability is already close to zero. The loss spends its effort on the negatives that are hard right now, and (our reading) that is one reason a huge batch helps: more captions per step means more chances that one of them is a hard negative for each photo. Press Next to its caption: once the right caption wins its row and column, the pull shrinks to almost nothing too.

\frac{\partial \htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial \htmlClass{t-blue}{u_i}} = \htmlData{step=3, link=tau}{\frac{1}{N\tau}} \sum_{j=1}^{N} \Big( \htmlData{step=2, link=neg}{\htmlClass{t-brown}{\tfrac{p_{ij} + q_{ij}}{2}}} \htmlData{step=1, link=pos}{\htmlClass{t-green}{{} - \delta_{ij}}} \Big)\, \htmlClass{t-yellow}{v_j} \qquad \htmlData{step=4, link=radial}{\frac{\partial \htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial x_i} = \frac{1}{\lVert x_i \rVert}\big(I - \htmlClass{t-blue}{u_i}\htmlClass{t-blue}{u_i}^{\top}\big)\frac{\partial \htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial \htmlClass{t-blue}{u_i}}}

Derived here from the loss above (CLIP v1 prints no gradient); u_i = x_i / \lVert x_i \rVert, where x_i is photo i's vector before normalization.

  • \htmlClass{t-green}{-\delta_{ij}}: minus one for the photo's own caption (j = i), zero otherwise. Gradient descent moves against the gradient, so this term pulls u_i toward its own caption v_i: the green arrow.
  • \htmlClass{t-brown}{p_{ij}}: the probability that photo i's row gives to caption j. \htmlClass{t-brown}{q_{ij}}: the probability that caption j's column gives to photo i. Caption j pushes the photo away in proportion to how confused the two are: p_{ij} is how much of the photo's row the wrong caption steals, q_{ij} how much of that caption's column the photo steals. These are the brown arrows. All the arrows shrink toward zero as the photo wins its own row and column outright.
  • 1/(N\tau): the same for every arrow. A small temperature enlarges this factor, but it also saturates the softmax, so easy pairs get almost no gradient. (The paper's stated reason for capping the logit scale is only that it was necessary to prevent training instability.)
  • The second formula is the normalization step on the way back. The factor I - u_i u_i^{\top} deletes the part of the gradient that points along u_i itself: changing a vector's length cannot change a cosine. Press Apply the normalization: the dashed arrow is the raw sum, the grey arrow is the part that gets deleted, and the bold arrow is what is left, which can only slide the photo around the circle.

From \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial x_i on it is ordinary backpropagation: x_i = W_i^{\top} f_i, so W_i gets \sum_{n=1}^{N} f_n\,(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial x_n)^{\top}, and each f_n (the [class] output) passes the rest down through the vision transformer. Captions get the mirror image: each v_j is pulled toward its own photo and pushed by the photos it is confused with (the same p and q terms, seen from the caption's side), and that gradient flows down the text lane to the [EOS] position and, through masked attention, to every word before it. The temperature's gradient is the sum over the whole table of each logit's gradient times the logit itself: it gets sharper when sharpening would lower the loss.

Press Take gradient steps from any starting point. The photo slides around the circle toward its caption and slows as it arrives, the same saturating gradient as the cold training run in the first figure.

Zero-shot: the text is the classifier

Here is the payoff. To classify a photo into any set of classes, write one caption per class, embed the captions, and pick the caption whose direction is closest to the photo. No new training, no labeled examples: the class names themselves become the classifier. Press Zero-shot a new photo: an unseen photo lands near the trained dog photo (that placement is the toy's assumption, standing in for a trained image encoder), and the bars show its softmax over the five prompts.

The wording of those captions matters. Alt-text is rarely a single word, so CLIP wraps each class name in a sentence. The template A photo of a {label}. alone improves accuracy on ImageNet by 1.3%, and averaging the embeddings of 80 differently worded prompts per class adds a further 3.5%.

The headline: with no ImageNet training images at all, CLIP's best model (ViT-L/14 at 336 pixels) reaches 76.2% top-1 zero-shot on ImageNet, which matches the performance of the original ResNet-50 despite using none of the 1.28 million crowd-labeled training examples that ResNet-50 was trained on. The 336-pixel version is the 224-pixel model with one extra step: the authors pre-train at a higher 336 pixel resolution for one additional epoch, an early sign of how much input resolution will matter later on this page.

ALIGN: the same game on noisier data

In the same month, Google's ALIGN played the same game with far less cleaning. It used 1.8B image-text pairs of raw alt-text with only simple frequency filters, for example exclude alt-texts shared by more than 10 images and discard texts shorter than 3 unigrams or longer than 20 unigrams. Its point was that the scale of the corpus can make up for its noise: in a controlled comparison, its noisy data outperforms Conceptual Captions with only 4x size, and it edged CLIP on zero-shot ImageNet, 76.4 vs 76.2.

The loss is the same shape with small differences: the ALIGN authors minimize the sum of two losses rather than their average, add label smoothing of 0.1, and learn the temperature from an initial 1.0 (in the paper's ablations, on a smaller EfficientNet-B5 and BERT-Base model, it converged to about 1/64).

What CLIP and ALIGN leave behind is an image encoder whose vectors already mean something in words. Almost every later model on this page starts from an encoder trained this way; the exceptions are BLIP (Act II), whose ViT starts from weights pre-trained on ImageNet, and Chameleon (Act VI), which has no image encoder at all. But the matching game only ever says "which caption?"; it cannot write a sentence. Getting from matching to talking is Act II. First, the loss itself hit a wall.

The softmax needs the whole batch

CLIP's loss asks every photo to pick its caption out of the whole batch, so no single cell of the table can be scored without looking at its entire row and column. SigLIP replaced that softmax with a plain yes/no question per cell, and the same five photos and captions from the last chapter show what changes.

The wall: every cell depends on the whole batch

Look again at CLIP's loss for one photo: a softmax over its row. The probability of the right caption is its score divided by the sum of the scores of every caption in the batch. Press Softmax view and pick any cell: the loss that cell feeds into spans its whole row, and the mirror-image loss for captions spans its whole column. One similarity is tied to 2N - 1 cells (9 of the 25 here).

At toy size that is harmless. At CLIP's batch of 32,768, the photos and captions are spread across many accelerators, each holding a slice of the batch. To finish even one row, a device needs every caption embedding from every other device (an all-gather), and the textbook implementation then builds the full table of pairwise similarities in memory. Even the numerics want the whole batch: a softmax is usually stabilised by subtracting the row's maximum first, which requires another pass over the full batch.

A yes/no question per cell

SigLIP (Sigmoid loss for Language-Image Pre-training) drops the competition. Each cell asks its own question: do this photo and this caption belong together? The diagonal cells should say yes, every other cell should say no, and each answer is scored by a logistic (sigmoid) loss, the same loss as any binary classifier. The paper's phrase is that the loss processes every image-text pair independently: the batch of N pairs becomes N^2 separate binary examples. Press Sigmoid view. The table now shows, for each cell, the probability the model gives to "match", computed from that cell alone, and the red outline shrinks to the single cell you picked.

There is a catch hiding in that table: in a batch of N pairs there are only N yes-cells and N^2 - N no-cells. The softmax never noticed, because each row had exactly one right answer. The sigmoid sees 20 no-cells for every 5 yes-cells here, and a real batch is far more lopsided.

Predict: what if the bias starts at 0?

SigLIP adds a learnable bias b to every cell's score before the sigmoid, and starts it at -10, with the scale t (the inverse temperature) starting at log 10 (t = 10) in its log form. A cosine is at most 1, so at t = 10 a score is at most 10, and with the bias at -10 every cell starts out saying "no": the prior, correct for all the negatives. With the bias at 0, a cell at right angles is a coin flip and every cell with a positive cosine says "yes". Before pressing, guess which start ends with all five pairs matched.

Press Train, bias starts at -10. Nothing in the table says yes at first (the readout counts 0 of 25), so the only strong signal is the five diagonal cells asking to be pulled together; they close up within a few dozen steps, the pairs spread around the circle, and all five match. Now press Train, bias starts at 0 from the same start. 12 of the 25 cells begin as "yes", 9 of them wrongly, and the twenty negatives dominate the first steps: the fastest way to silence them is to push everything apart, correct pairs included. Three photos and their three captions end up clumped in two lumps, the bias has only crawled part of the way down, and those three photos can no longer tell their captions apart.

That is the toy's picture of what the paper describes: without the bias, the heavy imbalance coming from the many negatives dominates the loss, leading to large initial optimization steps attempting to correct this bias, while the -10 start starts roughly close to the prior and does not require massive over-correction. The toy exaggerates the effect (it is two-dimensional and keeps t fixed). In the paper's ablation (Table 4, a Base model), ImageNet zero-shot accuracy is 62.0 vs 63.0 for no bias against the -10 start, and a bias started at 0 gives 61.7; with the scale t also started small (t' = \log 1, so t = 1: a hot start in chapter 1's terms), it falls to 53.7.

The two losses, side by side

First the softmax loss, as SigLIP writes it for CLIP, with t the inverse of the temperature \tau from chapter 1. Each term is a whole row or a whole column:

\htmlData{step=3}{\htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{soft}}} = -\frac{1}{2|B|}\sum_{i=1}^{|B|}\Big(} \htmlData{step=1, link=row}{\log \frac{e^{t\,\htmlClass{t-blue}{x_i}\cdot \htmlClass{t-yellow}{y_i}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{|B|} e^{t\,\htmlClass{t-blue}{x_i}\cdot \htmlClass{t-yellow}{y_j}}}} + \htmlData{step=2, link=col}{\log \frac{e^{t\,\htmlClass{t-blue}{x_i}\cdot \htmlClass{t-yellow}{y_i}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{|B|} e^{t\,\htmlClass{t-blue}{x_j}\cdot \htmlClass{t-yellow}{y_i}}}} \htmlData{step=3}{\Big)}

Unnumbered, Sec. 3.1 of SigLIP v4 (the same loss as CLIP's Fig. 3 pseudocode, with t = 1/τ).

Then the sigmoid loss. One term per cell, and nothing in the term mentions any other cell:

\htmlData{step=4, link=pairs}{\htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{sig}}} = -\frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{i=1}^{|B|}\sum_{j=1}^{|B|}} \log \sigma\big(\htmlData{step=1, link=label}{\htmlClass{t-green}{z_{ij}}} \cdot \big(\htmlData{step=2, link=logit}{t\,\htmlClass{t-blue}{x_i}\cdot \htmlClass{t-yellow}{y_j}} \htmlData{step=3, link=bias}{+\, b}\big)\big)

Unnumbered, Sec. 3.2 of SigLIP v4, written in the form of Algorithm 1 (logits = t x.y + b). The equation printed in Sec. 3.2 has the opposite sign on b; with b starting at -10, only the Algorithm 1 form makes every cell start at "no", so we follow Algorithm 1.

  • B: the batch of photo-caption pairs; |B| is its size, the N of the toy.
  • \htmlClass{t-blue}{x_i}, \htmlClass{t-yellow}{y_j}: the unit-length embeddings of photo i and caption j, so x_i \cdot y_j is the cosine in cell (i, j).
  • \htmlClass{t-green}{z_{ij}}: the label, +1 on the diagonal (a true pair) and -1 everywhere else.
  • t: the inverse temperature, learned as t = e^{t'}; it stretches cosines from [-1, 1] into scores from -t to t.
  • b: the learned bias, shared by every cell; it sets where "no" turns into "yes".
  • \sigma(u) = 1/(1 + e^{-u}): the sigmoid, the probability of the answer the label asks for. Its log is 0 when the cell answers confidently and correctly, and very negative when it is confidently wrong.
  • The sum runs over all |B|^2 cells but is divided by |B|, not |B|^2.

Why this lets the batch grow

Because each cell's term stands alone, the table never has to exist all at once. SigLIP splits the loss by device. With the batch spread over D devices, each device holds its own slice of photos and captions, the per-device batch b_{\text{dev}} = |B|/D (the paper calls this b too, a different quantity from the bias). Each device scores its own photos against its own captions, which contains all its positives, then passes its captions to the next device and scores the new block, which is all negatives, and so on around the ring until every photo has met every caption. A final sum across devices adds up the pieces.

Press The paper's mock setup: it is the example in SigLIP's Fig. 1, 3 devices and a global batch size of 12. The captions hop around three times and every cell gets scored, yet at any moment each device holds only one block of size 4 x 4, and there are no all-gathers. Press Softmax: full table for comparison, then drag the devices slider. The paper states the scaling: memory at any moment falls from |B|^2 to b^2 (b = |B|/D per-device batch), a factor of D^2. The paper adds that "Usually b is constant as scaling |B| is achieved by increasing the number of accelerators", so the per-device block stays the same size while the full table grows with the square of the device count. Passing captions around is cheap too: the paper notes that D collective permutes is typically faster than two all-gathers between D devices.

\htmlData{step=1, link=devices}{\htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{sig}}} = -\frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{d_i=1}^{D}} \htmlData{step=2, link=swap}{\sum_{d_j=1}^{D}} \htmlData{step=3, link=block}{\sum_{i=b_{\text{dev}} d_i}^{b_{\text{dev}}(d_i+1)}\ \sum_{j=b_{\text{dev}} d_j}^{b_{\text{dev}}(d_j+1)} \htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}_{ij}}}

Unnumbered, Sec. 3.3 of SigLIP v4; the paper writes the per-device batch as b, we write bdev to keep it apart from the bias.

  • First sum, over devices d_i: every device works in parallel on its own photos; the final cross-device sum adds their results.
  • Second sum, over d_j: the captions of device d_j visit each device in turn (the paper's label: "swap negs across devices").
  • Inner double sum: one b_{\text{dev}} \times b_{\text{dev}} block of cells, the only part of the table in memory at that moment; \mathcal{L}_{ij} is the per-cell term of the sigmoid loss above.

The softmax cannot be split this simply: each row's sum needs every caption before that row's term can be finished. In practice the saving is concrete. The paper reports that four TPU-v4 chips fit a batch of 4096 vs 2048 for a Base SigLIP model against the corresponding CLIP model.

What the bigger batches bought

The surprise is that SigLIP's authors, having made huge batches cheap, found they barely need them. The sigmoid loss wins most clearly at small batch: when the batch is smaller than 16 k it beats the softmax by a large margin, for example ImageNet zero-shot at batch 512: 51.5 vs 47.7 (SigLIP, sigmoid first) and 77.2 vs 74.6 for SigLiT (below). As the batch grows the gap closes. SigLIP performs best at batch size 32 k, whereas the softmax loss required 98 k, and the softmax did not beat it even there. The abstract's conclusion: the benefits of growing batch size quickly diminish, and a batch of 32 k is enough.

The extreme experiment was run on SigLiT, a cheaper variant: the image tower is a frozen, already-trained ViT whose embeddings are precomputed (the "Locked-image Tuning" setup), and only the text tower learns. SigLiT trained at a batch of one million; performance saturated around 32 k and the model peaks at 256 k. No from-scratch SigLIP was trained at a million. The frozen tower also makes SigLiT very cheap. In the abstract's words, "with only four TPUv4 chips, we train a SigLiT model that achieves 84.5% ImageNet zero-shot accuracy in two days."

Inside SigLIP: forward, then backward

SigLIP keeps CLIP's two-lane machine and changes one box: the loss. Step through it below and watch for the three differences, the per-cell sigmoid with its learnable t and b, a backward pass in which no cell's gradient waits for its row, and (in the SigLiT variant) an image lane that is frozen solid.

Backward: no cell waits for its row

Stage 7 claimed that a cell's gradient depends only on that cell. Here is the check. Both tables start mostly solved; then one wrong cell, the dog photo against the caption "cat", rises. Before you press, guess: in each table, which cells' gradients will change?

Press Dog photo drifts toward "cat". On the softmax side the red cell turns brown, as expected, but it drags others with it: the dog photo's own cell and the caption "cat"'s own cell suddenly pull hard, because they share a row or column with the intruder and their softmax probability has dropped. Every cell of that row and column changes a little. On the sigmoid side only the red cell changes. The true pairs were already being pulled (a cosine of 0.80 is still below the "yes" line that b = -10 draws), and they are pulled exactly as hard as before.

\frac{\partial \htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{sig}}}}{\partial \ell_{ij}} = \frac{1}{|B|}\big(\htmlData{step=1, link=cell}{\sigma(\ell_{ij})} \htmlData{step=2, link=own}{\htmlClass{t-green}{{} - \delta_{ij}}}\big) \qquad \htmlData{step=3, link=rowcol}{\frac{\partial \htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{soft}}}}{\partial \ell_{ij}} = \frac{1}{|B|}\Big(\htmlClass{t-brown}{\tfrac{p_{ij} + q_{ij}}{2}} \htmlClass{t-green}{{} - \delta_{ij}}\Big)} \qquad \htmlData{step=4}{\frac{\partial \htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{sig}}}}{\partial \htmlClass{t-blue}{x_i}} = t \sum_{j} \frac{\partial \htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}_{\text{sig}}}}{\partial \ell_{ij}}\, \htmlClass{t-yellow}{y_j}}

Derived here from the Algorithm 1 form of the loss (SigLIP v4 prints no gradient); \ell_{ij} = t\,x_i \cdot y_j + b. The softmax version is the one derived in chapter 1, with t = 1/\tau.

  • \sigma(\ell_{ij}): the probability that cell (i, j) says "yes". It is computed from that one logit, so nothing else in the batch can change it. Write u = z_{ij}\ell_{ij}; the cell's loss is -\log\sigma(u), whose derivative is -(1 - \sigma(u))\,z_{ij}, and for z = +1 and z = -1 both cases come out as the formula shown.
  • \htmlClass{t-green}{-\delta_{ij}}: minus one on the diagonal, the pull toward "yes" for a true pair. It fades only as that pair's own \sigma approaches 1.
  • \htmlClass{t-brown}{p_{ij}}, \htmlClass{t-brown}{q_{ij}}: the row and column softmax of CLIP's loss. Each has a denominator summed over the whole row or column, which is why raising one wrong cell changed the gradients of its neighbours.
  • The last formula is the step into the image lane. Photo i still collects from every caption in its row: its gradient is a sum over j. What changed is that each term of that sum is known the moment its own cell is computed, so the sum can be built one block at a time as captions pass between devices (our reading: the paper describes the chunked forward pass, not its backward). The bias gets the plain sum of all cell gradients, and t gets \sum_{ij} (\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\ell_{ij})\, x_i \cdot y_j.
