Act IITeaching a language model to see

Web captions are noisy

The text scraped alongside web images is often a file name, an advert or a holiday note, and a model trained on it learns that noise too. BLIP's fix was to let the model clean its own data: a captioner writes a new caption for every web image, and a filter throws out every caption, old or new, that does not match its picture.

The wall: alt-text is not a caption

CLIP and ALIGN showed that scale can paper over noisy text when all you want is a matching score. BLIP wanted more: a model that can also match finely and write captions, trained on the same kind of web data. Its authors put the problem plainly: human-annotated pairs such as COCO are expensive and few, while the web pairs used instead are plentiful, and the alt-texts often do not accurately describe the visual content of the images. Press Raw web pairs: of the five toy images, one is labeled with its file name, one with a sofa advert and one with a holiday date.

Throwing more of this at a model does not fix it; every bad pair teaches a wrong association. What BLIP needed was a way to judge each pair and to replace bad text with good text, at the scale of millions of images, without hiring annotators. The trick is that one model can learn both skills, judging and writing, from the same data.

One model, three jobs

BLIP is a single model with a vision transformer for the image and one text transformer that runs in three modes. Each mode is trained by its own objective, and all three are trained together on every image-text pair. Press the buttons below to see which parts each one uses.

ITC, image-text contrastive. This is the chapter 1 matching game. The text encoder reads the caption on its own, with no access to the image, and its [CLS] vector is scored against the image's vector; matched pairs are pulled together and the batch's other pairs pushed apart. BLIP borrows one refinement from an earlier model, ALBEF: a momentum encoder is introduced to produce features, a slowly updated copy of the model whose soft scores soften the hard "only the diagonal is right" targets, since some of the "negatives" in a web batch are in fact fine captions.

ITM, image-text matching. A yes-or-no question: does this caption belong to this image? Now the text transformer does see the image: a cross-attention layer inserted into every block lets each word look at the image features. The answer comes from an ITM head (a linear layer) on the output of a special [Encode] token. Easy negatives would teach little (a dog photo against a caption about spreadsheets), so BLIP mines hard ones. In the paper's words, "negatives pairs with higher contrastive similarity in a batch are more likely to be selected". ITC's scores make the wrong captions that look most right the likeliest picks, and ITM learns to tell them apart word by word.

LM, language modeling. Write the caption. The same stack, with causal self-attention so each word sees only the words before it (and the image through cross-attention), predicts the caption one token at a time, starting from a [Decode] token.

The sharing is the clever part. Encoding and decoding differ mainly in how words look at each other, bidirectionally or only backward, so BLIP's text encoder and decoder share all parameters except for the SA layers, between the encoder and the decoder: the paper's reason is that the differences between the encoding and decoding tasks are best captured by the SA layers. Cross-attention, feed-forward layers and embeddings are one set of weights, used by every pass that has them. The paper tested the alternatives (Table 3, 14M-image pre-training): sharing everything gives 224M parameters but the worst COCO image retrieval (R@1 59.5, the two jobs fight over one self-attention); sharing nothing costs 361M and scores 60.5; sharing all but self-attention costs 252M and scores best, 60.7.

Cost per step, then, is one heavy ViT pass and three light text passes, where a model with separate encoder and decoder would pay for more weights: sharing all but self-attention gives 252M parameters against 361M for no sharing, and the paper counts three forward passes through the text transformer per pair. The paper's reason for sharing the rest is that the embedding layers, CA layers and FFN function similarly between encoding and decoding tasks.

Captioning and filtering

Here is the bootstrap, which the paper calls CapFilt. First pre-train a BLIP model on the noisy corpus. Then make two copies of it and give each one job, each finetuned on its own with COCO's human-written captions:

  • the captioner is the image-grounded decoder, finetuned with the LM loss. It writes one caption per image for every web image. Press Captioner writes.
  • the filter is the image-grounded encoder, finetuned with ITC and ITM. It judges both the original web text and the new synthetic caption, and a text is considered to be noisy if the ITM head predicts it as unmatched to the image. Press Filter judges.

Watch what the filter does in the toy. The file name, the advert and the holiday note are struck out; so is one synthetic caption, which invented a camper and snow. Web texts that genuinely describe their pictures survive. Press Cleaned set: the kept texts, together with the human-annotated pairs, form a new dataset, and a new model is pre-trained on it from scratch.

Why two separately finetuned copies instead of one model doing both? If the captioner and filter share weights, the filter tends to approve its own twin's mistakes. The paper measures this confirmation bias (Table 4): with shared parameters the filter flags only 8% of synthetic captions as noise, against 25% when decoupled, and the downstream model is worse (COCO image-retrieval R@1 62.2 vs 63.1).

Predict: safe captions or surprising ones?

The captioner can decode in two ways. Beam search keeps the few most probable partial sentences at each step and returns the single most likely caption: fluent, safe, generic. Nucleus sampling draws each word at random from the smallest set of candidates whose probabilities add up to p (BLIP uses p = 0.9): livelier, more specific, and sometimes wrong. The filter catches more of the sampled captions, so nucleus sampling leaves the noisier synthetic data.

Before you press anything: which captioner should train the better model, the safe one or the noisy one? Press Captioner: beam search, then Captioner: nucleus sampling, and compare. In the toy, every beam caption passes the filter, but they are short and alike ("a dog on the beach", "a car parked on the street"); the sampled captions lose one to the filter yet mention frisbees, basil leaves and reflections, and the readout counts almost twice as many different content words. Then press Which trained the better model?

The noisy one wins. The paper's explanation, offered as a hypothesis, is in its section title, "Diversity is Key for Synthetic Captions": sampled captions are more diverse and surprising, so they carry more new information, while beam search tends to produce safe captions that are common in the dataset and adds little the model did not already know. The filter makes the gamble affordable: bold captions that are wrong get thrown away, and bold captions that are right stay.

What cleaning buys

BLIP pre-trains on 14M images (COCO and Visual Genome with human captions; Conceptual Captions, Conceptual 12M and SBU with web text) and on a 129M-image set that adds 115M LAION web images. On the 14M set (Table 1), COCO image retrieval R@1 goes from 60.7 without CapFilt to 61.5 with the filter alone, 62.0 with the captioner alone, and 63.1 with both. The gain survives scale: on the 129M set it goes from 62.0 to 64.3.

Two ideas from this chapter carry forward. Web data can be cleaned by a model trained on it, and BLIP-2, two chapters on, reuses BLIP's captioner for exactly that. And one text transformer can both judge and write, with only its self-attention layers kept separate; BLIP-2 goes one step further: its three objectives share the same weights and differ only in the attention mask.

So far, though, the model only writes captions. It cannot answer a question, follow an instruction or reason about what it sees, because its text side is a BERT-sized transformer (initialized from BERT-base), far smaller than the language models that can hold a conversation. The obvious move is to take a big pretrained language model that already can do all that, and bolt vision onto it without breaking it. That is the next chapter.
