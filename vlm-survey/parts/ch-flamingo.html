Don't break the language model

A large pretrained language model already knows how to write; teaching it to read pictures by retraining it risks erasing what it learned from text. Flamingo freezes the language model and slots new layers in between its old ones, each behind a gate that starts shut, so that on the first training step the whole network is the original language model and vision is dialled in from there.

The wall: a model too valuable to retrain

Flamingo is built on the Chinchilla language models, at 1.4B / 7.1B / 70B parameters, and on an NFNet-F6 image encoder of 435M parameters that was first trained with a CLIP-style contrastive loss (the matching game of the first chapter). Both are frozen. The finished models, Flamingo-3B, Flamingo-9B and Flamingo-80B, have 3.2B / 9.3B / 80B parameters in total.

Why not just train everything? The authors tried. In their ablations (on the smallest model, scored by an overall score that divides each benchmark result by the state of the art and averages, with 4 shots), fine-tuning the pretrained language model instead of freezing it scored 62.7 vs 70.7. They name the effect catastrophic forgetting: the model progressively forgets its pretraining while training on a new objective. So the language model stays frozen, and every new ability has to come from layers added around it.

A gate that starts shut

The new layers are cross-attention layers: the text tokens are the queries, and the visual tokens (blue dots in the figure) are the keys and values. Each is followed by a new feed-forward layer. Both outputs are added to the text token's vector on the residual stream, the running sum that every transformer layer reads from and writes to. In the middle panel, the yellow arrow is one text token's vector, the green arrows are what the new layers add, and the brown arrow is the frozen language model layer that follows. The brown dot is where the frozen language model alone would have put the token.

A freshly initialised layer outputs noise. Added to the residual stream at full strength, that noise knocks the frozen model off course before it has learned anything. Flamingo's fix is to multiply each new layer's output by \tanh(\alpha), where \alpha is one learnable number per layer, initialised to 0. Since \tanh(0) = 0, the new layers contribute nothing at first; in the paper's words, at initialization, the model output matches that of the pretrained LM.

Now answer the figure's question. Press Step 0, gated: both gate dots sit at the origin of the tanh curve, the green arrows have zero length, the model's dot lands on the brown one, and the next-word bars fill the frozen model's brown boxes exactly (the distance between the two distributions, their KL divergence, reads 0.000). Then press Step 0, no gates: the same random layers added at full strength drag the token far away, and the toy model's favourite next word flips from "cat" to "car". Finally drag training progress, or press Train: the gates open, the green arrows grow, and "dog" (the word the image supports) takes over.

The gate matters in practice: removing the tanh gating scored 66.5 vs 70.7 with it. There is also a neat consequence you can read straight off the equation below (it follows from the algebra; the paper does not spell it out). When \alpha = 0, the new layer's weights get zero gradient, because their output is multiplied by \tanh(0) = 0. The gate itself does get a gradient, because the slope of tanh at zero is 1. So the first thing training can change is the gates: vision is let in by opening them, and only then do the new weights start to learn.

The block, term by term

Here is one inserted block, including the frozen layer it sits in front of. Read the arrows in the figure from the origin outward; they are these four lines in order:

\begin{aligned} \htmlData{step=1, link=text}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{y}} &\leftarrow \htmlClass{t-yellow}{y} + \htmlData{step=2, link=gate}{\htmlClass{t-green}{\tanh(\alpha_{\text{xattn}})}} \cdot \htmlData{step=3, link=xattn}{\htmlClass{t-green}{\mathrm{Attn}}\big(q{=}\htmlClass{t-yellow}{y},\ kv{=}\htmlClass{t-blue}{x}\big)} \\ \htmlClass{t-yellow}{y} &\leftarrow \htmlClass{t-yellow}{y} + \htmlData{step=4, link=dense}{\htmlClass{t-green}{\tanh(\alpha_{\text{dense}})} \cdot \htmlClass{t-green}{\mathrm{FFW}}(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{y})} \\ \htmlClass{t-yellow}{y} &\leftarrow \htmlClass{t-yellow}{y} + \htmlData{step=5, link=frozen}{\htmlClass{t-brown}{\mathrm{Attn}_{\text{frozen}}}\big(q{=}\htmlClass{t-yellow}{y},\ kv{=}\htmlClass{t-yellow}{y}\big)} \\ \htmlClass{t-yellow}{y} &\leftarrow \htmlClass{t-yellow}{y} + \htmlData{step=5, link=frozen}{\htmlClass{t-brown}{\mathrm{FFW}_{\text{frozen}}}(\htmlClass{t-yellow}{y})} \end{aligned}

Written out from the Fig. 4 pseudocode of Flamingo v2 (no numbered equation).

  • \htmlClass{t-yellow}{y}: the text tokens' vectors on the residual stream (the yellow arrow, then the running tip).
  • \htmlClass{t-blue}{x}: the visual tokens, which come from the Perceiver Resampler below; they supply the keys and values.
  • \htmlClass{t-green}{\mathrm{Attn}}(q{=}\htmlClass{t-yellow}{y},\ kv{=}\htmlClass{t-blue}{x}): the new cross-attention, trained from scratch: each text token asks the visual tokens what is relevant to it (the first green arrow).
  • \htmlClass{t-green}{\mathrm{FFW}}: a new feed-forward layer, also trained from scratch (the dashed green arrow).
  • \htmlClass{t-green}{\tanh(\alpha_{\text{xattn}})}, \htmlClass{t-green}{\tanh(\alpha_{\text{dense}})}: the two gates: one learnable scalar per new layer, so two per block, both starting at 0 (the filled and hollow dots on the curve). tanh keeps each gate between -1 and 1.
  • \htmlClass{t-brown}{\mathrm{Attn}_{\text{frozen}}}, \htmlClass{t-brown}{\mathrm{FFW}_{\text{frozen}}}: the language model's own self-attention and feed-forward layer, unchanged (the brown arrow).

The gate scales the new layer's output, not its input, and it sits on the side branch, never on the main path: the residual stream itself is never scaled, and the frozen weights never change. What changes is the input the frozen layer receives. Put \alpha_{\text{xattn}} = \alpha_{\text{dense}} = 0 into the first two lines and they reduce to y \leftarrow y, which is the whole trick.

How many blocks? Inserting one in front of every language model layer is best but expensive, so the bigger models space them out. Flamingo-3B adds one before every LM block (24 layers); Flamingo-9B adds one every 4th block (10 layers over 40); Flamingo-80B adds one every 7th block (12 layers over 80). Even spaced out, the new layers are large: the paper's appendix says the 80B model's blocks account for 10B additional learned parameters.

Any image or video, 64 tokens

The visual tokens \htmlClass{t-blue}{x} need a fixed, small size: every text token cross-attends to them, in every inserted block. But the image encoder produces a grid of features whose size depends on the resolution, and a video produces one grid per frame. The Perceiver Resampler sits between the two and always hands over 64 tokens.

Predict first: slide frames from 1 to 30. How many tokens does the language model receive? The resampler owns a set of 64 learned vectors, the latents. They are the queries of a small transformer; the keys and values are all the visual features (every patch of every frame, flattened into one long list) together with the latents themselves. Whatever arrives, 64 vectors leave, and the "64 out" label never changes. Only the key count grows, and it grows inside the resampler, which runs once per image, instead of inside every cross-attention block of the language model.

\begin{aligned} \htmlData{step=1, link=features}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{x_f}} &\leftarrow \htmlData{step=1, link=features}{\mathrm{flatten}\big(\htmlClass{t-blue}{x_f} + e_{\text{time}}\big)} \\ \htmlData{step=2, link=latents}{\htmlClass{t-green}{z}} &\leftarrow \htmlClass{t-green}{z} + \htmlData{step=3, link=resampler}{\mathrm{Attn}\big(q{=}\htmlClass{t-green}{z},\ kv{=}[\htmlClass{t-blue}{x_f};\, \htmlClass{t-green}{z}]\big)} \\ \htmlClass{t-green}{z} &\leftarrow \htmlClass{t-green}{z} + \htmlData{step=3, link=resampler}{\mathrm{FFW}(\htmlClass{t-green}{z})} \end{aligned}

Written out from the Fig. 5 pseudocode of Flamingo v2 (no numbered equation); the last two lines repeat once per layer.

  • \htmlClass{t-blue}{x_f}: the frozen encoder's features, one grid per frame (an image is a video with one frame), flattened into a single list.
  • e_{\text{time}}: a learned embedding per frame, added to every feature of that frame. It is the only explicit position encoding: the paper found spatial grid encodings did not help, likely because the NFNet's convolutional features already carry spatial information.
  • \htmlClass{t-green}{z}: the 64 learned latents, which become the visual tokens \htmlClass{t-blue}{x} of the gated blocks.
  • [\htmlClass{t-blue}{x_f};\, \htmlClass{t-green}{z}]: keys and values are the features and the latents stacked together, in the pseudocode concat([x_f, x]), which the authors found slightly better than features alone.

The resampler is small and the same for all three model sizes: 6 / 1536 / 16 (layers / width / heads), about 194M parameters. Videos are trained on 8 frames at 1 fps and tested on 30 frames at 3 FPS, by interpolating the learned time embeddings; the three buttons show those settings. Against simpler connectors in the same ablation setup, the resampler, an MLP and a plain transformer scored 70.7 vs 66.6 vs 66.7.

Which image does each word see?

Flamingo reads documents where images and text alternate: a photo, a sentence about it, another photo, another sentence. If every text token could cross-attend to every earlier image, a caption could just as easily describe the wrong picture. Flamingo's rule is stricter: a text token cross-attends only to the visual tokens of the last image before it. Text that comes before any image sees none. The bottom panel shows the rule on a short prompt: each text chunk has a green cell under the one image it may see. Press Ablation: all earlier images to see the alternative fill the table's lower triangle.

Earlier images are not forgotten: they still reach a token indirectly, through the frozen self-attention over the earlier text, which did see them. The rule has a practical payoff. The model is trained with up to 5 images per sequence, yet it can use up to 32 pairs of images and texts at test time, since no text token ever faces more than one image. And it scored better: last image only against all earlier images, 70.7 vs 63.5. One training detail: for web pages, the text is mapped to the next image instead of the previous one with probability 1/2, a small data augmentation; at test time the image always comes first.

Few-shot, by prompting

Interleaving is what makes Flamingo usable without fine-tuning. To teach it a new task, write a prompt the way you would for a text model: a few example images each followed by its answer, then the query image. The model continues the pattern. To learn this, Flamingo trained on M3W, text and images scraped in place from 43.3M web pages, mixed with three image-text and video-text pair datasets (1.8 billion ALIGN pairs, 312 million LTIP pairs, 27 million video clips); how the four datasets are weighted and batched is the subject of the training-step chapter. Without M3W, the overall score fell to 53.4 vs 70.7.

The result: across 16 benchmarks, Flamingo-80B beat every earlier zero-shot and few-shot method, and on six tasks it beat models fine-tuned on each task's full training set while seeing only 32 task-specific examples (for example OK-VQA, 57.8 vs 54.4). The caption of the paper's Table 1 says seven; the table's own rows, the text and Fig. 2 all give six. Fine-tuned models still win where training sets are huge: on VQAv2, 67.6 vs 80.2.

Backward: why the first step moves only the gates (almost)

Stage 8 ended on a claim: at step 0 the gradient reaches the gates but not the weights behind them. Here is that gradient for a toy block (the new layers only; the toy has no <EOC> row), one bar per group of parameters.

Press Step 0: gates at 0. Three bars are exactly zero: the cross-attention weights, the feed-forward weights and the visual tokens. The last one matters most: the visual tokens are the resampler's output, so on the first step the resampler learns nothing either. The two gate bars are not zero. And the bottom bar, the gradient the block passes down to the frozen layers below it, lands exactly on the brown tick: at step 0 the block is invisible in the backward pass too. Drag the slider: as soon as the gates leave zero, the weight, visual-token and down-pass bars leave zero too, and the gate bars change (they do not simply grow). Press Gates opened and notice the sign: in this toy, opening the cross-attention gate in the positive direction raises the loss, so training would push \alpha_{\text{xattn}} negative instead.

The reason is one line of calculus. Write the first line of the block as y_1 = y + \tanh(\alpha)\,A, where A = \mathrm{Attn}(q{=}y,\ kv{=}x;\ W) is the new layer's output, and let g = \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial y_1 be the gradient arriving from above. The chain rule then gives:

\begin{aligned} \htmlData{step=1, link=weights}{\frac{\partial \htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial \htmlClass{t-green}{W}}} &= \htmlData{step=2, link=gate}{\htmlClass{t-green}{\tanh(\alpha)}}\, \Big(\frac{\partial A}{\partial \htmlClass{t-green}{W}}\Big)^{\!\top} g, \qquad \htmlData{step=3, link=visual}{\frac{\partial \htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial \htmlClass{t-blue}{x}} = \htmlClass{t-green}{\tanh(\alpha)}\, \Big(\frac{\partial A}{\partial \htmlClass{t-blue}{x}}\Big)^{\!\top} g} \\ \htmlData{step=4, link=alpha}{\frac{\partial \htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial \htmlClass{t-green}{\alpha}} = \big(1 - \tanh^2\alpha\big)\; g^{\top} A} \qquad\quad \htmlData{step=5, link=down}{\frac{\partial \htmlClass{t-red}{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial \htmlClass{t-yellow}{y}} = g + \htmlClass{t-green}{\tanh(\alpha)}\, \Big(\frac{\partial A}{\partial \htmlClass{t-yellow}{y}}\Big)^{\!\top} g} \end{aligned}

Derived here from the Fig. 4 pseudocode of Flamingo v2 (the paper prints no gradient). The feed-forward line, with \alpha_{\text{dense}} and \mathrm{FFW}(y) in place of \alpha and A, gives the same four formulas without the x term.

  • \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial \htmlClass{t-green}{W}: the new layer's weights. Every path from them to the loss goes through the multiplication by \tanh(\alpha), so the whole gradient carries that factor; at \alpha = 0 it is exactly zero.
  • \htmlClass{t-green}{\tanh(\alpha)}: the gate, the factor on every new-layer term (the whole of the first two formulas, the second term of the fourth).
  • \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial \htmlClass{t-blue}{x}: the visual tokens, and through them the resampler. Same factor, same zero.
  • \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial \htmlClass{t-green}{\alpha}: the gate's own gradient. Its factor is the slope of tanh, which is 1 at zero, times g^{\top}A: how much the layer's current output, noise included, points along the direction that would lower the loss. The gate opens (or goes negative: tanh runs from -1 to 1) according to whether the new layer's output helps.
  • \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial \htmlClass{t-yellow}{y}: what the block passes down to the frozen layers below. At \alpha = 0 it is just g, the frozen model's own gradient.

Now the prediction. Press What if W also started at 0? Then A = 0, so g^{\top}A = 0 and the gate's gradient vanishes too: the gate waits for the weights and the weights wait for the gate, and the cross-attention can never start. (The feed-forward gate still moves, because its own layer is still random.) So the recipe needs both halves: a gate at zero so that the output starts as the language model's, and random weights behind it so that the gate has something to measure. That pairing is our reading of the design; the paper states only the zero gate and cites ReZero for it. How long the gates lead depends on the optimizer (our derivation, not the paper's). Under plain SGD the weights' gradient stays proportional to \tanh(\alpha), so the gates lead for a while. Under AdamW, which divides each step by the gradient's own running size, the gates lead by one step only: from the second step the weights already move at nearly full speed (about 0.74 of the learning rate at step 2 with the default Adam betas, against 1.0 for the gates). Two caveats: Flamingo's warm-up starts at a learning rate of 0, so the literal first update moves nothing, and decoupled weight decay of 0.1 shrinks the weights slightly even when their gradient is zero.

Flamingo showed that a frozen language model can learn to see without forgetting how to write. But its bridge is not small: the 80B model adds the 10B additional learned parameters quoted above, trained on 1536 TPUv4 chips for 15 days. The next chapter asks how small the bridge between a frozen image encoder and a frozen language model can be.
