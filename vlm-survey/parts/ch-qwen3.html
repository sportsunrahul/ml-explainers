Putting it together

Qwen3-VL is the late-fusion shape from the start of the page: a vision encoder, a merger, a language model. Almost every part of it is a fix you have already met in an earlier chapter; click a part to see which one, then look at the two ideas that are new.

A chain of fixes, assembled

Read the diagram left to right and it is a tour of this page. An image enters at its own size, the fix from the resolution chapter, instead of being squashed to a small square. A SigLIP-2 encoder cuts it into patches (the patches chapter) and runs a vision transformer whose training descends from the sigmoid loss of the SigLIP chapter; Qwen3-VL starts from the public SigLIP-2 checkpoints and keeps training it on images of varying size. A merger folds every 2x2 block of patch vectors into one token, which keeps a large page from flooding the context. The tokens join the text in one sequence, each tagged with a (time, height, width) position. And the training starts the way LLaVA's did (the LLaVA chapter; the mechanics are in one training step): first only the bridge learns, with encoder and language model frozen, then everything is unfrozen.

Two things in the diagram are not in any earlier chapter: the extra wires from the middle of the encoder into the bottom of the language model, and the text labels in front of the video frames.

DeepStack: more than the last layer

Almost every connector on this page hands the language model the features of one encoder layer, the last or the next-to-last (Molmo, which concatenates two, is the exception). And apart from the cross-attention designs, Flamingo and BLIP, they all put the resulting tokens into the language model's input sequence, so vision enters at its first layer and nowhere else. But a vision transformer's layers hold different things: the paper's words are that intermediate features range from low- to high-level representations (think strokes and edges at one end, objects at the other). Handing over only the top risks losing exactly the fine detail that small print needs. Press With DeepStack. Features from three levels of the encoder each pass through their own small merger, and the result is added to the hidden states of the visual tokens in the first three layers of the language model. Nothing is appended to the sequence, so the context length does not change.

\htmlData{step=3, link=hidden}{\htmlClass{t-yellow}{h^{(\ell)}_{\text{vis}}}} \;\leftarrow\; h^{(\ell)}_{\text{vis}} \;+\; \htmlData{step=2, link=merge}{\htmlClass{t-green}{\mathrm{Merger}_{\ell}}}\big(\htmlData{step=1, link=level}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{v^{(\ell)}}}\big), \qquad \ell = 1, 2, 3

Written from the words of Sec. 2.2 of Qwen3-VL v2, which has no equation for it.

  • \htmlClass{t-blue}{v^{(\ell)}}: the patch features at encoder level \ell, one of the three blue taps. The paper does not say which encoder layers these are. Which level feeds which of the three language-model layers is not stated either; pairing level \ell with layer \ell, shallowest first, is this page's assumption.
  • \htmlClass{t-green}{\mathrm{Merger}_{\ell}}: a dedicated merger for that level, like the main one, mapping patch features to the language model's width.
  • \htmlClass{t-yellow}{h^{(\ell)}_{\text{vis}}}: the hidden states of the visual tokens in language-model layer \ell. Text tokens are untouched.
  • The arrow means "add into the residual stream": a residual shortcut, which is why it costs no extra tokens.

Did it help? The paper's ablation trains an internal 15B-A2B LLM, with all experiments pretrained on 200 billion tokens, and scores the models on validation sets without any post-training. So these are small, early-training numbers, not the release models. The answer to the prediction: mostly the reading tests, though not cleanly. The five largest gains are OCRBench (+2.6), InfoVQA (+2.3), MMStar (+2.2), ChartQA (+1.8) and DocVQA (+1.6): four reading tests and one general benchmark. The paper says DeepStack boosts fine-grained visual understanding, such as on the InfoVQA and DocVQA benchmarks, which is what you would expect if the middle layers carry detail the last layer has thrown away. AI2D and MMMU land in the middle, and the other general benchmarks barely move (+0.2 to +0.4). The split is not clean: MMStar, a general benchmark, beats DocVQA and ChartQA, and TextVQA, a reading test, is flat (down 0.1).

Positions and time

Press Positions. Qwen2-VL's M-RoPE gave each token three position numbers, time, height and width, and gave each number its own block of rotary dimensions. Rotary dimensions run from fast-turning (high frequency, good for fine, nearby distinctions) to slow-turning (low frequency, good for long distances). With contiguous blocks, each axis sees only its own slice of that range, which the Qwen3-VL paper calls an imbalanced frequency spectrum and says hurts long-video understanding. The fix is to interleave the three axes across the dimensions so that each spatial-temporal axis is uniformly represented across both low- and high-frequency bands. That is the whole of what the paper says: there is no equation for the new layout and no ablation isolating it.

Press Video time. The previous model in this line, Qwen2.5-VL (not covered on this page), tied the time position of each video frame to the clock, which produced excessively large and sparse temporal position ids for long videos. Qwen3-VL instead writes the time into the sequence as ordinary text, <3.0 seconds>, in front of each group of frames. It is the same trick as the boxes and points of the grounding chapter (and Qwen3-VL still answers with two grounding modalities: bounding boxes and points): when a number is awkward to build into the architecture, let the language model read and write it as text.

The family

The language models come in four dense sizes, 2B / 4B / 8B / 32B (Sec. 2 says three dense variants (Qwen3-VL-2B/4B/8B/32B) but lists four; the introduction says four), and two mixture-of-experts sizes, 30B-A3B and 235B-A22B (235B total, 22B activated per token). The native context is 256K tokens (S3 sequence length 262,144), enough for long documents and long videos in one sequence; the final pretraining stage is trained at that full length.

The answer: all four mergers get a gradient, the ViT does not move. In S0 the language model is a fixed reader, and the only thing being learned is how to write visual tokens it can read, at four depths at once.

That is where the chain of fixes has arrived for now: a contrastively trained encoder, native resolution, a small merger, positions that know about space and time, and a language model that reads it all as one sequence.
