Act VWhat actually matters

Most design choices were never tested

By 2024 every VLM was a stack of choices: which language model, which vision encoder, which connector, how many image tokens, whose captions. Most were inherited from the previous paper rather than tested, so two teams, Idefics2 (Hugging Face) and Molmo (Allen Institute for AI), went back and changed one thing at a time.

How to read an ablation

An ablation trains the same model twice with one thing changed and compares a score. Idefics2's runs are deliberately cheap: 6,000 steps each, scored by the average of four-shot results on four benchmarks (VQAv2, TextVQA, OKVQA and COCO captioning). Two cautions follow. A short run measures how fast a choice learns, not where it ends up. And the average mixes accuracies with a COCO captioning score on a different scale, so only differences within one table mean anything. Molmo scores its ablations with two numbers: a caption-quality metric right after pre-training, and the average over its 11 benchmarks after fine-tuning.

Backbones: the language model moved the needle more

Start on the Backbones tab. Holding the vision encoder fixed and swapping Llama-1-7B for Mistral-7B raises the average from 62.5 to 67.6. Holding the language model fixed and swapping the vision encoder moves it less, from 57.4 to at most 60.7. The paper's Finding 1: For a fixed number of parameters, the quality of the language model backbone has a higher impact on the performance of the final VLM than the quality of the vision backbone.

Be careful with that conclusion; the note under the chart says why. The two swaps are not the same size of step: the language models are 35.1% vs 60.1% on MMLU, a huge jump, while the encoders are 78.0% vs 83.2% on ImageNet. And the best encoder also ran at a higher resolution (384 against 224 pixels), which the paper itself credits for part of its TextVQA gain. So the evidence says "a much better LM helped more than a somewhat better encoder at a different resolution", which is weaker than the finding's wording. One more surprise: EVA-CLIP-5B, roughly ten times SigLIP's size, is no better; the authors suggest it may be under-trained.

Claim card: the language model matters more than the vision encoder (Idefics2, Finding 1)

Claim. At a fixed parameter count, a better language model helps a VLM more than a better vision encoder.

How it was measured. One-swap runs under the cross-attention architecture, each 6,000 steps, scored by the four-benchmark 4-shot average. Held fixed, in the paper's words: "We fix the size of the pretrained backbones, the data used for multimodal pre-training, and the number of training updates". Not held fixed: the vision encoder's size (CLIP-ViT-H, EVA-CLIP-5B and SigLIP-SO400M differ; the paper calls EVA-CLIP-5B ten times bigger in parameter counts than SigLIP-SO400M), how much better each replacement is on its own (MMLU 35.1% vs 60.1%, ImageNet 78.0% vs 83.2%) and the encoder's input resolution (224 vs 384).

Result. The LM swap yields a boost of 5.1 (62.5 to 67.6); the encoder swap yields a 3.3 increase (57.4 to 60.7).

What would have falsified it. The encoder swap giving the larger gain. Our reading: the design cannot tell "the LM matters more" from "this LM step was bigger", because the two steps are not matched in size; a fair test would compare swaps of equal standalone improvement at one resolution.

Cross-attention or one long sequence?

Two families of models have met on this page. Flamingo keeps the language model's sequence text-only and adds cross-attention layers that look at the image. LLaVA and its descendants simply put the image tokens into the sequence and let the LM's own self-attention do the work; Idefics2 calls this fully autoregressive. Which is better? Predict before you press: with both backbones frozen, cross-attention wins. What happens when the backbones are allowed to train?

Press Cross-attention or one sequence. Frozen (brown), cross-attention leads, 66.7 against 60.3, which makes sense: its new layers are extra trainable capacity, while the fully autoregressive model can only adapt through its connector. Trained (yellow), the order flips: fully autoregressive reaches 69.5 against 67.3. Finding 2, in the paper's words: The cross-attention architecture performs better than the fully autoregressive one when unimodal pre-trained backbones are kept frozen. However, when training the unimodal backbones, the fully autoregressive architecture outperforms the cross-attention one, even though the latter has more parameters.

"Allowed to train" hides an engineering detail. Unfreezing everything made the fully autoregressive runs diverge, and lowering the learning rate did not fix it. What worked was LoRA: the pretrained weights stay fixed and each gets a small trainable low-rank correction, which can be merged back in afterwards at no inference cost. One more honesty note: the paper's prose says frozen cross-attention leads by 7 points and that LoRA adds 12.9 points to the fully autoregressive model, but its own table gives 66.7 − 60.3 = 6.4 and 69.5 − 60.3 = 9.2 (only its 0.6 for cross-attention matches). The chart plots the table.

Claim card: which architecture wins depends on what is allowed to train (Idefics2, Finding 2)

Claim. Cross-attention beats one-sequence ("fully autoregressive") when the pretrained backbones are frozen; once they train, the one-sequence model wins despite having fewer parameters.

How it was measured. The same 6,000 steps protocol and four-benchmark average, both architectures, two training regimes. Frozen, only approximately 15% for the fully autoregressive architecture and 25% for the cross-attention of the parameters train. "Trained" means LoRA, used to adapt the pre-trained parameters while using standard full fine-tuning for the newly initialized ones, because full unfreezing diverged. Not held fixed: the data per step, since the cross-attention architecture feeds a single token per image to the language model, against 64 for the fully autoregressive architecture, so at the same batch and sequence length the two see different numbers of images and text tokens (the appendix names this and plots both against images and tokens seen, with the fully autoregressive model ahead throughout); and parameter count (cross-attention trains 1.3B more (2B trainable); 10% more flops).

Result. Frozen: 66.7 (cross-attention) vs 60.3. LoRA: 67.3 vs 69.5. The order flips.

What would have falsified it. Cross-attention still ahead, or tied, under LoRA. The flip is 2.2 points, with no repeats or error bars reported, so it is a clear direction rather than a precise margin.

The connector does matter

A common reading of LLaVA was that the connector hardly matters: a linear layer is enough. Idefics2's appendix does not test LLaVA's layer (which maps each patch separately); it compares ways of squeezing the patch sequence into a few tokens, and the Connector tab shows a wide spread: a linear layer over the flattened sequence scores 44.5, MapNet (a small learned mapping network) 51.8, and the Perceiver resampler 60.3. What does not matter is the Perceiver's depth: one, three or twelve layers all land within a point. (The appendix does not state the training setup. The MapNet and Perceiver values, 51.8 and 60.3, equal two frozen-backbone rows of the architecture table, which suggests they are the same runs; the paper does not say.) So the fair summary is: what the connector does matters; how big it is, much less.

Fewer image tokens, no loss

The Perceiver is also a compressor: it turns the encoder's patch grid into a fixed number of output tokens (Flamingo's resampler from Act II). Replacing no pooling with a Perceiver took each image from 729 to 64 tokens and raised the average by 8.5 points. Press 64 or 128 tokens: doubling the Perceiver's output to 128 tokens scores 71.2 against 71.7 for 64. For comparison, the other VLMs of the time used 576 (DeepSeek-VL) to 2890 (SPHINX-2k) tokens per image. For these benchmarks, most of those tokens were not buying anything.

Claim card: learned pooling to 64 tokens costs nothing (Idefics2, Finding 4)

Claim. Squeezing each image into a few learned tokens with a Perceiver both cuts compute and raises the score.

How it was measured. Two separate comparisons. (a) In the architecture table, the frozen fully autoregressive model with and without a Perceiver: 51.8 vs 60.3, which is where the paper's 8.5 points for 729 to 64 tokens comes from. (b) A separate pooling table, from a different fully autoregressive setup (its scores run about ten points higher), with 64 vs 128 Perceiver outputs. Same four-benchmark average; the absolute numbers of (a) and (b) are not comparable with each other.

Result. (a) 8.5 points gained by pooling. (b) 71.7 for 64 against 71.2 for 128: "we observe no gains when using more than 64 visual tokens".

What would have falsified it. The 729-token run scoring higher, or 128 tokens clearly beating 64. The paper itself names the limit, "in a hypothetical scenario of infinite training on unlimited data, performance might eventually improve". Add that these are short runs and that only one of the four benchmarks, TextVQA, is about reading text, which is exactly where the next section finds that more tokens pay.

Split the image when you need to read

The exception is text. Idefics2 can also cut an image into four crops plus the whole image, each encoded to 64 tokens, 64 / 320 tokens per image without or with splitting. Press Image splitting: the same weights read better with the extra tokens, TextVQA 70.4 → 73.0 and DocVQA 67.3 → 74.0, while reasoning (MMMU) and general questions (MMBench) do not move. It is the resolution story of Act IV again: detail costs tokens, and only some questions need the detail. The resulting recipe is Idefics2 itself: SigLIP-SO400M + Mistral-7B-v0.1, 8 billion parameters, fully autoregressive, 64 visual tokens per image (320 with splitting), max image resolution 980 pixels.

Molmo: captions from people's voices

Molmo's question is about data. The strongest open models of 2024 were trained on detailed captions written by GPT-4V or GPT-4o, which, the authors argue, makes them distillations of closed models. Molmo's datasets were all collected without the use of external VLMs; "without any reliance on synthetic data from other VLMs, including proprietary ones". The trick for getting long, detailed captions from people was to have them talk: annotators described each image aloud for a minute or more, the audio was transcribed, and a text-only LLM cleaned the transcript. PixMo-Cap has 712k distinct images with 1.3M transcripts and captions, and its captions average 196 words vs 11 words vs 37 words (PixMo-Cap vs COCO vs Localized Narratives).

Did speaking beat distilling? Press Whose captions. Against ShareGPT4V+o, popular sets of GPT-4V and GPT-4o captions, PixMo-Cap wins clearly (cap F1 36.3 vs 54.1). But the fair test is the one where the images are the same: Molmo also had GPT-4o caption the PixMo images, and the result is a tie. Human captions win slightly on cap F1, GPT-4o captions win slightly on the benchmark average (76.9 vs 77.5), and people rated the two models almost the same, Elo 1014 vs 1018. So the claim the evidence supports is "human spoken captions match a frontier VLM's captions", not "beat" them. That is still the point: an open team can get distillation-quality data without distilling. (One wrinkle worth knowing: cap F1 itself uses GPT-4o as the judge, comparing a caption against human transcripts; that is evaluation, not training data.)

Claim card: spoken human captions match GPT-4o's captions (Molmo, Table 3b)

Claim. In the paper's words, "Our human annotated data performs on par with distilling GPT-4o by using it to caption the same set of images".

How it was measured. "Our ablations test modifications to the Molmo-7B-D model configuration": one run pre-trains on PixMo-Cap's human captions, one on GPT-4o captions of the same images, and both then go through the same instruction fine-tuning (our reading: the appendix spells the pipeline out only for another row of this table, a LAION pre-stage, which is "then trained on the dense captions and then our instruction tuning mixture as normal"). Held fixed: model and images. Fine-tuning presumably too, but PixMo-Cap also appears in the fine-tuning mixture and the paper does not say whether the GPT-4o run swapped those captions as well (our reading). Metrics: cap F1 right after pre-training, whose scoring is itself done by GPT-4o ("We prompt GPT-4o to enumerate a list of all distinct atomic statements", then match them against human transcripts), the 11-benchmark average after fine-tuning, and a human-preference Elo. One confound favours the human run: cap F1 grades captions against PixMo's own human transcripts, the same kind of text the human run was trained on.

Result. cap F1 54.1 (human) vs 52.9 (GPT-4o); 11-avg 76.9 vs 77.5; Elo 1014 vs 1018, and head to head the GPT-4o-caption model wins 55% of non-tied votes against the default (Table 4). Small margins; the appendix puts it plainly: "if we caption all PixMo-Cap images with GPT-4o and train on those captions, both metrics perform strongly".

What would have falsified it. The GPT-4o-captioned run clearly ahead on all three measures. It is ahead on the two measures not graded against human transcripts (11-avg and Elo), by 0.6 on the average, 4 Elo points and a 55-45 head-to-head split, while the human run leads only on cap F1, the metric that favours it. So "on par" is the generous reading, and "better than distillation" is not supported.

The last tab, Caption count and crops, shows two more lessons from Molmo's tables. More human captions keep helping, slowly: going from none to the full set raises the 11-benchmark average by 2.0 points. And how the image is cut matters far more than the encoder brand (Molmo found CLIP, SigLIP and MetaCLIP roughly equal): a single low-resolution view scores 62.8, several crops 75.7, and crops that overlap, so patches at a crop's border can see their neighbours, 76.9.

What did careful ablation buy?

By the authors' own measures, a lot. In Molmo's human-preference study (pairwise votes turned into Elo ratings), Molmo-72B scores 1077, just behind GPT-4o at 1079 and ahead of Gemini 1.5 Pro (1074) and Claude 3.5 Sonnet (1069); on the average of its 11 academic benchmarks it is highest, 81.2 against 78.5, 78.3 and 76.7. Both of those are the authors' own evaluations. On the independent Chatbot Arena, the paper reports, Molmo-72B outperforms all open models but ranks lower than several proprietary models (e.g. GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet).

What the evidence supports, and what it does not

Solid: how the patch sequence is pooled matters (the Perceiver beats a linear layer and MapNet), and the Perceiver's depth does not; about 64 tokens per image is enough except for reading text, where splitting helps; human spoken captions match GPT-4o's captions and beat ShareGPT4V's; overlapping multi-crop beats a single view.

Confounded: "the LM matters more than the encoder" (unequal steps, different resolutions); cross-attention vs one sequence (depends on freezing, short runs, text-vs-table mismatch); anything compared across tables with different setups.

Step back and notice what every model so far has in common. Whatever the connector, whatever the captions, the recipe is the same: a vision encoder trained one way, a language model trained another, and a bridge bolted between them. The image is always translated into the LM's world at the door. Act VI asks the obvious next question: what if images were words from the start, drawn from the same vocabulary, read and written by one transformer?
