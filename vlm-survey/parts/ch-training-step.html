One training step, end to end

Under their different connectors, most models from here on train the same way: turn the image into tokens, put them in one sequence with the text, and ask the language model to predict the next token. What decides whether that works is the plumbing, which this chapter walks through one stage at a time: which examples are drawn, how they are spliced and batched, which tokens are graded, and which weights are allowed to move.

Two ways to learn from pictures and words

Press A. That is the step from the first chapter: two encoders run side by side, one on the images and one on the captions, and the loss is a table of every image against every caption. Nothing is generated; the model only learns to say which caption goes with which photo. The batch is doing real work here, because every other caption in it serves as a negative. CLIP's batch of 32,768 is part of the method, not just an efficiency choice.

Now press 1 Sample and step through with Next. The generative step is different in kind. Each example becomes one sequence, the language model reads it, and the loss asks it to write the right words. Examples in a batch never see each other: padded rows are independent, and packed rows are masked apart. Apart from how the loss is averaged (see the Molmo note below), the batch size mainly sets how noisy each gradient is and how busy the GPUs are. Everything on the rest of this page, from LLaVA to Qwen3-VL, trains with this second kind of step.

Where the examples come from

A step starts by drawing examples, and even that simple act is a design choice. LLaVA says only that its three kinds of instruction data are uniformly sampled in training, not whether per kind or per example. LLaVA-1.5 concatenates its ten splits, which are then sampled with the same probability; after concatenation that makes every example equally likely, so the big splits dominate. Molmo samples each dataset at a rate proportional to the square root of their size, then adjusts by hand (down-weighting some very large synthetic sets, up-weighting pointing data); the square root alone gives small datasets a larger share than their size would.

Flamingo does not mix inside a batch at all: each update takes a fixed-size batch from every dataset, and the mixture is written into the loss:

\htmlData{step=4, link=sum}{\sum_{m=1}^{M}} \htmlData{step=3, link=lambda}{\lambda_m} \cdot \htmlData{step=1, link=dataset}{\mathbb{E}_{(x, y)\sim \mathcal{D}_m}} \Big[ \htmlData{step=2, link=nll}{\htmlClass{t-red}{-\sum_{\ell=1}^{L} \log p(y_\ell \mid y_{<\ell}, x_{\le \ell})}} \Big]

Eq. 2 in Flamingo v2.

  • \mathcal{D}_m: the m-th of M = 4 datasets (interleaved web pages, two sets of image-text pairs, video-text pairs), the four rows in the lower half of stage 1.
  • The bracket: the usual next-token loss on one example's text, given its earlier text y_{<\ell} and the images before it x_{\le \ell} (the red bars).
  • \lambda_m: how much that dataset's loss counts: 1.0 / 0.2 / 0.2 / 0.03. The paper calls tuning them key to performance.
  • \sum_m: the weighted losses are added into one objective, and one update follows.

In practice the outer sum is a loop: We accumulate gradients over all datasets, one batch from each, before a single optimizer update. In the paper's ablation setup, 1 million gradient computations therefore make only 250,000 gradient updates. The obvious alternative, round-robin, is (in the words of Flamingo's appendix, which compares the same strategies for its contrastive encoder) to alternate batches of each dataset, updating the parameters on each batch. It sounds equivalent, but it scores 70.7 vs 62.9 in Flamingo's ablation, in favour of accumulating. The paper does not say why. One plausible reading (ours): when every update sees all the datasets at once, no single dataset gets to pull the weights its own way for a whole update.

From pixels and words to one sequence

Stages 2 to 4 turn one example into one row. The image side is the previous chapter's connector, run at LLaVA-1.5's size: a 336-pixel square, 14-pixel patches, 576 vectors out of the frozen encoder, each mapped by the connector to the LLM's embedding width. The text side is plain formatting: LLaVA's Table 2 wraps the conversation in a system message and alternating Human: / Assistant: turns, each closed by a <STOP> token (###), with an <image> placeholder in the first human turn. LLaVA's Eq. 2 even randomizes whether the placeholder comes before or after the first question; example A in stage 3 has it after.

Splicing is the moment the two modalities meet: the placeholder is replaced by the visual tokens, and from then on the language model just sees a sequence of vectors. With real-size images the picture dominates that sequence: our three short examples become 603, 617 and 599 tokens long, of which 576 each are the image.

Forming the batch

GPUs process a batch as one rectangular tensor, but the examples have different lengths. Press 5a: the simplest fix pads every row with dummy tokens up to the longest, and a pad mask keeps real tokens from attending to them. Guess how much of the batch is filler, then press 5b. In the toy it is 32 of 150 cells, 21%: every one of those cells is computed on and then thrown away.

Packing removes the filler: lay the examples end to end, then give the attention a block-diagonal mask so that each example can only see its own earlier tokens, as if it were alone. The same three examples fit in 118 cells, and the 32 free cells can take part of the next example. This general recipe is common practice across training code; the papers on this page describe their own variants of it.

At real scale the picture changes in an instructive way. With 576-token images, the same three examples waste only 1.7% to padding, because the image makes every row roughly the same length. Padding bites when lengths really spread. LLaVA-1.5 noticed that its text-only conversations are often longer than its image conversations, so it draws each batch from a single modality, which speeds up the training by 25%. It also shrinks the number of short sequences in the first place: QA pairs from the same training image are merged into a single conversation.

Press 5c for the most interesting variant. Many datasets ask several questions about the same image, and training on each question separately encodes that image again every time. Molmo puts the image once and every question after it, and masks attention so that each annotation's tokens attend to the image tokens, each other, but not to tokens from different annotations. The paper calls this equivalent to training on individual image-text pairs, and it ends up reducing the number of processed images by two-thirds and shortening training time by over half, at the price of only a 25% increase in sequence length for Molmo's data mix (Molmo caps sequences at 2304 tokens). Qwen2-VL, whose images turn into a varying number of tokens (a 224-pixel image becomes 66 tokens), says that images of varying resolutions are packed into a single sequence; the paper states this for inference, in its description of the vision encoder (packing image patches, in the style of NaViT), not for batching the language model's training examples.

Which tokens are graded

Press 6a. The language model reads the row in one pass and, at every position, predicts the next token; causal attention guarantees that no position can peek at its own answer. So every position followed by a word comes with a target, the next token along; positions followed by a visual token have none. Should all of them count? Make your guess, then press 6b.

They should not. The model is being trained as the assistant, so it is graded only on what the assistant says: LLaVA is trained to predict the assistant answers and where to stop. Grade it on the human's words too, and it would spend capacity learning to write questions; the visual tokens are never targets, since they are continuous vectors with no vocabulary entry to predict (the last visual position in example A is graded only because the token it predicts is the ### after the image). Look closely at the paper's Table 2 and you find one surprise: every <STOP> is marked as a target, including the ones that close the system message and the human's turns. The model learns where each turn ends, but never how the human fills it. The ignored positions (padding aside) still matter as context; they just contribute no loss. Over the whole toy batch, 47 of 150 cells (31%) carry loss; keep the same toy text but give each image its real 576 visual tokens and it is 2.5%. The exact figure depends on the toy's short answers, but the direction holds: most of what the model reads, it is never graded on.

Written as a probability, the objective is the chance of the answer given the image and the instructions, built one answer token at a time:

Eq. 3 in LLaVA v2 (the second line, its negative log, is the loss that is minimized).

  • \htmlClass{t-red}{x_i}: the i-th token. The paper colors it as a prediction token and introduces the equation "for a sequence of length L". Read with Table 2, only the prediction tokens (the red targets in stage 6b) contribute factors, so in effect the product runs over those.
  • \htmlClass{t-blue}{X_v}: the image, as its visual tokens. The paper writes it into every factor to stress that every answer is grounded in the image.
  • \htmlClass{t-yellow}{X_{\text{instruct},<i}}: the human's turns before position i; read, never graded.
  • X_{a,<i}: the assistant's own earlier answer tokens, which become context for later ones (in example A, the first answer is context for the second).
  • \theta: the weights being trained, which is where stage 7 comes in.

One subtle trap sits in how this loss is averaged. A batch is split across many GPUs, and if each GPU divides its summed loss by its own count of graded tokens, a GPU that happened to get short answers (like example C's single word) weights each of them more heavily. Molmo divides by the average count across all devices instead, and reports that captioning performance can drop by 0.5-1 points without this fix.

Which weights move

The backward pass sends the gradient of that loss back through everything that produced it, and the training recipe decides which weights actually change. Press 7a. In LLaVA's stage 1 both the encoder and the language model are frozen and only the connector \htmlClass{t-green}{W} learns, on 595K image-text pairs turned into one-turn chats (describe the image; answer: its caption), for 1 epoch with a learning rate of 2e-3 and a batch size of 128. Note what the gradient has to do: the loss sits at the top of the language model, and the connector sits below it, so the gradient must pass through every frozen LLM layer on its way down. Frozen means the weights do not change, not that the gradient stops. Below the connector nothing trains, so the encoder needs no backward pass at all; Flamingo makes the same point for its own frozen encoder: no backpropagation is performed through the frozen Vision Encoder.

Press 7b. Stage 2 unfreezes the language model and trains it together with the connector on the instruction data, for 3 epochs, with a learning rate of 2e-5 and a batch size of 32; the learning rate is a hundred times smaller than in stage 1. Skipping stage 1 was not free for LLaVA: on ScienceQA (fine-tuned on ScienceQA itself for 12 epochs), training without it reaches 85.81% against 90.92% with it. The lesson is not universal: Molmo, which pre-trains all of its weights on its dense captions, writes We find this step unnecessary when pre-training on PixMo-Cap. LLaVA-1.5 keeps the same two stages, with 558K pairs for the first and its 665K mixture for the second, but its connector is the 2-layer MLP drawn in stage 2 rather than the original single matrix \htmlClass{t-green}{W}, and its own schedule is 1 epoch per stage (learning rate 1e-3 and batch 256, then 2e-5 and batch 128).

That is the whole loop: sample, tokenize, splice, batch, mask, and send the gradient back to whatever is allowed to learn. Notice, though, that every image in this chapter's pipeline was a small square, 336 pixels on a side at most. A photo of a dog survives that. A page of a contract, a dense chart or a phone screenshot does not: its words shrink to a few pixels. Act IV is about what happens when the image has to be read, not just recognized.
