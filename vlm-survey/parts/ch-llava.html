Act IIIFrom describing to conversing

Chat needs conversations to learn from

Flamingo and BLIP-2 learned from captions and web pages, not from instructions. Flamingo can be prompted into multi-image dialogues, but in LLaVA's side-by-side tests BLIP-2 and OpenFlamingo (an open-source version of Flamingo) mostly describe the scene when given an open request ("What is unusual about this image?", "Can you explain this meme in detail?"). LLaVA's bet was that the bridge can be almost trivially simple if the training data is the right kind: conversations about images, written for it by a language model that was never shown the pictures.

The wall: nobody had written the conversations

By 2023 the web had offered up billions of image-caption pairs, and the previous chapters showed how to learn from them. A caption, though, is one flat sentence. It never asks a follow-up question, never explains its reasoning, never answers "why". A model trained only on captions learns to caption. What a visual assistant needs is data in the shape of the task: a person asks something about an image, an assistant answers, and the exchange goes on. Collecting that from human annotators at scale is slow and expensive, so almost none existed.

A model that cannot see writes the data

LLaVA's trick was to have GPT-4, used as a text-only model, write the conversations. GPT-4 never looks at the pixels; as the paper's caption puts it, the visual image is not used to prompt GPT. Instead each COCO image is described to it in words, two ways: the human-written captions COCO already has, and the list of labeled bounding boxes, each an object name with four normalized coordinates. From that text, GPT-4 invents what an assistant who could see the image would say. For each kind of output the authors wrote a few examples by hand, which the paper calls the only human annotations we have during data collection, and passed them to GPT-4 as in-context demonstrations.

This produced 158K training samples: 58K multi-turn conversations, 23K detailed descriptions and 77K complex-reasoning answers. The kind of data mattered more than any architecture change in this chapter. On the paper's own chat benchmark, where GPT-4 grades LLaVA's answers against its own text-only answers, the full mix scores 85.1. Conversations alone score 73.8, and the same model with no instruction tuning at all scores 21.5. Fine-tuned on the ScienceQA benchmark, LLaVA alone reaches 90.92%; the headline 92.53% is LLaVA combined with GPT-4 as a judge, above the previous best of 91.68%.

The simplest possible bridge

With the data taken care of, the connector could be nearly trivial. LLaVA takes the frozen CLIP ViT-L/14 encoder from the first chapter, reads out its grid of patch features, and multiplies every patch vector by one learned matrix. That is the whole bridge, a simple linear layer, and it outputs vectors the same size as the language model's word embeddings, so to the language model each patch looks like one more word:

\htmlData{step=3, link=tokens}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{H_v}} = \htmlData{step=2, link=proj}{\htmlClass{t-green}{W}} \cdot \htmlData{step=1, link=grid}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{Z_v}}, \qquad \htmlData{step=1, link=grid}{\htmlClass{t-blue}{Z_v} = g(X_v)}

Eq. 1 in LLaVA v2.

  • X_v: the input image.
  • g: the frozen CLIP vision encoder, and \htmlClass{t-blue}{Z_v} its grid of patch features: the blue grid in the figure, one vector per patch.
  • \htmlClass{t-green}{W}: the trainable projection matrix, the green box. The same matrix is applied to every patch.
  • \htmlClass{t-blue}{H_v}: the resulting visual tokens, one per patch, now the width of a word embedding: the blue part of the sequence bar.

Which layer of the encoder to read turned out to matter a little. LLaVA uses the grid features from just before the encoder's last layer; on ScienceQA, the last layer instead gives 89.96% against 90.92%. The paper's explanation is that the final layer summarizes the image globally for CLIP's matching game, while the layer before still describes local detail.

How many tokens is a picture?

Every connector on this page answers the same question differently: how does the image reach the language model, and how many vectors does it cost? Press the four models in turn.

Flamingo's resampler squeezes any grid into 64 latents, and those latents never enter the text sequence at all. New cross-attention layers (green) interleaved with the frozen language model's layers read them, so the sequence stays as long as the text. BLIP-2's Q-Former uses 32 queries, and their outputs are prepended to the text as a short soft prompt. LLaVA does no squeezing: every patch becomes a token, 16 × 16 = 256 of them at 224 pixels, sitting in the sequence right next to the words.

Now the prediction. LLaVA-1.5 moves to the 336-pixel CLIP encoder, a 1.5 times wider image. Press it: the grid goes from 16 to 24 patches per side, and the token count goes from 256 to 24 × 24 = 576, 2.25 times as many, because tokens grow with the square of the width. Press Compare all four for the whole picture. The trade is plain: LLaVA hands the language model everything the encoder saw and lets its full self-attention decide what matters, at the price of a sequence that the image mostly fills. The recipes chapter returns to whether squeezing or keeping every patch is better, once someone tested it carefully.

LLaVA-1.5: three small edits

Half a year later, the same group asked what the simplest changes could buy. Three turned out to matter. The linear layer becomes a two-layer MLP (the paper cites the same switch helping in self-supervised learning; it does not state the activation). The encoder becomes the 336-pixel CLIP, which is the 576-token version above. And the training mix adds academic question-answering datasets such as VQAv2 and GQA.

That last edit has a catch. Academic benchmarks want one-word answers ("yellow"), while chat data wants full sentences, and a model trained on both gets confused about which to give when. LLaVA-1.5's fix is to say which one you want, in plain words, at the end of every short-answer question: Answer the question using a single word or phrase. Multiple-choice questions get Answer with the option's letter from the given choices directly. The paper shows the effect on a model that has only finished alignment pretraining: asked about a shirt's color with a normal prompt it says "The man is wearing a yellow shirt."; with the formatting prompt appended it says "Yellow."

Two rows are big jumps: adding VQAv2 lifts MME by almost 400 points, and adding GQA's own training data lifts GQA by more than 10 points. The other changes are smaller, and the final model comes from stacking them all. The whole recipe is small: 558K image-caption pairs to align the connector, then a 665K-example instruction mix, 1.2M publicly available examples in total, trained in ~1 day on a single 8-A100 node (~6 hours of pretraining and ~20 hours of visual instruction tuning). With that, the paper reports state-of-the-art across 11 benchmarks, against models trained on hundreds of millions of pairs.

Inside LLaVA: forward, then backward

The connector figure showed how many tokens a picture costs; here is the whole training step around it, with the tensor shapes at every stage. The variant buttons switch between the two papers and between their two training stages, which differ architecturally in one thing: which boxes are allowed to learn (the data, learning rate, batch and epochs differ too).

The step counts show how small this recipe is. LLaVA's stage 1 is about 4,650 updates and its stage 2 about 14,800; LLaVA-1.5's are about 2,180 and about 5,200, with larger batches and a single epoch (more data only in stage 2: 558K vs 595K in stage 1). One inconsistency: LLaVA-1.5 says the authors keep the training iterations and batch size roughly the same for instruction tuning as LLaVA, yet its own table (batch 128, 1 epoch over 665K) gives the counts above, about a third of LLaVA's; and "the same set of hyperparameters" quoted in stage 8 holds only loosely, since batch sizes and epochs differ too. Stage 1 is cheap for another reason too: it updates only the projector, a single d_{\text{LLM}} \times 1024 matrix in LLaVA, though every one of its steps still pays for a full forward and backward pass through the frozen language model.

So a LLaVA-style model is three parts (a frozen encoder, a small learned bridge, a language model) plus a pile of image conversations. But how does a training step actually turn an image and a conversation into a gradient? What exactly is in a batch, which tokens is the model graded on, and which weights move?
