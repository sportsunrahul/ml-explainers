Seeing in tokens

How images are compressed into latents and discrete codes (VAEs, VQ-VAE, VQGAN), how those codes feed image generators, and how vision-language models connect a vision encoder to a language model so it can read pictures. This guide bridges diffusion models and LLMs.

1

Why turn images into tokens?

A 1024×1024 colour image has over three million numbers, most of them redundant. Generative models and language models both work far better on a compact representation. There are three consumers of that representation:

Latent diffusion needs a continuous latent that is small but decodes back to a sharp image. Autoregressive and masked image generators need discrete tokens from a codebook, so they can predict them like words. Vision-language models need embeddings that carry meaning, usually from a CLIP-style encoder, which an LLM can read alongside text. The first two come from autoencoders trained to reconstruct; the third comes from encoders trained to understand. Much current research tries to get one tokenizer that does all three.

2

Autoencoders and VAEs

An autoencoder has an encoder that squeezes an image into a small latent z and a decoder that reconstructs the image from z. Trained only to reconstruct, its latent space has gaps: points between training examples decode to garbage, so you can't sample new images from it.

The variational autoencoder (Kingma and Welling, 2013) fixes this. The encoder outputs a Gaussian distribution q(z|x) with mean μ and standard deviation σ rather than a single point, and training maximises the evidence lower bound (ELBO): reconstruct well, while keeping each q(z|x) close to a standard normal prior.

The reparameterisation trick writes the sample as μ + σ·ε with ε ~ N(0, I), so gradients flow through μ and σ. β = 1 is the standard VAE; β-VAE turns it up for smoother, more disentangled latents at the cost of blurrier reconstructions.Hover or tap a term to see it in the figure below.

The KL term pulls every encoding toward the origin and widens it, filling the gaps so any sample from the prior decodes to something plausible. Push it too hard and different images overlap, so reconstructions blur, a classic VAE weakness.

VQ-VAE: discrete codes

van den Oord et al. (VQ-VAE, 2017) made the latent discrete. The encoder produces a grid of vectors; each is replaced by its nearest entry in a learned codebook of K vectors. An image becomes a grid of integers, exactly the kind of sequence a Transformer can model.

sg is stop-gradient. The second term moves codes toward encoder outputs (the codebook loss); the third keeps the encoder committed to its code (the commitment loss). Rounding to the nearest code has no gradient, so the straight-through estimator copies the decoder's gradient straight to the encoder.

The big practical problem is codebook collapse: many codes are never chosen, get no updates, and stay dead, wasting capacity. Fixes include updating codes by an exponential moving average of their assigned vectors (a running k-means), re-initialising dead codes to random encoder outputs, and using low-dimensional ℓ2-normalised codes (ViT-VQGAN).

4

VQGAN: sharp tokens

Pixel-wise reconstruction losses reward blur, because an average of plausible images has lower squared error than any single sharp guess. Esser et al. (VQGAN, “Taming Transformers”, 2021) added a perceptual loss (distance between deep network features, LPIPS) and a patch-based adversarial loss from a discriminator. That let the tokenizer compress much more aggressively (16× per side, so a 256×256 image becomes 16×16 = 256 tokens) while decoding realistic textures. A GPT-style Transformer then learned to generate the token grid. The same autoencoder recipe, with a continuous KL latent instead of codes, became the Stable Diffusion VAE.

Below, a toy tokenizer cuts the picture into patches and assigns each patch to one of K codebook entries by k-means on its average colour. A real VQGAN uses a learned encoder, so each code stands for a whole texture pattern rather than a colour, but the bookkeeping is the same.

Tokenizers in practice

Every tokenizer trades three things: compression (fewer tokens is cheaper to generate), reconstruction (how faithful the decoded image is) and generability (how easy the latent is for a generator to model; a perfectly reconstructing but chaotic latent can be hard to learn). Continuous latents reconstruct best; discrete tokens suit next-token prediction.

6

Generating images from tokens

With discrete tokens, image generation becomes language modelling. DALL·E (2021) generated 1,024 image tokens after the text tokens with one Transformer. Parti (2022) scaled a ViT-VQGAN plus encoder–decoder Transformer to 20B parameters. MaskGIT (2022) predicts all tokens in parallel and keeps the most confident ones each round, needing only about 8 steps instead of 256. LlamaGen (2024) showed a plain Llama-style model with a good tokenizer competes with diffusion, and VAR (2024) predicts images coarse to fine, one resolution scale at a time. Diffusion on continuous latents still leads on quality for most tasks, but token-based generators fit naturally into multimodal LLMs that output images as well as text.

7

Vision-language models

A vision-language model (VLM) answers questions about images, reads documents and charts, and holds conversations about what it sees. Almost all share three parts: a vision encoder (usually a CLIP or SigLIP ViT), a connector that turns its features into something the LLM can consume, and a pretrained LLM. They differ mostly in the connector.

Flamingo (DeepMind, 2022) kept the LLM frozen, compressed each image to 64 tokens with a Perceiver resampler, and inserted new gated cross-attention layers between the LLM's layers, which made it strong at few-shot learning from interleaved image-text documents. BLIP-2 (2023) trained a small Q-Former whose 32 learned queries extract the image information most relevant to text. LLaVA (2023) did the simplest thing: a linear layer (an MLP in LLaVA-1.5) maps every ViT patch feature into the LLM's embedding space, and those visual tokens are simply placed before the text. Its training had two stages: align the projector on about 600K captioned images with everything else frozen, then fine-tune projector and LLM on about 158K GPT-4-generated visual conversations. That recipe became the template for most open VLMs. Early-fusion models such as Chameleon (2024) instead tokenize images with a VQ tokenizer and train one Transformer on interleaved discrete image and text tokens from scratch, so it can also generate images. Transfusion (2024) mixes next-token prediction for text with diffusion for images in one model.

8

Resolution and token budgets

A ViT-L/14 at 336 pixels produces 24×24 = 576 patch tokens. That's too coarse for small text in documents and charts, but raising resolution grows tokens quadratically. AnyRes tiling (LLaVA-NeXT) cuts a large image into encoder-sized tiles plus a low-resolution thumbnail for global context. Native resolution approaches (NaViT, Qwen2-VL) let the ViT accept any size and aspect ratio directly, packing variable numbers of patches, and Qwen2-VL adds 2D- and time-aware rotary positions (M-RoPE) for images and video. To keep the LLM's input manageable, models merge neighbouring patches (pixel shuffle, 2×2 pooling) or resample to a fixed number of tokens. Video multiplies the problem by the number of frames, so frame sampling and token compression are active research areas.

9

Evaluation and failure modes

Common benchmarks include VQAv2 (everyday visual questions), TextVQA, DocVQA and ChartQA (reading), MMMU (college-level multimodal reasoning) and MathVista. The best-known failure is object hallucination: describing things that aren't in the image because the language model's priors override weak visual evidence (the POPE benchmark probes this with yes/no questions). VLMs also struggle with counting, precise spatial relations, fine-grained comparisons between images, and small details lost to resizing. Much progress has come from better data: dense human-written descriptions, synthetic document and chart data, and grounding data that links phrases to boxes.

What works well

  • Reusing strong pretrained parts (CLIP-style encoders, LLMs) makes VLMs cheap to build.
  • Tokenizers let the same Transformer machinery handle images, video and audio.
  • Latent autoencoders cut the cost of diffusion by orders of magnitude.
  • VLMs read documents, screenshots and charts well enough to power agents.

What is still hard

  • Hallucinated objects and details, and overconfidence about what is visible.
  • Encoders trained for understanding reconstruct poorly, and vice versa, so unified models juggle two representations.
  • Token budgets: high resolution and long video overwhelm context windows.
  • Counting, spatial reasoning and fine-grained perception lag far behind language skills.
