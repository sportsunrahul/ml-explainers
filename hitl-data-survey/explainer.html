The loopLabels are the scarce input

Two decisions in every feedback loop

Picture the platform from the model's side. Images arrive all day from cameras, the model scores each one, and a person can label only a few of them. Every label costs a person's time, and not every label comes back right.

So every round of the loop makes two decisions. The first is which images go to a person. The second is which of the returned labels the model should believe. Part one of this page is about the first decision, and part two is about the second.

Each decision has a naive default: send a random sample, and believe every label. The rest of the page is the case for and against doing better than those two defaults. The case is not one-sided. Random sampling turns out to be a much stronger default than its reputation (chapter 5). Believing every label turns out to be a much weaker one: a large network will learn any label you give it, wrong ones included (chapter 7), and even famous test sets carry label errors (chapter 8).

Before reading on, make a guess. If you could label only one image in a hundred, which one would you pick? Most people answer "the one the model is least sure about". The next chapter builds exactly that, and the three after it show where it breaks.

The labeling decision

Write down your two current defaults: how images are picked for review today, and whether any returned label is ever doubted. Every chapter ends with one decision like this one, stated for a platform with a human in the loop.

Part oneChoosing what to label

Ask about the coin flips

Start with the simplest idea. The model already outputs probabilities, and where it is close to a coin flip, a label should teach it the most.

This is pool-based active learning. You hold a small labeled set and a large pool of unlabeled images. Each round you train, score every image in the pool, send the top few to a person (the papers call that person the oracle), add the new labels, and train again. The number of labels you can afford is the budget.

Everything depends on the score. The model's softmax output for one image is a probability vector, and with three classes that vector is a point in a triangle: a corner means "certainly this class", the center means "no idea". Hover over the pool to see where each image lands. There are three standard ways to squeeze that vector into one "how unsure" number, all collected in Settles' survey of active learning:

With two classes all three pick the same image, the one whose probability is closest to 0.5. That is the original rule of uncertainty sampling, from Lewis and Gale's 1994 text classifier. With more classes they disagree, and the triangle shows how: entropy is highest only at the very center, while margin is high along the whole line between two corners.

Now press the round buttons. Over the 20 pools the uncertainty run is ahead in the middle: margin sampling reaches 98.6% accuracy with 23 labels, where random sampling needs 48 for the same. The labels pile up along the boundaries between the arms, which is where a classifier's mistakes live. But look at the first round. With 8 labels, entropy sampling is behind random (58% against 73% at the median). With three labels the model's idea of "the boundary" is wrong, and it confidently asks about the wrong place. Chapter 5 comes back to this cold start.

Uncertainty sampling pays most where random sampling is weakest: rare classes. If a defect shows up in one image in a thousand, a random batch of a hundred images usually contains none of them, while a model that is unsure about a defect-like image asks about it. Lewis and Gale reported needing 500-fold fewer labels than random sampling for some rare text categories.

Three warnings travel with this method, and each gets a chapter. A softmax probability is often over-confident, so "unsure" is measured with a bent ruler. The score cannot tell an image that is ambiguous to everyone from an image the model has simply not learned yet (chapter 3). And the top five picks of one round often sit next to each other, so the batch wastes labels on near-copies (chapter 4).

The labeling decision

Once the model has seen a handful of labels per class, send the images whose top two classes are closest (the margin score), a few at a time, and retrain between rounds. Use it first where a class is rare: that is where random review wastes the most.

Ambiguous or unfamiliar? MC dropout and BALD

"Unsure" comes in two kinds. An ambiguous image (a blurred part, a borderline scratch) stays a coin flip however many labels you buy; an unfamiliar image is one the model has not learned yet, and one label fixes it.

Entropy cannot tell these two apart. Both images get a mean prediction near the center of the triangle, so both sit at the top of the dome. To separate them, the model needs to know how sure it is about itself.

Here is the Bayesian view in one paragraph. Many different weight settings fit the labels you have. If they all predict the same thing for an image, the data has settled the question. If they predict different things, the data has not, and a label for that image would rule some of them out. Gal and Ghahramani (2015 on arXiv, ICML 2016) showed a cheap way to draw such weight settings: keep dropout switched on at test time. Each forward pass drops a different random set of units, so each pass is a slightly different network. Run TTT passes and you get TTT predictions for one image: the blue points on the dome.

BALD (Bayesian Active Learning by Disagreement, Houlsby et al. 2011) turns that cloud into one score:

  • H[y∣x,D]\mathbb{H}[y \mid x, \mathcal{D}]H[y∣x,D]: the entropy of the average prediction. In the figure, the dome's height above the mean point.
  • E H[y∣x,ω]\mathbb{E}\,\mathbb{H}[y \mid x, \omega]EH[y∣x,ω]: the average entropy of the individual passes. In the figure, the average height of the blue points.

The dome is curved downward, so the average of the heights always sits below the height of the average. The gap is large only when the passes are individually confident but point to different corners: the model disagrees with itself. For the ambiguous image every pass says "a third each", every point sits at the top of the dome, and the gap closes. The label would cost the same, and teach nothing.

The labeling decision

Do not spend labels on images that are ambiguous to everyone: route them to a guideline decision instead ("where exactly is the class boundary?"). Send the images on which the model's own dropout passes, or ensemble members, disagree.

A batch of near-duplicates

Retraining after every single label is too slow, so real systems label in batches. The top ten most uncertain images of one round are often ten frames of the same confusing moment.

Uncertainty scores each image on its own, so near-copies get near-identical scores and are picked together. The second copy of a frame teaches almost nothing the first did not. Sener and Savarese (2018) turned the question around: instead of "which images are hard?", ask "which batch makes every unlabeled image close to some labeled one?" Distance is measured in the network's own feature space (the last fully connected layer).

Sener and Savarese (2018), Eq. 5, the k-Center problem.

  • s0\mathbf{s}^0s0: the images already labeled; s1\mathbf{s}^1s1: the new batch, at most bbb images.
  • The inner part is the covering radius δ\deltaδ: the distance from the worst-covered pool image to its nearest labeled image (the yellow discs).

Their Theorem 1 explains why this is the right target: the gap between the loss on the labeled subset and the loss on the whole pool is bounded by a term proportional to δ\deltaδ, plus a term that shrinks with the pool size. The exact problem is NP-Hard, but the greedy rule (always add the farthest point) gets within a factor of 2 of the best radius and runs in seconds.

Diversity alone is not the end of the story. BADGE (Ash et al., 2020) compared methods over 33 settings of dataset, batch size and architecture, and scored each method by how often it lost significantly to another (lower is better). Its averages: BADGE 0.66, margin 1.56, least confidence 5.93, random 6.01, core-set 6.9, entropy 8.01. Pure diversity and pure entropy both averaged worse than random. The fixes that held up mix the two signals. BADGE embeds each image by the gradient it would cause (large when uncertain) and spreads the batch over those embeddings. BatchBALD scores a whole batch jointly. Stochastic batch acquisition samples the batch in proportion to the scores instead of taking the top-k, at the cost of top-k itself: 0.2 +- 0.0 seconds per acquisition against 566.0 +- 17.4 for BatchBALD in Kirsch et al.'s Table 1.

The labeling decision

Never send a plain top-k batch from a video feed. Remove near-duplicates first, then spread the batch in feature space (or sample it in proportion to the uncertainty scores). One frame per moment is enough.

Why random is hard to beat

Many active-learning papers report large gains over random sampling. Two careful re-runs found most of those gains shrink, and sometimes flip, once the comparison is made fair.

Three things make random sampling hard to beat.

A tuned model gains more than clever selection. Munjal et al. re-ran the popular methods in one code base, retuned every model at every round, and repeated each run over 5 initial labeled sets and 5 seeds. With only 10% of CIFAR-10 labeled, adding RandAugment and stochastic weight averaging to a random-sampling baseline moved its accuracy from 69.16% to 82.16%. That one change is larger than the lead any selection rule showed over random in the study. Press the second button. In a transfer test (batches chosen by a VGG16, then a strongly regularized ResNet-18 trained on them), the best method led random by only 0.7 to 1.8 points, which is the size of the run-to-run spread the authors report, "A standard deviation of 1-2% in accuracy among different AL methods, indicating that out of chance, it is possible to achieve seemingly better results".

Claim card: Munjal et al. (2022)

Claim
Under identical, well-tuned training, active-learning methods give small and inconsistent gains over random sampling, and strong regularization shrinks them further.
How measured
CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 (plus limited ImageNet runs at a 5% budget); on CIFAR, 10% initial labels, 10% more per round up to 40%; 25 models per method; hyperparameters re-searched at every round; non-parametric significance tests.
Result
No method beat random in every setting. In a transfer test with a strongly regularized ResNet-18, the best method's lead over random was 0.7 to 1.8 points.
Would have falsified it
One method beating a strongly regularized random baseline by more than the seed spread, across datasets, budgets and architectures.

Other careful re-runs agree. Mittal et al. found that with modern augmentation "all the AL methods show similar performance within a range of 0.4%" on CIFAR-10: the methods became hard to tell apart from each other, though they still beat random. Beck et al. are more positive: "AL techniques are 2× to 4× more label-efficient compared to RS with the use of data augmentation". The honest summary is that active learning helps, by less than early papers claimed, and only against a baseline that was given the same care.

At a tiny budget, the right pick is the opposite of uncertain. Chapter 2's toy already showed this: with three labels, uncertainty sampling asked about the wrong places. Hacohen, Dekel and Weinshall explain why with the model on the left. Split the data into an easy, typical region and a hard, rare one. With few labels, each extra label in the typical region removes more error, because that region is most of the data and is learned fastest; once it is learned, labels in the hard region pay more. Drag the sliders: the switch point moves, but it always exists. Their method, TypiClust, embeds the pool with self-supervised learning, clusters it, and labels the most typical image of each uncovered cluster, where typicality is the inverse of the mean distance to the K=20K = 20K=20 nearest neighbours. With 10 labels on CIFAR-10 and semi-supervised training, it reached 93.2%, a gain of 39.4% over random selection.

Claim card: Hacohen, Dekel and Weinshall (2022)

Claim
The best query strategy flips with the budget: typical images at low budgets, atypical (uncertain) images at high budgets.
How measured
A two-region theory (Eq. 2, Theorem 1), then accuracy minus random against the number of labels in supervised, self-supervised-embedding and semi-supervised training.
Result
With a handful of labels per class, "all other baseline AL methods perform on par with random selection or worse"; TypiClust leads. Uncertainty methods overtake it only at larger budgets.
Would have falsified it
Uncertainty sampling beating random at one to ten labels per class, or typical-image selection still winning at large budgets.

A selected set is a biased set. Settles' survey puts it plainly: with active learning, "the labeled instances are a biased distribution, not drawn i.i.d. from the underlying natural density". The selected images over-represent the boundaries the model found hard. That is what you want for training. It follows (our inference) that it is exactly what you do not want for measuring accuracy. A validation set built from actively selected images reports accuracy on the hardest cases of the model that chose them, not on the stream your users see.

The labeling decision

For a new camera or product (one to ten labels per class), label the most typical image of each cluster of an unlabeled embedding, not the most uncertain. Switch to uncertainty sampling once the labeled set is in the thousands. Before you buy an active-learning pipeline, compare it to random sampling trained with strong augmentation over several seeds. And always label a random slice of the stream for the evaluation set.

One labeling round, end to end

Part one in one place: every step of a single round, with the compute and labeling cost each step adds. Edit the settings to see the bill change.

Each step links back to where it was argued:

  1. Pool. Every unlabeled image the system could ask about.
  2. Score. TTT stochastic forward passes per image give the probabilities for margin, entropy or BALD (chapter 2, chapter 3). With T=1T = 1T=1 only margin and entropy are available.
  3. Pick. Top scores, spread in feature space, or the most typical image per cluster at cold start (chapter 4, chapter 5).
  4. Remove near-duplicates. One frame per moment.
  5. Send to labelers. kkk votes per image cost kkk times as much, and buy a way to spot disagreement (chapter 8).
  6. Aggregate. Majority vote, or a model of each labeler's accuracy.
  7. Split. Selected images go to training. A separate random slice of the stream, labeled with more votes, is the evaluation set (chapter 5).
  8. Retrain and evaluate on the random slice only, and check the new labels for errors before the next round (chapter 9, chapter 12).

Part twoTraining when labels are wrong

Clean first, wrong later

A network trained on a mix of right and wrong labels learns the consistent pattern first and memorizes the exceptions later. For a while, its own loss tells you which labels are wrong; after that window closes, it cannot.

Start with how bad it can get. Zhang et al. (2017) replaced every CIFAR-10 label with a random one and trained standard networks anyway: "when trained on a completely random labeling of the true data, neural networks achieve 0 training error." An Inception network fit the random labels to 100.0% training accuracy, with 9.78% test accuracy: chance. A big network has room for any labeling, so training accuracy says nothing about whether the labels are right.

Arpit et al. (2017) asked what happens on the way there. With part of the labels randomized, validation accuracy peaks early and then falls: "Thus the model first learns the simple and general patterns of the real data before fitting the noise (which results in decreasing validation accuracy)." The images that share a pattern pull the weights the same way; each wrong label pulls alone, so it is learned last.

Our run shows the same thing in detail. Test accuracy peaks at 77.14% at epoch 12, then falls to a mean of 60.58% over the last ten epochs. At epoch 10, the network predicts 75.9% of the mislabeled training images as their true class: it disagrees with the label it is trained on. Only 4.8% are fit to the wrong label. By epoch 30 that share is 98.2%. In the ridge, the red histogram sits far to the right of the green one early, then slides on top of it.

That window is the signal every method in part two uses. Keep the 60% of images with the smallest loss at epoch 10 and 93.3% of what you keep has a correct label; do the same at epoch 200 and you keep 59.0%, no better than the labels themselves.

Claim card: Arpit et al. (2017)

Claim
Networks fit simple, shared patterns before they memorize noise; regularization slows the memorization.
How measured

Result

Would have falsified it
Validation accuracy that does not peak before the noise is fit, or clean and noisy examples learned at the same rate.

One caution from real data. Labels flipped by a random number generator are the easy case. Wei et al. collected real human labels for CIFAR-10 and found that "deep neural nets memorize features more easily when learning with real-world human annotations than synthetic ones." Human mistakes cluster on images that genuinely look like the other class, so they sit closer to the clean distribution and the window is narrower.

The labeling decision

Never read training accuracy as a sign of label quality. If you want the model to point at suspect labels, read the per-image loss early in training (around the validation peak), or from a model that never trained on those labels (chapter 9), not from the final model.

How noisy are real datasets?

Synthetic noise is a lab setting. The real question is how many labels are wrong in datasets people actually use, including the test sets that decide which model ships.

Northcutt, Athalye and Mueller checked the test sets of ten popular benchmarks. They let confident learning (chapter 9) flag likely errors, then showed the flagged examples (a random sample of them for the two largest datasets) to five crowd workers next to the given label and the suggested one. An example counted as an error when fewer than three of the five workers accepted its given label. Their headline: "we estimate an average of at least 3.3% errors across the 10 datasets", and "label errors comprise at least 6% of the ImageNet validation set". On ImageNet, 5,440 flags led to 2,916 confirmed errors. Not every flag was an error: "51% of the algorithmically-flagged candidates are indeed erroneously labeled, on average across the datasets".

Now the part that matters for model selection. On the images whose label is wrong, a model scores well by reproducing the wrong label, and big models are best at that. Press the second button. Against the corrected labels, ResNet-18 rises from rank 34 to rank 1, and NASNet-Large falls from rank 1 to 29. On the full validation set the ranking barely moves, because errors are a few percent of it. But if your test set were a little noisier, the authors found, "on ImageNet with corrected labels: ResNet-18 outperforms ResNet-50 if the prevalence of originally mislabeled test examples increases by just 6%".

Claim card: Northcutt, Athalye and Mueller (2021)

Claim
Label errors are common in benchmark test sets, and they can change which model looks best.
How measured

Result

Would have falsified it
Workers rejecting most flags, or model rankings on the correctable set staying the same under corrected labels.

Crowd labels are noisier still before anyone checks them. Wei et al. had three crowd workers label every CIFAR-10 training image. A single crowd label per image was wrong 17.23% of the time; a majority vote of three cut that to 9.03%; and taking, for each image, any of the three labels that was wrong gives a worst case of 40.21%.

The labeling decision

Audit the evaluation set before you trust a model comparison. Label evaluation images with more votes than training images, re-check the ones a model confidently disagrees with, and report accuracy on the corrected set. A leaderboard built on unchecked labels can favor the model that best copies your labelers' mistakes.

Confident learning finds the wrong labels

Let a model that never saw an image's label vote on it. Where its confident vote disagrees with the label, the label is a suspect; counting those disagreements also tells you how labels got flipped.

Two ideas make this work. First, the probabilities must be out of sample. A model that trained on an image's label has partly memorized it (chapter 7) and will defend it. So split the data into k = 4 folds, train on three, and predict the fourth, four times over.

Second, each class gets its own threshold, because a model is not equally sure of every class. Ours is confident about ships and frogs and hesitant about cats, so a fixed cut-off would flag every hard class and miss the easy ones:

Compare the two matrices. The confident joint puts its biggest off-diagonal counts in exactly the five pairs we flipped. It estimates 12,196 errors against the true 10,065. Now click the cells. In the automobile-to-truck cell, 97.5% of the flagged images really are mislabeled, and in horse-to-deer 98.6%. Overall, our flags were 55.7% precise and found 61.0% of the errors; flagging every image where the model's top class differs from the label gave 48.5% precision and 66.4% recall. The paper, with a ResNet-50 at 40% noise, reports 78% precision and 91% recall. Our small CNN is right on only 77.7% of held-out images, and the flags can only be as good as the model that votes. Trained on the cleaned data, the paper's CIFAR-10 models reach 86.7% test accuracy at 40% noise against 60.2% for plain training (asymmetric noise from a random transition matrix, sparsity 0). The method assumes noise depends on the class, not on the individual image; the library that implements it is cleanlab.

The labeling decision

Before each training round, run k-fold predictions over the labeled set and send the off-diagonal images of the confident joint back to review, most suspicious first. Treat them as candidates, not verdicts. Read the largest off-diagonal cells as a report on your labeling guide: they name the class pairs your labelers confuse.

Two networks check each other

Chapter 7 showed that early in training a small loss usually means a correct label. Co-teaching trains only on the small-loss part of each batch, and adds one twist: two networks pick for each other.

Why two networks? One network that picks its own small-loss images trains on its own mistakes: a wrong label it happens to fit gets a small loss, gets kept, and gets fit harder. Two networks with different random starts make different mistakes, so each filters some of the other's. Press "Plain training": with one network, by epoch 200 every flipped image has a small loss, and the sort tells you nothing.

In our run, the clean share of network fff's kept set rose from 60.3% in epoch 1 to 92.9% in epoch 10, then slipped to 88.0% by epoch 200. Over the last ten epochs Co-teaching averaged 78.56% test accuracy, against 60.58% for plain training on the same labels. The paper's own CIFAR-10 numbers bracket ours: 74.02% +/- 0.04% at 50% noise and 82.32% +/- 0.07% at 20%, against 48.87% +/- 0.52% and 76.25% +/- 0.28% for standard training. The slow slide in precision is what the follow-up Co-teaching+ attacked: as training goes on, the two networks come to agree, and the peer check weakens.

Inside Co-teaching: forward, then backward

The labeling decision

You do not have to wait for the review queue to empty before you train. Train with small-loss selection, set the drop rate from an estimate of the noise rate (chapter 9 gives one), and send the images that both networks keep dropping to review.

Drop the label, keep the image

Co-teaching throws away the images it doubts, and their pixels are still worth learning from. DivideMix drops only the doubtful label and keeps the image as unlabeled data for semi-supervised learning.

The division step is simple. Take each training image's loss, fit a mixture of two Gaussians to all of them, and read off, for each image, the probability that it belongs to the low-loss bump:

Move the epoch buttons. At epoch 10 the flipped labels form their own high-loss bump and the fit separates them well. By epoch 30 the network has memorized them (chapter 7) and both bumps collapse onto zero. DivideMix fights this from both sides: it trains for only a short warm-up (10 epochs for CIFAR-10 and 30 epochs for CIFAR-100) before it starts dividing, and it never trains on a label it has doubted.

The unlabeled images are not wasted. For each one, both networks predict on two augmentations, the four predictions are averaged and sharpened into a guessed label ("co-guessing"), and all images, labeled and unlabeled, are mixed pairwise with MixMatch. As in Co-teaching, there are two networks, and each one divides the data for the other: "the GMM for one network is used to divide training data for the other network".

The results were a large step. On CIFAR-10 with 50% symmetric noise, plain cross-entropy reaches 79.4% at its best epoch and 57.9% at the end; DivideMix reaches 94.6% and 94.4%. With 40% asymmetric noise it reaches 93.4% best and 92.1% last. Read "best" with care: it is "the best test accuracy across all epochs", which uses the test set to pick the epoch; "last" is "the averaged test accuracy over the last 10 epochs". Later work such as ELR reports numbers selected on a held-out validation set instead. Also note that DivideMix's "symmetric" noise draws the new label from all classes, so some "flipped" labels stay correct, unlike the Co-teaching definition used in our runs.

Inside DivideMix: forward, then backward

The labeling decision

When a batch of labels is unreliable (crowd work, auto-labels from an older model), keep the images even where you doubt the labels; semi-supervised training can still use them. The images whose clean probability sits near 0.5 are where a human label is worth the most: send those to review first.

Closing the loopBack to the person

Which labels to re-check first

Re-checking labels is active learning again, applied to images that already have a label. The reviewer's time is the budget, and the order of the queue decides how much of it is wasted.

The naive order is random, and it fixes errors at the rate they occur: 9.9% of the flipped labels after 5,000 reviews. Sorting by confident learning's normalized margin fixes 25.3% with the same effort.

Bernhardt et al. (2022) add a second idea. Some images are not mislabeled but genuinely ambiguous; a reviewer looks, hesitates, and the image needs several more votes before a majority forms. Their score puts clear errors first and ambiguous images last:

Φ(x,l^;θ)=CE(l^,pθ)−H(pθ)\htmlData{step=1, link=phi}{\Phi(\mathbf{x}, \hat{\mathbf{l}}; \theta)} = \htmlData{step=2, link=phi}{\mathrm{CE}(\hat{\mathbf{l}}, p_\theta)} - \htmlData{step=3, link=phi}{\mathrm{H}(p_\theta)}Φ(x,l^;θ)=CE(l^,pθ​)−H(pθ​)

Bernhardt et al. (2022), Eq. 2. CE\mathrm{CE}CE: cross-entropy of the current label counts l^\hat{\mathbf{l}}l^ against the model's prediction (high when the label looks wrong). H\mathrm{H}H: entropy of the prediction (high when the image is ambiguous). This is the ambiguity term that BALD also subtracts (chapter 3); both scores push ambiguous images down the queue.

In their simulation, with several noisy annotators per image, even the plainly trained (vanilla) selector reached 90% correct labels with 2.5x fewer re-annotations than random on CIFAR-10H and 3x fewer on a chest X-ray benchmark. In our one-look simulation the entropy term cannot help, and Φ\PhiΦ fixes 23.3% after 5,000 reviews, close to the margin order. Their recommendation on where to spend the budget matches chapter 8: "we generally recommend prioritising the cleaning of evaluation data up to an acceptable level before applying any remaining budget to clean the training set".

Spend review time on the evaluation set first. Then order the training-set queue by how wrong the label looks (lowest margin, or highest Phi when ambiguous images need several votes), and stop when the queue's hit rate drops toward the base error rate.

The labeling decision

When you read a new paper in either area, check three things before you adopt it: the strength of its random or plain-training baseline, whether its noise is synthetic or human, and whether it picked its reported epoch on the test set.

Family tree

Choosing what to label

  • 1994, Lewis and Gale: label the examples whose probability is closest to 0.5 (uncertainty sampling).
  • 2009, Settles: the survey that names least confidence, margin and entropy, and warns that actively chosen sets are biased.
  • 2011, Houlsby et al.: BALD, information gain about the model, written in label space.
  • 2015, Gal and Ghahramani: dropout at test time samples from an approximate posterior.
  • 2017, Gal, Islam and Ghahramani: BALD and friends on image CNNs through MC dropout.
  • 2017, Sener and Savarese: core-sets; fill a batch to cover feature space (k-Center).
  • 2019, Kirsch et al. (BatchBALD): score the batch jointly. Ash et al. (BADGE): gradient embeddings plus k-means++ seeding, uncertainty and diversity in one. Sinha et al. (VAAL) and Yoo and Kweon (learning loss): task-agnostic selectors.
  • 2019 to 2023, Mittal et al., Munjal et al., Beck et al., Lüth et al.: fair re-runs; strong baselines shrink the gains.
  • 2021, Kirsch et al.: sample the batch from the scores instead of taking the top-k.
  • 2022, Hacohen, Dekel and Weinshall (TypiClust) and Yehuda et al. (ProbCover): at tiny budgets, label typical images.

Training when labels are wrong

  • 2016, Zhang et al.: networks fit random labels.
  • 2016, Patrini et al.: correct the loss with an estimated noise transition matrix.
  • 2017, Arpit et al.: clean patterns are learned before noise is memorized.
  • 2017, Jiang et al. (MentorNet): a learned curriculum picks the samples to train on. 2018, Han et al. (Co-teaching): two networks pick small-loss samples for each other. 2018, Zhang and Sabuncu: a robust loss (generalized cross-entropy).
  • 2019, Yu et al. (Co-teaching+): keep only the samples where the two networks disagree, then exchange small-loss picks among them. Berthelot et al. (MixMatch): the semi-supervised recipe DivideMix builds on.
  • 2019, Northcutt, Jiang and Chuang (confident learning): count, rank and prune with per-class thresholds.
  • 2020, Li, Socher and Hoi (DivideMix): a two-bump fit on the loss, then semi-supervised learning on the doubted part. Liu et al. (ELR): regularize toward early predictions; report with a validation set.
  • 2020 to 2021, Beyer et al., Yun et al.: ImageNet relabeled with multi-label and localized labels. Northcutt, Athalye and Mueller: label errors in ten test sets. Wei et al. (CIFAR-N): real human noise on CIFAR.
  • 2021, Bernhardt et al.: active label cleaning; order re-annotation by noisiness minus ambiguity. 2022, Goh, Tkachenko and Mueller (CROWDLAB): combine several annotators with a model.

Summary

A label is the scarce input of a vision loop, and two decisions decide what it buys. To choose what to label, ask about the images the model is unsure of, but only after it has seen a few labels per class (before that, label typical images); skip images that are ambiguous to everyone (BALD tells them apart); spread each batch instead of taking near-copies; and keep a random, carefully checked slice to measure accuracy, because random sampling with a well-tuned model is a hard baseline and a selected set is a biased one. To train when labels are wrong, use the fact that networks learn the consistent pattern before the exceptions: out-of-sample probabilities find suspect labels (confident learning), small-loss selection with two networks trains through the noise (Co-teaching), and a two-bump split keeps the doubted images as unlabeled data (DivideMix). Real datasets, test sets included, carry errors, so check the evaluation labels first and order the review queue by how wrong a label looks.
