Confident learning finds the wrong labels

Let a model that never saw an image's label vote on it. Where its confident vote disagrees with the label, the label is a suspect; counting those disagreements also tells you how labels got flipped.

Two ideas make this work. First, the probabilities must be out of sample. A model that trained on an image's label has partly memorized it (chapter 7) and will defend it. So split the data into k = 4 folds, train on three, and predict the fourth, four times over.

Second, each class gets its own threshold, because a model is not equally sure of every class. Ours is confident about ships and frogs and hesitant about cats, so a fixed cut-off would flag every hard class and miss the easy ones:

\begin{aligned} \htmlData{step=1, link=threshold}{t_j} &\htmlData{step=1, link=threshold}{= \frac{1}{|\mathbf{X}_{\tilde y = j}|} \sum_{\mathbf{x} \in \mathbf{X}_{\tilde y = j}} \htmlClass{t-blue}{\hat p(\tilde y = j; \mathbf{x})}} \\ \htmlData{step=2, link=joint}{\mathbf{C}[i][j]} &\htmlData{step=2, link=joint}{= \big|\{\, \mathbf{x} \in \mathbf{X}_{\tilde y = i} : \htmlClass{t-blue}{\hat p(\tilde y = j; \mathbf{x})} \ge t_j \,\}\big|} \\ \htmlData{step=3, link=count}{\#\,\text{errors}} &\htmlData{step=3, link=count}{\approx n \,\big(1 - \operatorname{tr} \hat{\mathbf{Q}}\big)} \end{aligned}

Northcutt, Jiang and Chuang (2021), Eq. 2 (threshold), Eq. 1 (confident joint, simplified: when several classes pass their thresholds, the image counts once, under the most probable one) and Eq. 3, which calibrates the counts into a joint distribution \hat{\mathbf{Q}}; the error count follows from it.

  • t_j: the average probability the model gives class j on images labeled j (the blue bars).
  • \mathbf{C}[i][j]: images labeled i on which the model is at least as confident about class j as it usually is about j. Off-diagonal cells are suspects.
  • \hat{\mathbf{Q}}: \mathbf{C} rescaled so its rows match the observed (given) label counts; the mass off its diagonal estimates how many labels are wrong.

Compare the two matrices. The confident joint puts its biggest off-diagonal counts in exactly the five pairs we flipped. It estimates 12,196 errors against the true 10,065. Now click the cells. In the automobile-to-truck cell, 97.5% of the flagged images really are mislabeled, and in horse-to-deer 98.6%. In the cat-dog cell it is 53.2%: we flipped both directions, so the model learned that cats and dogs are interchangeable. And in the reverse cell, truck-to-automobile, it is 0.0%: those trucks were labeled correctly, but the noisy labels taught the model to call trucks automobiles. Noise in one direction creates false suspicion in the other.

Overall, our flags were 55.7% precise and found 61.0% of the errors; flagging every image where the model's top class differs from the label gave 48.5% precision and 66.4% recall. The paper, with a ResNet-50 at 40% noise, reports 78% precision and 91% recall. Our small CNN is right on only 77.7% of held-out images, and the flags can only be as good as the model that votes. Trained on the cleaned data, the paper's CIFAR-10 models reach 86.7% test accuracy at 40% noise against 60.2% for plain training (asymmetric noise from a random transition matrix, sparsity 0). The method assumes noise depends on the class, not on the individual image; the library that implements it is cleanlab.

The labeling decision

Before each training round, run k-fold predictions over the labeled set and send the off-diagonal images of the confident joint back to review, most suspicious first. Treat them as candidates, not verdicts. Read the largest off-diagonal cells as a report on your labeling guide: they name the class pairs your labelers confuse.
