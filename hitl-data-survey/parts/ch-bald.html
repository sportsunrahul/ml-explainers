Ambiguous or unfamiliar? MC dropout and BALD

"Unsure" comes in two kinds. An ambiguous image (a blurred part, a borderline scratch) stays a coin flip however many labels you buy; an unfamiliar image is one the model has not learned yet, and one label fixes it.

Entropy cannot tell these two apart. Both images get a mean prediction near the center of the triangle, so both sit at the top of the dome. To separate them, the model needs to know how sure it is about itself.

Here is the Bayesian view in one paragraph. Many different weight settings fit the labels you have. If they all predict the same thing for an image, the data has settled the question. If they predict different things, the data has not, and a label for that image would rule some of them out. Gal and Ghahramani (2015 on arXiv, ICML 2016) showed a cheap way to draw such weight settings: keep dropout switched on at test time. Each forward pass drops a different random set of units, so each pass is a slightly different network. Run T passes and you get T predictions for one image: the blue points on the dome.

BALD (Bayesian Active Learning by Disagreement, Houlsby et al. 2011) turns that cloud into one score:

\htmlData{step=1, link=gap}{\mathbb{I}[y, \omega \mid x, \mathcal{D}]} = \htmlData{step=2, link=hmean}{\mathbb{H}[\,y \mid x, \mathcal{D}\,]} \; - \; \htmlData{step=3, link=eh}{\mathbb{E}_{p(\omega \mid \mathcal{D})}\, \mathbb{H}[\,y \mid x, \omega\,]}

Gal, Islam and Ghahramani (2017), Sec. 4; first written for Gaussian processes by Houlsby et al. (2011), Eq. 2. Hover a term to light its part of the dome.

  • \mathbb{H}[y \mid x, \mathcal{D}]: the entropy of the average prediction. In the figure, the dome's height above the mean point.
  • \mathbb{E}\,\mathbb{H}[y \mid x, \omega]: the average entropy of the individual passes. In the figure, the average height of the blue points.
  • The difference is the mutual information between the label y and the weights \omega: how much the label would tell you about which network is right.

The dome is curved downward, so the average of the heights always sits below the height of the average. The gap is large only when the passes are individually confident but point to different corners: the model disagrees with itself. For the ambiguous image every pass says "a third each", every point sits at the top of the dome, and the gap closes. The label would cost the same, and teach nothing.

Gal, Islam and Ghahramani ran this on MNIST with a small dropout CNN: start from 20 labels, add 10 per round, retrain from scratch each time. The number of acquired labels (after the first 20) each score needed to reach a given test error:

BALD's advantage shows where some images are genuinely ambiguous. In the paper's skin-lesion experiment the authors suggest BALD helped because it "avoided selecting noisy points". The paper does not state how many passes T it used for MNIST.

The cost is T forward passes per pool image instead of one, and the model must have dropout layers. An ensemble of a few independently trained networks gives the same kind of disagreement signal at a higher training cost.

The labeling decision

Do not spend labels on images that are ambiguous to everyone: route them to a guideline decision instead ("where exactly is the class boundary?"). Send the images on which the model's own dropout passes, or ensemble members, disagree.
