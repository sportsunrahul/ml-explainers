The loop

Two decisions in every round

A vision model scores a stream of images. A person can label only a few. Each round, the system decides which images go to a person, and then which returned labels to believe. The stage plays one round with real images.

The model draws a blue confidence bar under each image. The three shortest bars get a yellow ring and go to the labeler. One of the three tags is wrong: the image's label was flipped in our noisy copy of CIFAR-10. In the training set its loss stays high, so it gets a red strike and goes back to review.

Main findings of the survey

  • Asking about the images the model is least sure of saves labels in the middle of a campaign, but not at the very start, and not when a batch is full of near-copies.
  • Random sampling is a much stronger baseline than its reputation: with strong augmentation and several seeds, most active-learning gains shrink to the size of the seed-to-seed spread.
  • A network learns the consistent pattern before it memorizes wrong labels. That window is what confident learning, Co-teaching and DivideMix all use.
  • Real datasets carry label errors, test sets included, and a noisy test set can rank the wrong model first. Check the evaluation labels first.

Labels are the scarce input. Spend them where they teach the most, and doubt the ones that disagree with everything else.

Ask about the coin flips

Uncertainty sampling

In you train on a few labels, score every unlabeled image, and send the top few to a person. The score is how unsure the model is. Three standard scores turn the model's into one number: least confidence (one minus the top probability), margin (the gap between the top two) and (how spread the whole vector is).

Ambiguous or unfamiliar?

A batch of near-duplicates

Core-sets

Batches are labeled together, and the ten most uncertain images are often ten frames of one moment. Sener and Savarese pick the batch so that every unlabeled image is close, in the network's feature space, to some labeled one: the problem, solved greedily by always adding the farthest point.

Toy data. Brown points are the pool, with a burst of near-copies on the model's boundary. Yellow discs show how far the worst-covered point is from a label (the covering radius).

Diversity alone is not enough either: over 33 settings in the BADGE study, plain core-set and plain entropy both averaged worse than random. Methods that mix uncertainty and spread (BADGE, BatchBALD, stochastic sampling) held up.

Never send a plain top-k batch from video. Deduplicate, then spread the batch.

Why random is hard to beat

Budgets, baselines and bias

Three reasons. A well-regularized model gains more than clever selection does: in Munjal et al., augmentation plus weight averaging lifted random sampling from 69.16% to 82.16% at 10% of CIFAR-10, and in a transfer test the active-learning gains over random shrank to under two points. At tiny budgets the right pick is the opposite of uncertain: label images (TypiClust). And an actively chosen set is biased, so it cannot be your test set.

Left: TypiClust's two-region model with the paper's toy settings, computed: positive means "label typical images". Right: Munjal et al. Table 4, each method minus random on ResNet-18 (VGG16-selected batches).

Compare against random with strong augmentation, start cold projects with typical images, and keep a random slice for evaluation.

6 of 9

Clean first, wrong later

Memorization of noisy labels

A network can fit any labels, even random ones. But it learns the consistent pattern first. In our CIFAR-10 run with 39.69% of labels flipped, test accuracy peaked at 77.14% at epoch 12. At epoch 10 the network predicted 75.9% of the mislabeled images as their true class; by epoch 30 it had fit 98.2% of them to the wrong label.

Computed from our run: per-image training-loss histograms (log scale) per epoch, green for correct labels and red for flipped ones. Early, the red sits far to the right; later it slides onto the green.

How noisy are real datasets?

Pervasive label errors in test sets

Northcutt, Athalye and Mueller had five crowd workers check the flagged test examples (a random sample for the two largest datasets) in ten benchmarks: at least 3.3% of labels are wrong on average, 2,916 in the ImageNet validation set alone. On the 1,428 ImageNet images that could be corrected, the ranking of 34 models flips: ResNet-18 goes from last to first, NASNet-Large from first to 29th.

Find the wrong labels

Confident learning

Train on three quarters of the data, predict the fourth, four times over: now every image has a vote from a model that never saw its label. Count the images labeled i on which the model is at least as confident about class j as it usually is about j. That count matrix, the , names the confused pairs and estimates how many labels are wrong.

Computed on our run with five flipped class pairs: the matrix finds all five. Click a cell: the automobile-truck and horse-deer cells are 97.5-98.6% real errors, cat-dog 53.2% (both directions were flipped); the reverse truck-to-automobile cell is 0%, because the noise taught the model to doubt correct trucks.

Flags are candidates for review, not verdicts, and they are only as good as the model that votes.

Train through the noise

Co-teaching and DivideMix

Train only on the small-loss part of each batch, and let two networks pick for each other so neither trains on its own mistakes. In our run, the kept set was 92.9% clean at epoch 10 and averaged 78.56% test accuracy over the last ten epochs, against 60.58% for plain training.

Computed: one real batch of 32 images sorted by each network's loss at epoch 10; the kept images are outlined, flipped labels carry a red strike.

Fit two bumps to the loss histogram. Images in the low bump keep their label; images in the high bump lose the label but keep the image, and semi-supervised learning guesses a label for them. DivideMix reached 94.6% on CIFAR-10 with 50% symmetric noise (best epoch).
